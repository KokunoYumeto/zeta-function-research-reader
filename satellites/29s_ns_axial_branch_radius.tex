\section{Four axial branch singularities and their arithmetic transport}
\label{sec:ns-axial-branch-radius}

The preceding exact coefficient formulas determine where the source's
axial analytic germ first fails to extend holomorphically. We compute its
sharp radius, reach four boundary singularities from the original inverse
branch, and prove that the zeroth arithmetic residue carries precisely
the same singularities. The input is the original axial trace from
\cite[Appendix B, pp.~144--147]{openaiNS2026release}; no source amplitude,
physical viscosity, or time exponent is changed.

Retain $0<h<1/100$, $A=1/2+h$, $D=1/2-h$, $0<j_0\leq1/20$,
$\nu>0$, and $\tau=1-t>0$. The source concentration coordinate is still
$q=\tau/(1-\eta^2)$. To avoid confusing it with a new abbreviation, put
\begin{equation}\label{nab:parameters}
 p_*=1-2h=2D,\qquad q_*=2h=1-p_*,\qquad
 B_*=p_*^{p_*}q_*^{q_*},\qquad
 R=B_*^{-1/2}=p_*^{-D}q_*^{-h}.
\end{equation}
The actual germ is
\begin{equation}\label{nab:germ}
 \begin{split}
 x&=\frac{z}{\sqrt\nu\,\tau^D}
       =f(\eta):=\eta(1-\eta^2)^{-D},\\
 \mathcal W(x)&=F(\eta(x)),\qquad
 F(u)=(1-u^2)^A(4u+j_0),\\
 w_0(z,t)&=\sqrt\nu\,\tau^{-A}\mathcal W(x).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
At zero the logarithm of $1-\eta^2$ is zero, $\eta(0)=0$, and
$\eta'(0)=1$. These choices specify the initial inverse branch.
Assertions about the actual cutoff-summed field below use the same
central cutoff-one axis neighborhood as Sections~\ref{sec:ns-actual-second-radial}
and~\ref{sec:ns-all-axis-mellin}: the fixed preterminal time is one for
which that neighborhood contains the origin. The analytic formula
$\mathcal W$ itself is defined independently of that localization and
has the stated radius for every $\tau>0$. No identification with a
cutoff-modified trace outside the original central patch is made.

\begin{theorem}\label{nab:radius-theorem}
For every parameter in the stated source range, the Taylor series of
$\mathcal W$ at zero has exact radius $R$. Four distinct genuine
square-root singularities accessible from this disk occur at
\begin{equation}\label{nab:critical-values}
 x_{\varepsilon,\varsigma}
      =\varepsilon R e^{-i\varsigma\pi D},
       \qquad \varepsilon,\varsigma\in\{-1,1\}.
\end{equation}
Thus the physical axial Taylor radius is exactly
$\sqrt\nu\,\tau^D R$. The zeroth Mellin residue of the actual axial
average, considered as a function of $z$, has that same radius and the
same four singularities, with the explicit forward and inverse maps
given below.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Exact coefficients, including every exceptional zero}

Write $\mathcal W(x)=\sum\mathsf a_nx^n$. In addition to
$\mathsf a_0=j_0$, $\mathsf a_1=4$, equation
\eqref{nax:axial-coefficients} and the gamma recurrence give, for all
$j\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{nab:reciprocal-gamma}
 \begin{split}
 \mathsf a_{2j}
   &=j_0A(-1)^j\frac{\Gamma(p_*j+A)}{\Gamma(j+1)}
                   \operatorname{rgamma}(2-q_*j-D),\\
 \mathsf a_{2j+1}
   &=4(-1)^j\frac{\Gamma(p_*j+1)}{\Gamma(j+1)}
                   \operatorname{rgamma}(2-q_*j).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{rgamma}=1/\Gamma$ is entire, including at the
gamma poles. To verify the identity, write the finite-product binomial
as a gamma quotient away from denominator poles and then continue the
entire reciprocal factor. Both numerator arguments are positive.
Consequently the exact zero conditions, without an asymptotic exception,
are
\begin{equation}\label{nab:exact-zeros}
 \begin{split}
 \mathsf a_{2j}=0&\iff q_*j+D\in\{2,3,4,\ldots\},\\
 \mathsf a_{2j+1}=0&\iff q_*j\in\{2,3,4,\ldots\}.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
The recurrence and reflection identities used here are
\cite[\S5.5, (5.5.1), (5.5.3)]{askeyRoyDLMF5}.

For $j>A/q_*$ in the even case and $j>1/q_*$ in the odd case,
reflection gives positive finite envelopes:
\begin{equation}\label{nab:envelopes}
 \begin{split}
 \mathsf a_{2j}&=(-1)^{j+1}\mathcal E_j
                          \cos((2j-1)\pi h),\\
 \mathcal E_j&=\frac{j_0A}{\pi}
       \frac{\Gamma(p_*j+A)\Gamma(q_*j-A)}{\Gamma(j+1)}>0,\\
 \mathsf a_{2j+1}&=(-1)^{j+1}\mathcal O_j\sin(2\pi hj),\\
 \mathcal O_j&=\frac4\pi
       \frac{\Gamma(p_*j+1)\Gamma(q_*j-1)}{\Gamma(j+1)}>0.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Indeed $\operatorname{rgamma}(2-u)
=-\Gamma(u-1)\sin(\pi u)/\pi$ for $u>1$, including integer
$u\geq2$ by continuity. For the even term use
$\sin(\pi(q_*j+D))=\cos((2j-1)\pi h)$. At $u=1$ the formal
zero times a pole is not a zero; \eqref{nab:reciprocal-gamma}, rather
than that undefined product, covers the excluded threshold and every
smaller index. For example, $h=1/200$, $j=100$ gives the nonzero odd
coefficient $1/25$, whereas $j=200$ gives a genuine zero. No division
by a trigonometric factor is used.

\subsection{Sharp large-order envelopes and the exact Taylor radius}

For fixed $h$, positive-real Stirling expansion
\cite[\S5.11(i), (5.11.1), (5.11.3)]{askeyRoyDLMF5} gives
\[
 \Gamma(cj+b)=\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-cj}(cj)^{cj+b-1/2}
       \left(1+\frac{B_2(b)}{2cj}+O_{b,c}(j^{-2})\right),
 \quad c>0,\quad B_2(b)=b^2-b+\tfrac16.
\]
This follows by expanding $\log(1+b/(cj))$ in the logarithmic formula;
the constant exponential shift cancels. Therefore the product with
shifts $(b,c)$ and slopes $(p_*,q_*)$ is
\begin{equation}\label{nab:gamma-product}
 \begin{split}
 \frac{\Gamma(p_*j+b)\Gamma(q_*j+c)}{\Gamma(j+1)}
 ={}&\sqrt{2\pi}\,p_*^{b-1/2}q_*^{c-1/2}
             B_*^j j^{b+c-3/2}\\
 &\cdot\left(1+\frac1j\left[
       \frac{B_2(b)}{2p_*}+\frac{B_2(c)}{2q_*}-\frac1{12}
                  \right]+O_h(j^{-2})\right),
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
where the shifts below are fixed functions of $h$. The pairs
$(A,-A)$ and $(1,-1)$ yield
\begin{equation}\label{nab:sharp-envelopes}
 \begin{split}
 \mathcal E_j&=C_e B_*^j j^{-3/2}
                 (1+c_e/j+O_h(j^{-2})),\\
 C_e&=j_0A\sqrt{2/\pi}\,p_*^h q_*^{-1-h},\qquad
 c_e=\frac{11-2q_*-7q_*^2}{24p_*q_*},\\
 \mathcal O_j&=C_o B_*^j j^{-3/2}
                 (1+c_o/j+O_h(j^{-2})),\\
 C_o&=4\sqrt{2/\pi}\,p_*^{1/2}q_*^{-3/2},\qquad
 c_o=\frac{13-13q_*+q_*^2}{12p_*q_*}.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Every constant follows from \eqref{nab:gamma-product}; none is fitted
to numerical data. These are envelope asymptotics, not ratios divided
by the possibly zero sine or cosine in \eqref{nab:envelopes}. They
assert no uniform estimate as $h\downarrow0$.

The upper bounds give a limsup at most $\sqrt{B_*}$ for each parity.
For the reverse bound, the subtraction formula for sine gives
\[
 |\sin\delta|\leq|\sin\theta|+|\sin(\theta+\delta)|.
\]
Set $\delta=\pi q_*$. It lies strictly between zero and $\pi/50$.
Each phase sequence in \eqref{nab:envelopes} is a shifted sine with
increment $\delta$. Each pair of successive indices therefore has a
term of magnitude at least $\sin\delta/2>0$. The positive envelopes
give infinitely many matching lower bounds for each parity, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{nab:limsups}
 \limsup_j|\mathsf a_{2j}|^{1/(2j)}
 =\limsup_j|\mathsf a_{2j+1}|^{1/(2j+1)}=\sqrt{B_*}.
\end{equation}
Cauchy--Hadamard proves the exact radius $R$. This proof covers every
allowed real $h$, including rational parameters with infinitely many
zero coefficients and irrational parameters with very small nonzero
subsequences. Since $0<p_*,q_*<1$, one has $R>1$.

\subsection{The four singularities belong to the original inverse branch}

We first prove that the actual inverse $\eta$ is holomorphic throughout
$|x|<R$, not just on a smaller unquantified neighborhood. The Lagrange
formula \eqref{nax:lagrange}, applied to $F(u)=u$, gives
\[
 [x^{2j+1}]\eta(x)=\frac{(-1)^j}{2j+1}
                      {p_*j+D\choose j},\qquad
 [x^{2j}]\eta(x)=0.
\]
For $j>D/q_*$ reflection rewrites the first expression as
\[
 \frac{(-1)^j\sin(\pi(q_*j-D))}{\pi(2j+1)}
 \frac{\Gamma(p_*j+D+1)\Gamma(q_*j-D)}{\Gamma(j+1)}.
\]
The positive gamma-product estimate, with shifts $(D+1,-D)$ and
the extra factor $(2j+1)^{-1}$, bounds this by
$O_h(B_*^j j^{-3/2})$. The inverse power series thus converges on
$|x|<R$. Its initial inverse identity continues along paths by local
uniqueness wherever $f'$ is nonzero.

Put $r=q_*^{-1/2}>1$. In the $\eta$ plane travel from zero to $ir$
on the imaginary axis, then along the upper circle $|\eta|=r$ to
either $r$ or $-r$. This path avoids $\eta=\pm1$. On its first segment,
\[
 |f(iv)|=v(1+v^2)^{-D},\qquad
 \frac{d}{dv}\bigl(v(1+v^2)^{-D}\bigr)
       =(1+v^2)^{-D-1}(1+q_*v^2)>0.
\]
Even the endpoint modulus is less than $r(r^2-1)^{-D}=R$.
On each open circular arc,
\[
 |1-r^2e^{2i\theta}|^2=(r^2-1)^2+4r^2\sin^2\theta
       >(r^2-1)^2,
\]
so $|f(\eta)|<R$ until its endpoint. The lower-half-plane conjugate
paths have the same property. Direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{nab:fprime}
 f'(\eta)=(1-\eta^2)^{-D-1}(1-q_*\eta^2).
\end{equation}
It is nonzero before the endpoints. On every compact initial part
of an image path, successive local inverses agree with the disk's
$\eta(x)$, starting from their common germ at zero. This proves
that the prescribed paths are continuations of that original inverse;
it does not presume global injectivity on another logarithm sheet.

At $\eta_c=\varepsilon/\sqrt{q_*}$ let $\varsigma$ record the
endpoint logarithm
\[
 \log(1-\eta^2)\longrightarrow
                   \log(p_*/q_*)+i\varsigma\pi.
\]
The four resulting image endpoints are exactly
\eqref{nab:critical-values}, distinct because $0<D<1/2$.
For instance the upper approach to $+r$ has $\varsigma=-1$,
and the upper approach to $-r$ has $\varsigma=1$.

\subsection{Convergent square-root expansions with nonzero amplitudes}

Let $d_c=1-\eta_c^2=-p_*/q_*$, evaluated using the just specified
local logarithm. Differentiating \eqref{nab:fprime} gives
\[
 f''(\eta_c)=-2q_*\eta_c d_c^{-D-1},\qquad
 \frac{f''(\eta_c)}{x_c}=\frac{2q_*^2}{p_*}\ne0.
\]
It follows that
\[
 1-x/x_c=-\frac{q_*^2}{p_*}(\eta-\eta_c)^2
                   +O((\eta-\eta_c)^3).
\]
For an analytic inversion proof, factor the right side as
$(\eta-\eta_c)^2H_c(\eta)$, where
$H_c(\eta_c)=-q_*^2/p_*\ne0$. A local holomorphic square root of
$H_c$ exists, and $(\eta-\eta_c)\sqrt{H_c(\eta)}$ has nonzero
derivative at $\eta_c$. Its holomorphic inverse, composed with
$\sqrt{1-x/x_c}$, gives a convergent Puiseux expansion. With that
square root asymptotic to the positive one on the circle approach,
\begin{equation}\label{nab:puiseux-inverse}
 \eta(x)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{q_*}}
   +\kappa_{\varepsilon,\varsigma}\sqrt{1-x/x_c}
   +O(1-x/x_c),\qquad
 \kappa_{\varepsilon,\varsigma}
       =-i\varepsilon\varsigma\frac{\sqrt{p_*}}{q_*}.
\end{equation}
The approach has imaginary part of sign $-\varepsilon\varsigma$;
this fixes the displayed sign, while $\kappa^2=-p_*/q_*^2$ checks
its square. Finally
\begin{equation}\label{nab:no-cancellation}
 F'(\eta_c)=-\frac2{q_*}d_c^{-D}
                   (4+\varepsilon A j_0\sqrt{q_*})\ne0.
\end{equation}
To verify it, differentiate $d^A(4\eta+j_0)$, use $A+D=1$ and
$\eta_c^2=1/q_*$. The possible correction to $4$ has magnitude
less than $51/2000$, since $A<51/100$, $j_0\leq1/20$,
$\sqrt{q_*}<1$. Substitution proves
\begin{equation}\label{nab:puiseux-W}
 \mathcal W(x)=F(\eta_c)
       +F'(\eta_c)\kappa_{\varepsilon,\varsigma}
                           \sqrt{1-x/x_c}+O(1-x/x_c).
\end{equation}
The leading fractional-power coefficient is nonzero at all four
points. In particular a holomorphic extension there is impossible.
This finishes the analytic part of Theorem~\ref{nab:radius-theorem}.

\subsection{The exact same branch geometry in the arithmetic residue}

Define a function of the physical axial coordinate and time by
\[
 J_0(z,t)=\operatorname{Res}_{s=0}
                \mathcal M\mathcal A_h Z_{\rm act}(s,z,t),
 \qquad c_{h,0}=\frac{2^h-1}{4}>0.
\]
The variables $s$ and $z$ have different explicit roles: $s$ is the
Mellin variable, whereas $z$ is the original physical axis coordinate.
Theorem~\ref{nax:extension} gives, on the source's central real-axis
patch, the exact forward and inverse identities
\begin{equation}\label{nab:arithmetic-inverse}
 J_0(z,t)=c_{h,0}w_0(z,t),\qquad
 w_0(z,t)=c_{h,0}^{-1}J_0(z,t).
\end{equation}
Indeed evaluation at radial $\sigma=0$ of the actual axial angular
average is $w_0$, and the zeroth residue multiplies that value by
$c_{h,0}$. The full radial remainder has zero zeroth coefficient;
it is not discarded from the whole Mellin transform. Therefore
\eqref{nab:arithmetic-inverse} is an equality of the corresponding
holomorphic $z$ germs. It is not merely an equality of a finite set
of numerical derivatives.

Set $z_c=\sqrt\nu\,\tau^D x_c$. Equations
\eqref{nab:germ}, \eqref{nab:puiseux-W}, and
\eqref{nab:arithmetic-inverse} prove the explicit expansion
\begin{equation}\label{nab:arithmetic-puiseux}
 \begin{split}
 J_0(z,1-\tau)=c_{h,0}\sqrt\nu\,\tau^{-A}
 \bigl(&F(\eta_c)+F'(\eta_c)\kappa_{\varepsilon,\varsigma}
                       \sqrt{1-z/z_c}\\
       &+O(1-z/z_c)\bigr).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Every leading coefficient and the inverse scalar in this transport
are nonzero. Both physical germs consequently have exact radius
$\sqrt\nu\,\tau^D R$. Their complete derivative formulas include
\begin{equation}\label{nab:physical-limsup}
 \begin{split}
 \partial_z^nw_0(0,1-\tau)
     &=n!\nu^{(1-n)/2}\tau^{-A-nD}\mathsf a_n,\\
 \partial_z^nJ_0(0,1-\tau)
     &=c_{h,0}n!\nu^{(1-n)/2}\tau^{-A-nD}\mathsf a_n,\\
 \limsup_n\left|\frac{\partial_z^nJ_0(0,1-\tau)}{n!}\right|^{1/n}
     &=\frac1{\sqrt\nu\,\tau^D R}.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
The same limsup holds for $w_0$. The physical singularity locations
shrink at the original power $\tau^D$; the original amplitude
$\sqrt\nu\,\tau^{-A}$ remains visible.

The transferred singularities are in the continued physical variable
$z$ of a fixed endpoint residue at $s=0$. Equations
\eqref{nab:arithmetic-inverse}--\eqref{nab:arithmetic-puiseux} are the
precise relation; they do not locate zeros of $\zeta(s)$ or determine
the sign of the complete Weil pairing. The full smooth cutoff field
has other variables and remainders and has not been asserted globally
holomorphic. The original existence theorem remains imported. The
standard proofs above cover the whole stated parameter range; the
accompanying exact finite and high-precision checks are regressions,
not a formal Lean certificate or a numerical substitute for those proofs.
