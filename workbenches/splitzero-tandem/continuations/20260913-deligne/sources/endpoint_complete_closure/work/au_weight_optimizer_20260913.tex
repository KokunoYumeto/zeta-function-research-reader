This is a separate continuation of the sealed arithmetic volume upper
estimate AU.1--33 and AU.40--49. It solves the weight optimization in
AU.19 completely, including boundary optima, zero polynomial coefficients,
the sole nonunique case, and an evaluable one-dimensional characterization.
The signed raw-jet minors, source coordinate phases, factorials and
arithmetic masses remain explicit. The improvement over equal weights is
proved to be less than one natural-logarithm unit for every degree within
that specified family. The continuation (OW.30)--(OW.40) proves a stronger
source estimate by keeping the even moments together. It then solves the
entire resulting diagonal family using the actual moments through degree
$2q$. Its exact comparison with AU.19 removes a term of order $q\log q$;
the earlier less-than-one theorem retains its precise, narrower domain.

\section{The original source and the signed full-jet minors}

Keep $g=2\xi$, the original complete monic zero packet $h$, and
\[
 w_h(t)=|(g/h)(1/2+it)|^2/(2\pi),\quad
 m_k=w_h^{*k},\quad \mu_h=\int_\R w_h(t)dt,\quad
 S=c+iu,\quad c=k/2.                                      \tag{OW.1}
\]
The source polynomial norm is
$\int_\R|P(c+iu)|^2m_k(u)du$ and its mass is $\mu_h^k$.
For the original degree-$q$ monic relation $\chi$ put
\[
 \psi(u)=i^{-q}\chi(c+iu)=\sum_{j=0}^q\psi_ju^j,
 \quad\psi_q=1,\qquad
 \psi_j=\frac{i^{j-q}}{j!}\chi^{(j)}(c).                  \tag{OW.2}
\]
The last identity is the polynomial Taylor expansion at the original
centre $c$. The exact ring map $P(S)\mapsto P(c+iu)$ has inverse
$f(u)\mapsto f((S-c)/i)$ and intertwines multiplication by $S$ with
$c+iM_u$. Its raw derivative of order $d$ at
$\zeta=(\lambda-c)/i$ is $i^dP^{(d)}(\lambda)$.
No coefficient phase or derivative factorial has been reassigned.

List the distinct roots $\zeta_a$ of $\psi$, in the original order,
with their complete lengths $\ell_a$, so $\sum_a\ell_a=q\ge1$.
For $N\ge q-1$ the full raw-jet matrix is
\[
 (E_N)_{(a,d),j}=
 \begin{cases}j!\zeta_a^{j-d}/(j-d)!,&j\ge d,\\0,&j<d,
 \end{cases}
 \quad 0\le d<\ell_a,\quad0\le j\le N.                  \tag{OW.3}
\]
Taylor expansion proves that its kernel consists of polynomials
divisible by $\psi$. Thus $Z:=E_{q-1}$ is invertible by degree,
and $E_q(\psi_0,\ldots,\psi_q)^T=0$. Write
$\Delta_j=\det E_q[:,\{0,\ldots,q\}\setminus\{j\}]$, with
the retained increasing column order. The precise identity is
\[
 \boxed{\Delta_j=(-1)^{q-j}\psi_j\det Z\quad(0\le j\le q).}
 \tag{OW.4}
\]
To prove the sign, append any row of $E_q$ as a final row and expand
the resulting zero determinant along that row. It shows that
$((-1)^j\Delta_j)_{j=0}^q$ lies in the kernel of $E_q$.
That kernel has dimension one because its first $q$ columns form $Z$.
It is therefore a scalar multiple of the displayed monic coefficient
vector. Its last coordinate is $(-1)^q\Delta_q=(-1)^q\det Z$,
which proves (OW.4). In particular a zero coefficient gives exactly
a zero minor, rather than a deleted column or shortened jet space.

The raw confluent determinant, in the same row order, is
\[
 \det Z=\left(\prod_a\prod_{d=0}^{\ell_a-1}d!\right)
          \prod_{a<a'}(\zeta_{a'}-\zeta_a)^{\ell_a\ell_{a'}}.
 \tag{OW.5}
\]
For completeness take the ordinary Vandermonde determinant with
temporarily distinct nodes grouped into the prescribed clusters.
Successive divided-difference row operations remove its within-cluster
Vandermonde factors. The finite Taylor expansion of each monomial
shows that its order-$d$ divided-difference row tends to the raw
derivative divided by $d!$. Multiplication of these rows by $d!$
gives (OW.5), with the cross-cluster factors still in their original
order. Equivalently this proves the identity after cancellation of
polynomial factors, so no analytic source approximation is involved.

Fix $0<b<\pi/2$ and retain the two literal Laplace values
\[
 M_k=M_h(b)^k+M_h(-b)^k,\quad M_h(b)=\int_\R e^{bt}w_h(t)dt,
 \qquad \beta_j=\frac{b^{2j}}{(2j)!}>0.                   \tag{OW.6}
\]
For positive auxiliary weights $\tau_j$ the matrix used in AU.19 is
$A_q(\tau)=E_q\diag(\tau_j\beta_j)E_q^*$.
Cauchy--Binet and (OW.4) prove
\[
 \begin{split}
 \det A_q(\tau)
 &=|\det Z|^2\sum_{j=0}^q a_j\prod_{l\ne j}\tau_l,\\
 a_j&=|\psi_j|^2\prod_{l\ne j}\beta_l
       =\Bigl(\prod_{l=0}^q\beta_l\Bigr)\eta_j,\qquad
 \eta_j=\frac{|\psi_j|^2}{\beta_j}
       =\frac{(2j)!}{(j!)^2b^{2j}}|\chi^{(j)}(c)|^2.
 \end{split}                                               \tag{OW.7}
\]
In particular $a_q>0$ and $\eta_q=(2q)!b^{-2q}>0$.
To verify Cauchy--Binet directly, expand the determinant multilinearly
in the columns of $E_q$ and then its adjoint. Repeated column choices
vanish by alternation; collecting each $q$-element set gives its
minor times its conjugate and the product of its diagonal weights.
This yields exactly the first line of (OW.7). The signed identity is
proved before taking the squared modulus.

For arbitrary positive $\tau$, let $s=\sum_j\tau_j$ and
$t_j=\tau_j/s$. The map $(\tau_j)\mapsto(s,(t_j))$ is a bijection
from the positive cone to $(0,\infty)$ times the open simplex, with
inverse $\tau_j=st_j$. The determinant in (OW.7) acquires exactly
the factor $s^q$. Thus the $q\log s$ in AU.19 cancels exactly.
This changes auxiliary optimization coordinates and does not change
$m_k$, its mass, or either source or quotient norm. Multiplication
of all $a_j$ by the common positive product in (OW.7) multiplies the
objective by that product, with the maximizing weights unchanged.
It suffices to solve the following finite optimization with all its
original coefficients retained.

\section{Global maximizers, including every zero coefficient}

For $a_0,\ldots,a_q\ge0$ with $a_q>0$, define
\[
 \mathcal S_q=\{\tau\in\R^{q+1}:\tau_j\ge0,\ \sum_j\tau_j=1\},
 \quad D_a(\tau)=\sum_{j=0}^qa_j\prod_{l\ne j}\tau_l,
 \quad d(a)=\max_{\tau\in\mathcal S_q}D_a(\tau).          \tag{OW.8}
\]
The objective is a polynomial and the simplex is compact. Its value
at equal weights is positive, so a positive maximum exists. Put
$I=\{j:a_j>0\}$, $p=|I|$, $A=\max_j a_j$, and $S_a=\sum_j a_j$.

\begin{theorem}[Complete boundary and uniqueness classification]
For $q\ge2$ the maximizing weights are precisely the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $A\ge S_a-A$, choose its index $j$. Unless exactly two
coefficients are positive and equal, the maximizer is unique:
$\tau_j=0$, $\tau_i=1/q$ for every $i\ne j$, and
$d(a)=A/q^q$.
\item If exactly two coefficients $a_j=a_l=A$ are positive, every
maximizer, and only a maximizer, satisfies
$\tau_j+\tau_l=1/q$ and $\tau_i=1/q$ at the other $q-1$ indices.
Its value is again $A/q^q$.
\item If $A<S_a-A$, then $p\ge3$ and the maximizer is unique and
has every coordinate strictly positive. At a zero coefficient it
satisfies $\tau_j=1/q$.
\end{enumerate}
For $q=1$, $D_a=a_0\tau_1+a_1\tau_0$ is linear: it is maximized
at the endpoint selecting its larger coefficient, or everywhere if
the two coefficients are equal. Its value is $\max(a_0,a_1)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
An elementary determinant representation proves the needed concavity.
Set $v_i=\sqrt{a_i/a_q}$ for $0\le i<q$ and
\[
 M(\tau)=\diag(\tau_0,\ldots,\tau_{q-1})+\tau_q vv^*.
 \qquad D_a(\tau)=a_q\det M(\tau).                       \tag{OW.9}
\]
For positive first $q$ weights, the rank-one determinant expansion
gives $\det M=\prod_{i<q}\tau_i(1+\tau_q\sum_{i<q}|v_i|^2/\tau_i)$.
This is (OW.9); since both sides are polynomials it holds at boundary
weights too. Every $M(\tau)$ is positive semidefinite on the simplex,
and $D_a>0$ precisely when it is positive definite.
For a real affine line $M(t)=M_0+tE$ through positive definite
matrices, differentiating the inverse identity gives
$(M^{-1})'=-M^{-1}EM^{-1}$. The determinant's multilinear derivative
therefore proves
\[
 (\log\det M)'=\Tr(M^{-1}E),\qquad
 (\log\det M)''=-\Tr((M^{-1/2}EM^{-1/2})^2)\le0.
 \tag{OW.10}
\]
The final matrix is Hermitian, so its squared eigenvalues are
nonnegative, and equality holds exactly when $E=0$.

If $p\ge3$, at least two coordinates of $v$ are nonzero. The
linear map from $\tau$ to $M(\tau)$ is injective: an off-diagonal
entry between two such coordinates forces a zero increment of
$\tau_q$ whenever the matrix increment is zero; then its diagonal
forces every other increment to be zero. Thus $\log D_a$ is
strictly concave on its positive domain, which is convex as the
inverse image of the positive definite cone. There can be at most
one maximizer with positive objective.

If a simplex point has at least two zero coordinates, every monomial
in $D_a$ contains a zero, so its objective is zero. A positive
boundary maximum must therefore have exactly one zero coordinate,
say $\tau_j=0$, and then
$D_a=a_j\prod_{i\ne j}\tau_i$. It requires $a_j>0$.
For $q$ nonnegative numbers of sum one, concavity of $\log x$
(whose second derivative is $-x^{-2}$) gives product at most $q^{-q}$,
with equality exactly when they all equal $1/q$; a zero factor
has product zero. Hence every positive boundary maximum is the
candidate just described. At this point
\[
 \partial_iD_a=a_j/q^{q-1}\ (i\ne j),\qquad
 \partial_jD_a=(S_a-a_j)/q^{q-1}.                          \tag{OW.11}
\]
If $S_a-a_j>a_j$, the feasible path
$\tau_j=\varepsilon$, $\tau_i=(1-\varepsilon)/q$ for $i\ne j$
has positive derivative $(S_a-2a_j)/q^{q-1}$ at zero. That
boundary point cannot maximize. Conversely, if $S_a-a_j\le a_j$,
the gradient of $\log D_a$ at the candidate has all active entries
equal and the inactive entry no larger. Its scalar product with
any feasible displacement is nonpositive. Concavity along the
segment to any point with positive objective gives
$\log D_a(\tau)\le\log D_a(\tau^*)+
\nabla\log D_a(\tau^*)\cdot(\tau-\tau^*)\le\log D_a(\tau^*)$.
This derivative is valid at the boundary because $M(\tau^*)>0$
and remains positive in a neighborhood. Points of zero objective
cannot exceed it. This proves the boundary criterion and, for
$p\ge3$, uniqueness from strict concavity.

If $p=1$, $D_a=A\prod_{i\ne j}\tau_i$ and the same product
inequality gives the stated unique maximizer. If $p=2$, with
positive coefficients at $j,l$, factor the polynomial exactly as
\[
 D_a(\tau)=\left(\prod_{i\notin\{j,l\}}\tau_i\right)
                (a_j\tau_l+a_l\tau_j).                  \tag{OW.12}
\]
At fixed $\tau_j+\tau_l$, unequal coefficients allocate that
sum entirely to the larger coefficient's complementary variable.
The remaining $q$ factors of total sum one have the unique equal
product maximum. If the two coefficients coincide, their sum is
one factor instead, and equality in that same product inequality
gives exactly the listed segment of maximizers.

When $A<S_a-A$, no boundary candidate can maximize, so compactness
gives an interior maximum and strict concavity makes it unique.
It remains to verify the stated zero coordinate value; that follows
from the complete interior stationarity calculation below. The
case $q=1$ is immediate from its displayed linear polynomial.
\end{proof}

No artificial coefficient phase is required to apply (OW.9) to the
original raw-jet matrix. In fact $E_q=Z[I_q,-\psi_{<q}]$ by monic
remainder, so
\[
 A_q(\tau)=ZB_0^{1/2}
  \bigl(\diag(\tau_0,\ldots,\tau_{q-1})+\tau_q zz^*\bigr)
  B_0^{1/2}Z^*,\quad
 B_0=\diag(\beta_0,\ldots,\beta_{q-1}),\quad
 z_i=-\psi_i\sqrt{\beta_q/\beta_i}.                       \tag{OW.13}
\]
Its $|z_i|^2$ equals $\eta_i/\eta_q$. Define the diagonal unitary
$U_{ii}=-\psi_i/|\psi_i|$ when $\psi_i\ne0$ and $U_{ii}=1$
otherwise. Then $z=Uv$, with $v_i=\sqrt{\eta_i/\eta_q}$,
and its central matrix is $UM(\tau)U^*$. This is the exact
phase-preserving congruence connecting the auxiliary determinant
proof to the original columns; all the phase entries remain in $U$.

\section{A complete monotone scalar description of the interior optimum}

Assume $q\ge2$ and $A<S_a-A$, so the preceding theorem gives its
unique interior maximizer. For positive weights put
$P=\prod_j\tau_j$ and $S=\sum_j a_j/\tau_j$; then $D_a=PS$.
Differentiating every factor gives
$\partial_iD_a=P(S/\tau_i-a_i/\tau_i^2)$.
The Lagrange multiplier for $\sum\tau_i=1$ is $qD_a$ because
$\sum_i\tau_i\partial_iD_a=qD_a$, each monomial having degree $q$.
Consequently its complete equations are
\[
 a_i=S\tau_i(1-q\tau_i).                                \tag{OW.14}
\]
If $a_i=0$, positivity gives $\tau_i=1/q$. At a positive
coefficient let $y_i=1-q\tau_i$. The equation shows
$0<y_i<1$, and counting the zero coordinates gives
\[
 \sum_{i\in I}y_i=1,\qquad
 a_i=c_*y_i(1-y_i),\quad c_*=S/q>0,\qquad
 \tau_i=(1-y_i)/q.                                       \tag{OW.15}
\]
Here $c_*$ is the optimizer's scalar, distinct from the source centre
$c=k/2$ in (OW.1). Conversely any positive solution of (OW.15),
together with $\tau_i=1/q$ at zero coefficients, has sum one and
satisfies (OW.14): indeed
$\sum_i a_i/\tau_i=q c_*\sum_{i\in I}y_i=q c_*$.
Concavity then proves that this stationary point is the global
maximizer, and uniqueness has already been established.

For $0\le t\le1/A$ and $i\in I$ define the continuous functions
\[
 f_i(t)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-a_it}}2
       =\frac{a_it}{2(1+\sqrt{1-a_it})},\quad
 F(t)=\sum_{i\in I}f_i(t),\qquad t=4/c_*.
 \tag{OW.16}
\]
All square roots are the nonnegative real roots. The second formula
is its exact rationalization, valid also at $t=0$.
Each $f_i$ is strictly increasing on this interval, has value zero
at zero, and takes values at most $1/2$.

\begin{theorem}[The unique scalar root and the complete maximizing value]
Under $A<S_a-A$ exactly one of the following descriptions applies.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $F(1/A)\ge1$, there is a unique $t_*>0$ in $(0,1/A]$
with $F(t_*)=1$. Set $y_i=f_i(t_*)$ at every positive coefficient.
\item If $F(1/A)<1$, the largest coefficient has a unique index $j$.
There is a unique $t_*\in(0,1/A)$ solving
\[
 H(t):=\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{j\}}\frac{a_i}{A}
   \frac{1+\sqrt{1-At}}{1+\sqrt{1-a_it}}=1.               \tag{OW.17}
\]
The function $H$ is strictly decreasing, with
$H(0)=(S_a-A)/A>1$ and $H(1/A)=2F(1/A)-1<1$.
Set $y_j=1-f_j(t_*)$ and $y_i=f_i(t_*)$ for $i\ne j$.
\end{enumerate}
In either case the weights are those in (OW.15), with zeros handled
as stated, and the exact value is
\[
 \boxed{d(a)=\frac{4}{q^qt_*}\prod_{i\in I}(1-y_i).}     \tag{OW.18}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In (OW.15) at most one $y_i$ can exceed $1/2$ because their sum
is one. If none does, its quadratic equations have precisely the
smaller roots $f_i(t)$, and their sum is $F(t)=1$.
Continuity, strict increase and $F(0)=0$ prove existence and
uniqueness exactly in the first listed case.

If $y_j>1/2$, each other $y_i$ is strictly smaller than
$1-y_j<1/2$, because $p\ge3$ gives at least two other positive
terms. The function $x(1-x)$ is strictly increasing on $(0,1/2)$.
It follows that $a_i<c_*y_j(1-y_j)=a_j$, so the large root must
occur at the unique largest coefficient. Its sum equation is
$\sum_{i\ne j}f_i(t)=f_j(t)$. At $t>0$ dividing by the positive
$f_j$ and using the second formula in (OW.16) gives exactly (OW.17).

If $F(1/A)<1$, the maximum is unique already from the endpoint
values: two largest coefficients would contribute $1/2+1/2$ and
the other positive terms would make $F(1/A)>1$.
For $a_i<A$ and $0<t<1/A$, direct logarithmic differentiation gives
\[
 \frac d{dt}\log\frac{1+\sqrt{1-At}}{1+\sqrt{1-a_it}}
  =\frac{(1-a_it)^{-1/2}-(1-At)^{-1/2}}{2t}<0.
 \tag{OW.19}
\]
Thus every term of $H$ decreases strictly. Its value at zero is
given by continuity, and $f_j(1/A)=1/2$ gives the second endpoint
value. The intermediate value theorem proves its unique root.
The constructed $y$ has positive entries summing to one and solves
the quadratic equations, so (OW.15) proves it is the optimum.
Conversely if $F(1/A)\ge1$, an index with a large root would
have to be the unique largest one; its decreasing $H$ would have
$H(1/A)=2F(1/A)-1\ge1$ and hence no root inside $(0,1/A)$.
At equality, the largest root is exactly $1/2$ and coincides with
the first construction. This proves completeness of the cases.

Finally $\prod_j\tau_j=q^{-(q+1)}\prod_{i\in I}(1-y_i)$
and $S=q c_*=4q/t_*$. Multiplication gives (OW.18), with the
full original dimension exponent $q$ retained.
\end{proof}

The zero-coefficient assertion can also be proved globally without
interior differentiation. If $p\ge2$ and $z=q+1-p>0$, write
$s=\sum_{i\in I}\tau_i$ and $\tau_i=s\sigma_i$ on $I$.
The polynomial factors as
$(\prod_{i\notin I}\tau_i)s^{p-1}D_{a|I}(\sigma)$.
For fixed $s$, the first product is at most $((1-s)/z)^z$.
The derivative of $\log((1-s)^zs^{p-1})$ vanishes only at
$s=(p-1)/q$, and its second derivative is strictly negative.
The endpoints have zero product. Hence every maximum has
$\tau_i=1/q$ at zero coefficients, and
\[
 d(a)=\frac{(p-1)^{p-1}}{q^q}\,d_{p-1}(a|I),           \tag{OW.20}
\]
where the last maximum uses the explicitly displayed positive-index
simplex of dimension $p-1$. If $z=0$, the same formula has factor
one. For $p=1$, the first theorem gives the value directly, with
the positive coordinate zero and the other $q$ weights $1/q$.
Thus the reduction has an explicit map and determinant power,
without changing the dimension of the original arithmetic quotient.

For three positive coefficients $a,b,d$ satisfying the strict
triangle inequalities, the solution even has a rational expression.
Put $\Delta=2ab+2ad+2bd-a^2-b^2-d^2>0$. Then
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 \tau_a=\frac{2a(b+d-a)}{q\Delta},\quad
 \tau_b=\frac{2b(a+d-b)}{q\Delta},\quad
 \tau_d=\frac{2d(a+b-d)}{q\Delta},\\
 \tau_i=1/q\quad(a_i=0),\qquad
 \boxed{d(a)=\frac{4abd}{q^q\Delta}.}
 \end{gathered}                                           \tag{OW.21}
\]
Indeed $\Delta=4ab-(a+b-d)^2>0$ because
$0<a+b-d<2\min(a,b)\le2\sqrt{ab}$.
On the positive-index simplex the proposed coordinates
$\sigma_a=a(b+d-a)/\Delta$ and its cyclic counterparts are
positive and sum to one. For its quadratic polynomial
$a\sigma_b\sigma_d+b\sigma_a\sigma_d+d\sigma_a\sigma_b$,
direct substitution makes every gradient coordinate $2abd/\Delta$.
Euler's degree-two identity then gives its value $abd/\Delta$;
concavity proves maximality. Equation (OW.20) supplies exactly
the powers and the weights in (OW.21).

\section{Evaluable enclosures and the exact maximum gain}

For exact rational nonnegative input coefficients, the branch
comparison at $1/A$ uses only finitely many nonnegative square roots.
Equality is permitted as the first branch's endpoint. In the strict
cases, (OW.16) or (OW.17) has a continuous strictly monotone scalar
function on a known compact interval with opposite endpoint signs.
Rational bisection therefore gives its unique root. Square roots
themselves admit rational brackets by squaring positive rationals
and bisecting. If an exact trial point is a root, it already solves
the problem. Otherwise convergent radical brackets eventually
resolve the nonzero sign at that trial point. A positive lower
bracket for $t_*$ is obtained after finitely many bisections because
$t_*>0$. Substitution in (OW.18) then yields convergent value
brackets; every denominator is bounded away from zero on those
root brackets. Boundary maximizers require only the exact coefficient
comparison $A\ge S_a-A$ and their explicit product formula.

There is also a uniform certificate that avoids deciding a branch
from uncertain coefficients. For two nonnegative coefficient lists,
the product inequality used in (OW.11) gives, at every simplex point,
\[
 |D_a(\tau)-D_{\widehat a}(\tau)|
       \le q^{-q}\sum_j|a_j-\widehat a_j|.
\]
Taking a maximizing point for each list in turn proves
\[
 \boxed{|d(a)-d(\widehat a)|
       \le q^{-q}\sum_j|a_j-\widehat a_j|.}              \tag{OW.22}
\]
The maximum is also increasing in each coefficient because each
monomial is nonnegative. Thus nonnegative rational boxes
$a^-\le a\le a^+$ give $d(a^-)\le d(a)\le d(a^+)$, and their
value width is at most $q^{-q}\sum(a_j^+-a_j^-)$.
These statements remain valid if a corner list is all zero, where
its maximum is exactly zero, or its reference last coefficient
vanishes: a positive reference coefficient can be selected by the
explicit simultaneous permutation of coefficient and simplex indices,
whose inverse permutation restores every original index. No
unresolved arithmetic equality must be assumed to construct the
enclosure. For the original list $a_q>0$ its lower value eventually
stays positive, so logarithms can also be enclosed by their ordinary
monotone integral $\log x=\int_1^xdt/t$.

A rational candidate has a direct exact value certificate, so no radical
equality needs to be decided merely to enclose the answer. At any simplex
point $\tau$ with $D:=D_a(\tau)>0$, calculate the full polynomial
gradient $g_i=\partial_iD_a(\tau)$ and set
\[
 h=\max_i g_i-qD\ge0.
\]
The sign follows from $\sum_i\tau_i g_i=qD$. Concavity and the
simplex constraint give, for every point of positive objective,
\[
 \log D_a(\sigma)\le\log D+
       \frac{\sum_i g_i(\sigma_i-\tau_i)}D
       \le\log D+h/D.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \boxed{D\le d(a)\le D e^{h/D},\qquad
 h<D\ \Longrightarrow\ d(a)\le\frac{D^2}{D-h}.}         \tag{OW.22a}
\]
The last inequality follows from
$e^x=\sum_{n\ge0}x^n/n!\le\sum_{n\ge0}x^n=(1-x)^{-1}$
for $0\le x<1$. The polynomial gradient is defined also at a
boundary point; the argument is valid there because its matrix
(OW.9) is positive definite. Thus an exactly stationary rational
candidate, including a boundary maximum with the correct inactive
gradient, has $h=0$ and certifies its exact value.

Here is a terminating way to combine this certificate with scalar
evaluation, including an unresolved equality $F(1/A)=1$.
Use rational brackets for every square root. If the endpoint interval
separates from one, select the proved branch and bisect its scalar
root. If an endpoint interval still contains one, also form the
candidate from its smaller-root values at $1/A$. At any scalar
trial whose comparison interval contains zero, form its candidate
as well, instead of making an unsupported sign decision. Rational
midpoints of the root brackets give nonnegative $y$ approximations;
replace the corresponding nonnegative auxiliary weights by their
ratios to their positive sum, making an exact rational simplex
point. One may keep zero-coefficient weights exactly $1/q$ and
apply the positive-index rescaling (OW.20) only to the remaining
coordinates. These are the explicit positive-cone coordinate maps
already proved, and no source measure changes. Test (OW.22a) at
each such candidate.

As square-root intervals narrow, a strictly nonzero branch or
bisection sign is eventually determined. If a tested equality is
exact, its candidate tends instead to the already proved optimum.
In a fixed strict branch, successive root brackets tend to its
unique root. In all cases the resulting candidates tend to the
maximizer or to a member of the stated maximizing segment.
There $D>0$ and $h=0$ by (OW.11) or (OW.14), and $D,h$ are
continuous functions of the candidate. Thus eventually $h<D$ and
the rational certificate width $D^2/(D-h)-D$ is smaller than any
prescribed positive rational tolerance. Every returned interval is
certified solely by the rational polynomial gradient and (OW.22a),
independently of the numerical branch-selection history.

\begin{theorem}[The finite gain is uniformly less than one]
Let $\tau^{\rm eq}_j=1/(q+1)$ and $S_a=\sum a_j>0$.
For every $q\ge1$,
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 D_a(\tau^{\rm eq})=\frac{S_a}{(q+1)^q},\qquad
 \frac{S_a}{(q+1)^q}\le d(a)\le\frac{S_a}{q^q},\\
 \boxed{0\le\mathfrak g(a):=
 \log\frac{d(a)}{D_a(\tau^{\rm eq})}
 \le q\log(1+1/q)<1.}
 \end{gathered}                                           \tag{OW.23}
\]
The lower equality holds precisely when all coefficients are equal.
The upper non-strict equality holds precisely when exactly one
coefficient is positive.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The equal-weight value is obtained by substituting each weight.
Every product of $q$ selected nonnegative weights has sum at most
one and is therefore at most $q^{-q}$. Summing with coefficients
$a_j$ gives the upper bound. If just one coefficient is positive,
its boundary maximizer attains that bound. If two or more are
positive, equality would require every corresponding product to
attain $q^{-q}$. For the term omitting $j$, that requires
$\tau_j=0$ and $\tau_i=1/q$ at every other index. Two distinct
positive terms cannot meet those two incompatible conditions.
Thus the upper equality has exactly the stated case.

If equal weights maximize, they are an interior point. Differentiating
the polynomial there gives
$\partial_iD_a=(q+1)^{-(q-1)}(S_a-a_i)$, which must be constant
in $i$ under the sum constraint. Thus every $a_i$ is equal.
Conversely equal coefficients give equal gradient coordinates at
equal weights, and the determinant concavity proves maximality
for $q\ge2$; for $q=1$ it is the constant linear polynomial.
Finally $\log(1+x)<x$ for $x>0$, since
$\log(1+x)=\int_0^x(1+t)^{-1}dt<\int_0^xdt=x$.
Apply this with $x=1/q$ to prove the strict upper bound one.
\end{proof}

One additional fully explicit lower bracket for the gain is
\[
 \mathfrak g(a)\ge
 \max\{0,q\log(1+1/q)+\log(A/S_a)\}.                    \tag{OW.24}
\]
It follows by testing the best boundary point. In the boundary
regime its second expression is the exact gain. When $p\ge2$,
testing equal weights on the positive-index simplex in (OW.20)
also gives
$\mathfrak g(a)\ge q\log(1+1/q)
+(p-1)\log((p-1)/p)$, with every zero retained through the
factor in (OW.20).

\section{Joint even moments and exact optimization of their diagonal family}

Keep the source, chart, polynomial and every raw-jet row from (OW.1)--(OW.7).
For $0\le j\le q$ write
\[
 \mu_{2j}:=\int_\R u^{2j}m_k(u)du>0,\qquad
 \mu_0=\mu_h^k,\qquad
 S_q(b):=\sum_{j=0}^q\beta_j\mu_{2j}.
 \tag{OW.30}
\]
The inequalities are strict because the source density is positive almost
everywhere and $u^{2j}>0$ off zero. Finiteness follows from (OW.6) and its
exponential-series majorant. These are moments in the original $u$ chart;
the zeroth moment remains the full $k$-fold source mass.

For a coefficient vector $p=(p_0,\ldots,p_q)^T$ the ordinary complex
Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives the pointwise statement
\[
 \left|\sum_{j=0}^qp_ju^j\right|^2
 \le\left(\sum_{j=0}^q\frac{|p_j|^2}{\beta_j}\right)
      \left(\sum_{j=0}^q\beta_j u^{2j}\right)
 \le\left(\sum_{j=0}^q\frac{|p_j|^2}{\beta_j}\right)\cosh(bu).
\]
The first inequality uses the two vectors $p_j/\sqrt{\beta_j}$ and
$\sqrt{\beta_j}u^j$, with complex conjugation in the inner product;
the second uses the full nonnegative even exponential series.
Its finite tail is strictly positive for every $u\ne0$. Integration,
including both original Laplace values, therefore proves
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 H_q\preceq S_q(b)\diag(\beta_j^{-1})
       \prec\frac{M_k}{2}\diag(\beta_j^{-1}),\\
 0<S_q(b)<\int_\R\cosh(bu)m_k(u)du
   =\frac{M_h(b)^k+M_h(-b)^k}{2}=\frac{M_k}{2}.
 \end{gathered}                                                \tag{OW.31}
\]
The second matrix inequality is strict positive definiteness because it
is a strictly positive scalar difference times a positive diagonal matrix.
The convolution identity here follows by integrating $e^{b(t_1+\cdots+t_k)}$
against $\prod_iw_h(t_i)dt_i$; its nonnegative integrand permits iterated
integration. The identical calculation applies at $-b$.

Inverting (OW.31) and applying the unchanged full raw-jet matrix gives
\[
 K_q^{\rm raw}\succeq S_q(b)^{-1}E_q\diag(\beta_j)E_q^*.
\]
The determinant identity (OW.7) at the literal weights all equal to one
is $|\det Z|^2B_\beta\sum_j\eta_j$. The original numerator comparison
$K_{2q-1}^{\rm raw}\preceq K_{2q-1}^{\nu,\rm raw}$ is unchanged. Hence
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 C_k\le\mathcal U_{\rm fin}:=
 q\log S_q(b)-\log B_\beta-\log\Bigl(\sum_j\eta_j\Bigr)
                       -\log V_{2q-1}^\nu,\\
 \mathcal U_{\rm fin}<\mathcal U_{\rm cosh}:=
 q\log(M_k/2)-\log B_\beta-\log\Bigl(\sum_j\eta_j\Bigr)
                       -\log V_{2q-1}^\nu.
 \end{gathered}                                                \tag{OW.32}
\]
Thus the pointwise finite sum supplies its own proved source bound; it
does not use the older scalar estimate $M_k\sum\tau_j$ from AU.19.
Direct subtraction, with no moment or factorial discarded, gives
\[
 \begin{split}
 \mathcal U_{\rm eq}-\mathcal U_{\rm cosh}
      &=q\log(2(q+1)),\\
 \mathcal U_{\rm opt}-\mathcal U_{\rm fin}
      &=q\log(2(q+1))-\mathfrak g(\eta)
                +q\log\frac{M_k}{2S_q(b)}>0.
 \end{split}                                                  \tag{OW.33}
\]
The positivity follows from $\mathfrak g(\eta)<1$ and
$q\log(2(q+1))\ge\log4>1$ for $q\ge1$.
For the last elementary inequality,
$\log4=2\int_1^2dt/t>2(2-1)/2=1$.

The same argument determines a larger family completely. To avoid
identifying these new diagonal weights with the cofactor coefficients
$a_j$ in (OW.7), denote them by $r_j>0$ and put
\[
 s(r):=\sum_{j=0}^qr_j\mu_{2j},\qquad
 H_q\preceq s(r)\diag(r_j^{-1}),\qquad
 K_q^{\rm raw}\succeq s(r)^{-1}E_q\diag(r_j)E_q^*.
 \tag{OW.34}
\]
Indeed pointwise Cauchy--Schwarz with $p_j/\sqrt{r_j}$ and
$\sqrt{r_j}u^j$, followed by integration, gives the middle inequality
for every coefficient vector $p$. Inversion reverses the positive

$Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}\preceq I$ and its positive eigenvalues are at most
one, so $X^{-1}\succeq Y^{-1}$. Congruence by $E_q$ proves the final

The positive-cone map and its inverse are
\[
 (r_j)\longmapsto\left(s(r),\ t_j=\frac{r_j\mu_{2j}}{s(r)}\right),
 \qquad (s,t)\longmapsto r_j=\frac{s\,t_j}{\mu_{2j}}.
 \tag{OW.35}

They are mutually inverse bijections between $\R_{>0}^{q+1}$ and
