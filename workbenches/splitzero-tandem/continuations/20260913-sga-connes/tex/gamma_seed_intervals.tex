\section{Certified theta moments and the original gamma arithmetic coefficients}



This continuation proves and evaluates the fourth and sixth moments of the
original theta seed, then transports the enclosures through the exact
coefficient map for the reference parameter $\lambda=13/4$ in
\emph{An exact gamma pushforward for the arithmetic Toda control}.
The seed retains $g=2\xi$, the density $|g(1/2+\ii t)|^2/(2\pi)$,
the Fourier coordinate $t$, and its actual total mass. The reference
retains the argument $\lambda+\ii t/2$ and its different actual mass.
We give the full analytic formulas, an explicit infinite-square tail for
every finite derivative order, and the exact rational enclosures used
by the executable certificate.

\subsection{The theta input and its transform, with the original factors}

On $\mathcal S=\{z\ne0:|\arg z|<\pi/4\}$ use the principal
logarithm, and put
\begin{align}
 D&=-z\partial_z,\qquad L=D-\tfrac12,\qquad
 \vartheta(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{-\pi n^2z^2},\tag{GS.1}\\
 f_0(z)&=D(D-1)\vartheta(z)
 =2\sum_{n\ge1}(4\pi^2n^4z^4-6\pi n^2z^2)e^{-\pi n^2z^2}.
 \tag{GS.2}
\end{align}
The series and each differentiated series converge locally uniformly:
on a compact subset of $\mathcal S$, every summand is bounded by
a polynomial in $n$ times $e^{-a n^2}$ for some $a>0$.

\begin{lemma}[Inversion and both endpoints]
On the specified sector,
\begin{equation}
 \vartheta(z)=z^{-1}\vartheta(1/z),\qquad
 f_0(z)=z^{-1}f_0(1/z),\qquad
 f_0(\bar z)=\overline{f_0(z)}.\tag{GS.3}
\end{equation}
Every $D$-derivative of $f_0$ decays faster than every power both at
zero and at infinity on a closed proper subsector.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $x>0$, Fourier transformation with kernel $e^{-2\pi\ii \eta u}$
maps $e^{-\pi x^2u^2}$ to $x^{-1}e^{-\pi\eta^2/x^2}$.
The value at $\eta=0$ follows from the Gaussian integral, whose
square in polar coordinates is one. Differentiation in $\eta$ and
integration by parts give $I'(\eta)=-2\pi\eta I(\eta)/x^2$,
which proves the stated formula with that initial value.
Periodize this Gaussian over integer translates. Its Fourier
coefficients are the displayed rapidly decreasing transform values;
its absolutely convergent Fourier series evaluated at zero proves
$\vartheta(x)=x^{-1}\vartheta(1/x)$. Holomorphic uniqueness extends
the identity to $\mathcal S$.

For $(RF)(z)=z^{-1}F(1/z)$, direct differentiation gives
$DR=R(1-D)$ and hence $D(D-1)R=RD(D-1)$. Applying this to
$R\vartheta=\vartheta$ proves the second equality in (GS.3).
The real series coefficients prove the third. On
$|\arg z|\le\beta<\pi/4$ one has
$\Re z^2\ge |z|^2\cos(2\beta)>0$; the series and each
derivative therefore have Gaussian decay at infinity with a fixed
polynomial factor. Inversion and $DR=R(1-D)$ transport all these
bounds to zero, with every derivative retained.
\end{proof}

Define, on these rapid sector functions,
\begin{equation}
 (\M F)(s)=\int_0^\infty F(x)x^{s-1}\dd x,
 \qquad (UF)(y)=e^{y/2}F(e^y),
 \qquad (\F_+u)(t)=\int_{\mathbb R}u(y)e^{\ii ty}\dd y.
 \tag{GS.4}
\end{equation}
Here $U:L^2(\mathbb R_{>0},\dd x)\to L^2(\mathbb R,\dd y)$
is a surjective linear isometry: $x=e^y$ proves the norm identity,
and its inverse is $u\mapsto[x\mapsto x^{-1/2}u(\log x)]$.
The endpoint bounds prove that the Mellin integral is entire.
For $\Re s>1$, absolute integration of (GS.2) is justified by
$\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-\Re s}<\infty$. Substitution $v=\pi n^2x^2$
then gives
\begin{align}
 (\M f_0)(s)
 &=\pi^{-s/2}\zeta(s)
       \{4\Gamma(s/2+2)-6\Gamma(s/2+1)\}\notag\\
 &=s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)=:g(s)=2\xi(s).
 \tag{GS.5}
\end{align}
The factor $2$ in (GS.2) and the substitution factor $1/2$
both occur before the first equality. Integration by parts proves
the gamma recurrence used in the second equality. The entire Mellin
integral continues the right side. Substitution $x\mapsto1/x$
and real conjugation in (GS.4) prove
\begin{equation}
 g(1-s)=g(s),\qquad g(\bar s)=\overline{g(s)}.
 \tag{GS.6}
\end{equation}
In particular the following original density is even:
\begin{equation}
 w(t)=\frac{|g(1/2+\ii t)|^2}{2\pi},\qquad
 \mu_j=\int_{\mathbb R}t^j w(t)\dd t.\tag{GS.7}
\end{equation}
All moments exist. To see the exponential bound directly, rotate
the Mellin contour to the ray $e^{\ii\beta}\mathbb R_{>0}$ with
$|\beta|<\pi/4$. The endpoint bounds make the closing arcs vanish,
and the rotated formula has the factor $e^{\ii\beta s}$.
On the line $\Re s=1/2$, choosing the sign of $\beta$ to match
$\Im s$ yields $|g(1/2+\ii t)|\le C_\beta e^{-|\beta||t|}$.
Thus $\int e^{a|t|}w(t)\dd t<\infty$ for every $0<a<\pi/2$.
Evenness gives $\mu_{2r+1}=0$.

\subsection{Every derivative polynomial and the exact higher-moment map}

Let $H_r=L^rf_0$ for integers $r\ge0$. Direct differentiation of
(GS.4) gives $ULF=-(UF)'$ on the rapid sector functions. Integration
by parts in $\F_+$, with the endpoint bounds already proved, therefore
gives the commuting transform identity
\begin{equation}
 \F_+(UH_r)(t)=(\ii t)^r g(1/2+\ii t).\tag{GS.8}
\end{equation}
Every sign and power $\ii^r$ belongs to this map.

\begin{theorem}[Exact higher-moment formula]
Define polynomials $P_r\in\mathbb Q[y]$ by
\begin{align}
 P_0(y)&=8y^2-12y,\qquad
 P_{r+1}(y)=-2yP_r'(y)+(2y-\tfrac12)P_r(y),\tag{GS.9}\\
 P_r(y)&=\sum_{a=1}^{r+2}p_{r,a}y^a,\qquad
 p_{r+1,a}=2p_{r,a-1}-(2a+\tfrac12)p_{r,a},\tag{GS.10}
\end{align}
where coefficients outside their indicated range are zero. Then
\begin{equation}
 H_r(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}P_r(\pi n^2x^2)e^{-\pi n^2x^2},\qquad
 \mu_{2r}=\int_0^\infty |H_r(x)|^2\dd x
          =2\int_1^\infty H_r(x)^2\dd x.\tag{GS.11}
\end{equation}
The first four complete polynomials are
\begin{align}
 P_0&=8y^2-12y,\notag\\
 P_1&=16y^3-60y^2+30y,\notag\\
 P_2&=32y^4-224y^3+330y^2-75y,\notag\\
 P_3&=64y^5-720y^4+2116y^3-1635y^2+\tfrac{375}{2}y.
 \tag{GS.12}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
With $y=\pi n^2x^2$, the operator $D$ is $-2y\partial_y$.
Consequently
$L(P(y)e^{-y})=\{-2yP'(y)+(2y-1/2)P(y)\}e^{-y}$.
This proves (GS.9), the series identity in (GS.11), and (GS.10)
by collecting each specified coefficient. It also proves inductively
that the degree is $r+2$, the leading coefficient is $2^{r+3}$,
and the constant coefficient is zero. Applying the recurrence three
times gives the complete rational lists (GS.12).

Fourier Plancherel in the convention (GS.4) has factor $1/(2\pi)$:
$\int|u|^2\dd y=(2\pi)^{-1}\int|\F_+u|^2\dd t$.
For the Schwartz functions here this follows by inserting
$e^{-\varepsilon t^2}$ into Fourier inversion, applying Fubini,
and evaluating the Gaussian kernel of integral one; letting
$\varepsilon\downarrow0$ gives inversion and the inner-product
identity. Applying it to (GS.8) and the unitary $U$ proves the
first integral equality in (GS.11).

The relation $DR=R(1-D)$ gives $RL=-LR$, while $Rf_0=f_0$.
Thus $RH_r=(-1)^rH_r$, or
$H_r(1/x)=(-1)^r xH_r(x)$ for $x>0$. In the integral over
$(0,1)$ substitute $x=1/v$. The factor $v^2$ from the squared
identity cancels the Jacobian $v^{-2}$ and yields exactly the
integral over $(1,\infty)$. The sign $(-1)^r$ is retained before
squaring. All values $H_r(x)$ are real because their series has
real coefficients, which finishes (GS.11).
\end{proof}

The numerical driver in the delivered Gamma note writes $q_0=4y^2-6y$
and uses the same linear recurrence for $q_r$. Its exact coefficient
map to this presentation is $q_r\mapsto P_r=2q_r$, with inverse
$P_r\mapsto P_r/2$. Thus $H_r=2\sum_nq_r(\pi n^2x^2)e^{-\pi n^2x^2}$.
At the integral level (GS.11) consequently gives a factor $8$
in front of the sum using $q_rq_r I_{2a+2b}$. At the gamma-function
level below it gives a factor $4$ in front of $q_rq_r\Gamma/C^\alpha$.
The coefficient map accounts for both presentations without changing
the theta input or its mass.

\subsection{The incomplete-gamma square and a proved omitted tail}

For real $C>0$ and integers $p\ge0$ put
\begin{equation}
 I_p(C)=\int_1^\infty x^p e^{-Cx^2}\dd x
       =\frac{\Gamma((p+1)/2,C)}{2C^{(p+1)/2}},\qquad
 \Gamma(a,C)=\int_C^\infty v^{a-1}e^{-v}\dd v.\tag{GS.13}
\end{equation}
Substitution $v=Cx^2$ proves the equality. Only positive real arguments
and their positive real powers occur here. The companion recurrence,
including its endpoint term, is
\begin{equation}
 I_{p+2}(C)=\frac{p+1}{2C}I_p(C)+\frac{e^{-C}}{2C}.
 \tag{GS.14}
\end{equation}
It follows by integration by parts in (GS.13), whose upper endpoint
vanishes exponentially.

Let $H_{r,J}$ restrict the original series (GS.11) to $1\le n\le J$,
where $J\ge0$ is an integer. Put $T_{r,J}=2\int_1^\infty H_{r,J}^2\dd x$.
The entire finite expression is
\begin{equation}
 T_{r,J}=\sum_{m,n=1}^J\sum_{a,b=1}^{r+2}
 p_{r,a}p_{r,b}(\pi m^2)^a(\pi n^2)^b
 \frac{\Gamma(a+b+1/2,\pi(m^2+n^2))}
 {[\pi(m^2+n^2)]^{a+b+1/2}}.\tag{GS.15}
\end{equation}
Here the endpoint factor $2$ in (GS.11) cancels the factor $1/2$
in (GS.13); the coefficients $p_{r,a}$ already contain the complete
original theta factor. Empty sums at $J=0$ have value zero.

To specify the tail by finite positive expressions, define Stirling
numbers of the second kind by
$\left\{\begin{smallmatrix}0\\0\end{smallmatrix}\right\}=1$,
zero when one index is zero and the other positive, zero outside
$0\le b\le j$, and
\[
 \left\{\begin{matrix}j+1\\b\end{matrix}\right\}
 =b\left\{\begin{matrix}j\\b\end{matrix}\right\}
  +\left\{\begin{matrix}j\\b-1\end{matrix}\right\}.
\]
For $a\ge1$, $d>0$, integers $p\ge0$, set
\begin{align}
 \rho_a&=e^{-d(2a+1)},\notag\\
 \mathsf H_p(a,d)&=\sum_{j=0}^p\binom pj a^{p-j}
 \sum_{b=0}^j\left\{\begin{matrix}j\\b\end{matrix}\right\}
       \frac{b!\rho_a^b}{(1-\rho_a)^{b+1}},\tag{GS.16}\\
 \mathsf A_r(a)&=\sum_{b=1}^{r+2}|p_{r,b}|\pi^b
                         \mathsf H_{2b}(a,\pi).\tag{GS.17}
\end{align}

\begin{theorem}[Every finite derivative order has an explicit square tail]
For every integer $r\ge0$ and $J\ge0$, with $K=J+1$,
\begin{equation}
 |\mu_{2r}-T_{r,J}|\le E_{r,J}:=
 4\mathsf A_r(1)\mathsf A_r(K)
 I_{4r+8}(\pi(1+K^2)).\tag{GS.18}
\end{equation}
The right side is a finite positive expression and tends to zero as
$J\to\infty$. In particular the infinite version of (GS.15) is
absolutely convergent and equals $\mu_{2r}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $x\ge1$, integers $a\ge1$ and $p\ge0$, write $n=a+j$.
The inequality $(a+j)^2-a^2=2aj+j^2\ge(2a+1)j$ proves
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\ge a}n^p e^{-\pi n^2x^2}
 \le e^{-\pi a^2x^2}\sum_{j\ge0}(a+j)^p\rho_a^j
 =e^{-\pi a^2x^2}\mathsf H_p(a,\pi).\tag{GS.19}
\end{equation}
For the equality, expand $(a+j)^p$ and then use
$j^v=\sum_b\left\{\begin{smallmatrix}v\\b\end{smallmatrix}\right\}(j)_b$,
where $(j)_b=j(j-1)\cdots(j-b+1)$. This identity follows
inductively from $j(j)_b=(j)_{b+1}+b(j)_b$ and the displayed
Stirling recurrence. Differentiating the geometric series $b$
times gives
$\sum_{j\ge0}(j)_b\rho^j=b!\rho^b/(1-\rho)^{b+1}$,
which proves the last equality in (GS.19).

Since $x^{2b}\le x^{2r+4}$ for every original exponent $b\le r+2$,
(GS.19) gives the three explicit inequalities
\begin{align*}
 |H_r(x)|,\ |H_{r,J}(x)|
 &\le\mathsf A_r(1)x^{2r+4}e^{-\pi x^2},\\
 |H_r(x)-H_{r,J}(x)|
 &\le\mathsf A_r(K)x^{2r+4}e^{-\pi K^2x^2}.
\end{align*}
The same estimate for the sum of absolute values of all summands
proves absolute integrability of the expanded double square. In
particular termwise integration in the infinite formula is legitimate.
Now use $|H_r^2-H_{r,J}^2|\le|H_r-H_{r,J}|(|H_r|+|H_{r,J}|)$
and the endpoint factor $2$ in (GS.11). The resulting constant is
exactly $4$, the power is $4r+8$, and integration gives (GS.18).

For fixed $r$, the denominators in (GS.16) are bounded away from
zero uniformly for integers $a\ge1$; thus $\mathsf H_p(a,\pi)$
is at most a constant times $(1+a)^p$, and $\mathsf A_r(a)$
has at most polynomial growth. For $C\ge\pi$,
\[
 I_p(C)\le e^{-C}\int_1^\infty x^p e^{-\pi(x^2-1)}\dd x.
\]
The last integral is finite. Hence the factor with
$C=\pi(1+K^2)$ dominates that polynomial growth and forces
$E_{r,J}\to0$, proving the remaining assertions.
\end{proof}

In the two new cases the coefficients in (GS.17) are completely
specified by
\begin{align}

 &=75\pi\mathsf H_2+330\pi^2\mathsf H_4
   +224\pi^3\mathsf H_6+32\pi^4\mathsf H_8,\notag\\
 \mathsf A_3(a)
 &=\tfrac{375}{2}\pi\mathsf H_2+1635\pi^2\mathsf H_4
   +2116\pi^3\mathsf H_6+720\pi^4\mathsf H_8
   +64\pi^5\mathsf H_{10},\tag{GS.20}
\end{align}
where each $\mathsf H_p$ on the right means $\mathsf H_p(a,\pi)$.
These absolute coefficients enter the error estimate only. Every
original negative coefficient stays in the exact square (GS.15).

\subsection{The coefficient morphism at the actual gamma reference}

For $\lambda>0$ put
\begin{equation}
 r_\lambda(t)=\frac{|\Gamma(\lambda+\ii t/2)|^2}{2\pi},\qquad
 c_\lambda=2^{1-2\lambda}\Gamma(2\lambda),\qquad
 \alpha=2\lambda.\tag{GS.21}
\end{equation}
We prove the constants needed for the coefficient map. The beta
integral follows from the product of two gamma integrals by the
substitution $v=x_1+x_2$, $u=x_1/(x_1+x_2)$ with Jacobian $v$.
After $u/(1-u)=e^x$ it reads
\begin{equation}
 \int_{\mathbb R}(2\cosh(x/2))^{-2\lambda}e^{\ii vx}\dd x
 =\frac{|\Gamma(\lambda+\ii v)|^2}{\Gamma(2\lambda)}.
 \tag{GS.22}
\end{equation}
The integrand before transformation and its first two derivatives
decay exponentially at both ends. Two integrations by parts therefore
give an integrable Fourier transform, so Fourier inversion applies
with the same Gaussian justification as above. Using its evenness
and substituting $t=2v$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_{\mathbb R}e^{\ii yt}r_\lambda(t)\dd t
 =c_\lambda(\cosh y)^{-2\lambda}.\tag{GS.23}
\end{equation}
The factor $2$ from $t=2v$ is present in $c_\lambda$.
The function $(2\cosh(x/2))^{-2\lambda}$ is holomorphic on
$|\Im x|<\pi$ with the branch positive on the real line and
decays exponentially on each closed narrower strip. Shifting the
contour in (GS.22) up for $v>0$ and down for $v<0$ proves
$|\Gamma(\lambda+\ii v)|^2\le C_c e^{-c|v|}$ for each
$0<c<\pi$. Thus the density in (GS.21) has exponential moments
for every exponent of absolute value less than $\pi/2$.
Analytic continuation of (GS.23) yields
\begin{equation}
 \int e^{\theta t}r_\lambda(t)\dd t
 =c_\lambda(\cos\theta)^{-\alpha}\quad(|\theta|<\pi/2).
 \tag{GS.24}
\end{equation}

Define the monic polynomials by the local generating function
\begin{align}
 F_\lambda(z,t)&=(1-\ii z)^{-\lambda+\ii t/2}
                 (1+\ii z)^{-\lambda-\ii t/2}
 =\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{b_n^{(\lambda)}(t)}{n!}z^n,\tag{GS.25}\\
 b_0&=1,\quad b_1=t,\quad
 b_{n+1}=t b_n-n(n+\alpha-1)b_{n-1}.\tag{GS.26}
\end{align}
The logarithms in (GS.25) vanish at zero. For real $z$ close to
zero, $F_\lambda(z,t)=(1+z^2)^{-\lambda}e^{t\arctan z}$.
Equation (GS.24) and
$\cos(\arctan z+\arctan v)=(1-zv)/\sqrt{(1+z^2)(1+v^2)}$
give
\begin{equation}
 \int F_\lambda(z,t)F_\lambda(v,t)r_\lambda(t)\dd t
 =c_\lambda(1-zv)^{-\alpha}.\tag{GS.27}
\end{equation}
Exponential integrability permits every derivative at $(0,0)$
under the integral. Coefficient comparison proves
\begin{equation}
 \int b_n^{(\lambda)}(t)b_j^{(\lambda)}(t)r_\lambda(t)\dd t
 =c_\lambda n!(\alpha)_n\delta_{nj}.\tag{GS.28}
\end{equation}
The highest coefficient of (GS.25) is $t^n/n!$, so these
polynomials are monic. Finally differentiating (GS.25) gives
$(1+z^2)\partial_zF_\lambda=(t-\alpha z)F_\lambda$;
coefficient comparison proves (GS.26), including all its constants.

The actual amplitude linking the two measures is
\begin{equation}
 A_{1,\lambda}(t)=\frac{g(1/2+\ii t)}{\Gamma(\lambda+\ii t/2)},
 \qquad B_{1,\lambda}(t)=|A_{1,\lambda}(t)|^2,
 \qquad w(t)=B_{1,\lambda}(t)r_\lambda(t).\tag{GS.29}
\end{equation}
Gamma has no zeros and no poles at the displayed arguments. Here is
a direct proof on their half-plane. For $\Re z>0$, repeated integration
by parts gives $B(z,n+1)=n!/[z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)]$.
Substitution $u=v/n$ in the beta integral and
$(1-v/n)^n\le e^{-v}$ prove $n^z B(z,n+1)\to\Gamma(z)$ by
dominated convergence. The reciprocal finite products converge to
\[
 z e^{\gamma z}\prod_{j\ge1}(1+z/j)e^{-z/j},\qquad
 \gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{j=1}^n j^{-1}-\log n\right).
\]
Integral comparison proves existence of the real limit. The logarithm
of the tail product converges absolutely because
$\log(1+z/j)-z/j=O(j^{-2})$, and every factor is nonzero on
$\Re z>0$. The product is therefore finite and nonzero; by the
previous limit it is $1/\Gamma(z)$. The original gamma integral is
holomorphic on this half-plane, which also excludes poles there.
The phase-preserving source map is multiplication by $A_{1,\lambda}$
from $L^2(w\dd t)$ to $L^2(r_\lambda\dd t)$; the exact norm
identity follows by (GS.29). It is onto: the zero set of $g$ on
the line is discrete because $g$ is entire and $g(2)>0$ by (GS.5).
Division by the amplitude off that null set gives the inverse and
the same norm equality.

For completeness the full source phase at the chosen parameter is
\begin{equation}

It follows from (GS.5), $s(s-1)=-(t^2+1/4)$, and three gamma

The squared modulus of the denominator in (GS.30) is
$4^{-3}(t^2+1/4)(t^2+25/4)(t^2+81/4)$, so

The last denominator exceeds the numerator by

\subsection{Certified infinite quantities and exact rational error transport}

The companion checker evaluates (GS.15) for $r=0,1,2,3$ and
$J=8$ in Arb real balls at 256-bit precision, with one arithmetic
thread and python-flint 0.9.0. All polynomial input is exact
rational arithmetic; $\pi$, exponentials, powers and gamma functions
are enclosed by Arb. The upper-incomplete-gamma call is
\texttt{C.gamma\_upper(alpha)}, with argument $C$ and order
$\alpha$, precisely as in (GS.13). Each finite ball is enlarged
by a symmetric ball with radius the upward-rounded upper endpoint
of (GS.18). It therefore encloses the infinite quantity.
The computed tails satisfy the rational inequalities
\begin{equation}
 E_{0,8}<7\cdot10^{-107},\quad E_{1,8}<4\cdot10^{-103},\quad
 E_{2,8}<2\cdot10^{-99},\quad E_{3,8}<14\cdot10^{-96}.

\subsection{Exact tensor coefficient interface through degree six}

The map from the certified one-factor data to every finite tensor
degree can also be stated without changing the mass or sum variable.
Let $k\ge1$, $u=t_1+\cdots+t_k$, and $m_k=w^{*k}$. Fubini
gives $\int m_k=\mu_0^k$, and exponential integrability proves
all the moments below are finite. Multiplying the generating
functions (GS.25) gives the finite polynomial identity
\begin{equation}
 b_n^{(k\lambda)}(u)=
 \sum_{n_1+\cdots+n_k=n}\frac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_k!}
                     \prod_{j=1}^k b_{n_j}^{(\lambda)}(t_j).
 \tag{GS.38}
\end{equation}
Its proof is literal multiplication: the exponents of $1\mp\ii z$
add to $-k\lambda\pm\ii u/2$, and the coefficient of degree
$n$ has only finitely many terms.

Use $\lambda=13/4$, $\alpha=13/2$, $a_j=(\alpha)_j c_j$, and
the formal coefficient series $\mathcal A(z)=\sum_{j\ge0}a_jz^j$.
For any coefficient appearing below only finitely many $c_j$ are
needed. Integration of (GS.38), using (GS.32) separately in each
factor, proves
\begin{equation}
 \int b_n^{(k\lambda)}(u)m_k(u)\dd u
 =c^k n!d_{k,n},\qquad d_{k,n}=[z^n]\mathcal A(z)^k.
 \tag{GS.39}
\end{equation}
No interchange of an infinite formal series with an integral is
required. Evenness gives the complete first four nonzero expressions
\begin{align}
 d_{k,0}&=c_0^k,\notag\\
 d_{k,2}&=k c_0^{k-1}a_2,\notag\\
 d_{k,4}&=k c_0^{k-1}a_4+\binom{k}{2}c_0^{k-2}a_2^2,\notag\\

                  +\binom{k}{3}c_0^{k-3}a_2^3.
 \tag{GS.40}
\end{align}
For degree six the partitions into even positive degrees are
$6$, $4+2$ and $2+2+2$; their distinct-factor choices have respectively
$k$, $k(k-1)$ and $\binom{k}{3}$ possibilities. This proves all
three coefficients, including the mixed term and its sign.

$c_0>0$, even the displayed negative powers at $k=1,2$ are defined;
their accompanying zero combinatorial factors give the correct
