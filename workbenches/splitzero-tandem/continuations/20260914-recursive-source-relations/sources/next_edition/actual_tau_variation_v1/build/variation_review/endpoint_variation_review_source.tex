\subsubsection{1. Original finite spaces and their types}\label{endpoint-variation_review--original-finite-spaces-and-their-types}

Fix the actual \(h,k,\chi=\chi_{h,k}\) of the source, with \(k\ge1\), monic \(\chi\) of degree \(q\ge1\), and \(N\ge q-1\). Set

\[
U=\mathcal P_{N-q},\qquad P=\mathcal P_N,\qquad
E=\mathbb C[S]/(\chi),\qquad r=N-q+1.
\tag{AV1}
\]

At \(N=q-1\), the convention is \(U=\mathcal P_{-1}=\{0\}\), so \(r=0\). The source spaces carry their increasing-power coefficient frames, and \(E\) has its increasing-power remainder frame. The fixed maps are

\[
B:U\longrightarrow P,\quad y\longmapsto\chi y,\qquad
J:P\longrightarrow E,\quad p\longmapsto[p]_\chi,\qquad
s:E\longrightarrow P,
\tag{AV2}
\]

where \(s\) chooses the polynomial of degree less than \(q\). Polynomial division by the original monic \(\chi\) proves

\[
JB=0,\qquad Js=I_E,\qquad
\ker J=\operatorname{im}B,\qquad
P=\operatorname{im}B\oplus\operatorname{im}s.
\tag{AV3}
\]

In particular \(B\) is injective. The coefficient \(y\) in \(p=By+sJp\) is the actual polynomial quotient in division by \(\chi\).

All stars below are conjugate transpose in these fixed frames. Let

\[
M(t)=M_N(t)=M(0)+t\Delta M,\qquad
\Delta M=M(1)-M(0),\qquad H(t)=B^*M(t)B.
\tag{AV4}
\]

These are the source's matrices from \(m_t=(1-t)m_0+tm_1\) on the original line \(S=k/2+iu\); no scalar mass is divided out. Their entries have the original finite moments. For nonzero \(p\in P\), polynomial evaluation vanishes at only finitely many real \(u\), whereas \(m_t\) is positive almost everywhere, so \(p^*M(t)p>0\). Thus \(M(t)\) is positive definite on \(0\le t\le1\). For nonzero \(y\in U\), injectivity of \(B\) gives \(y^*H(t)y=(By)^*M(t)(By)>0\).

The lower bound \[
M(t)\succeq
\min\{\lambda_{\min}(M(0)),\lambda_{\min}(M(1))\}I_P
\] holds on the compact interval. Continuity gives a real open neighborhood of this interval on which \(M(t)\) remains positive definite. The corresponding assertion holds for \(H(t)\) when \(r>0\). All inverse matrices and sections below are smooth there. This uses finite matrices only.

\subsubsection{2. The actual section and its minimum property}\label{endpoint-variation_review--the-actual-section-and-its-minimum-property}

Every lift of \(x\in E\) is uniquely \(sx+By\), with \(y\in U\). Define

\[
d=H^{-1}B^*Ms:E\longrightarrow U,\qquad
R=s-Bd:E\longrightarrow P.
\tag{AV5}
\]

For \(x\in E\) and \(y\in U\), direct expansion gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
(sx+By)^*M(sx+By)
={}&x^*(s^*Ms-s^*MBH^{-1}B^*Ms)x\\
&+(y+dx)^*H(y+dx).
\end{aligned}
\tag{AV6}
\]

Here \(H^{-1}\) is Hermitian, and \(d^*Hd=s^*MBH^{-1}B^*Ms\). Thus the unique least-norm lift is \(Rx\), attained at \(y=-dx\). Direct substitution gives

\[
JR=I_E,\qquad B^*MR=0,\qquad
G:=R^*MR=s^*Ms-s^*MBH^{-1}B^*Ms.
\tag{AV7}
\]

The map \(R\) is injective because \(JR=I_E\). Therefore \(G\) is positive definite, so its inverse exists before using the inverse quotient formula.

\subsubsection{3. Inverse quotient formula without circularity}\label{endpoint-variation_review--inverse-quotient-formula-without-circularity}

For every \(p\in P\), the vector \(p-RJp\) lies in \(\ker J\). Exactness and injectivity of \(B\) therefore give a unique \(y\in U\) with

\[
p=By+RJp.
\tag{AV8}
\]

Multiplication by \(R^*M\), using \(R^*MB=(B^*MR)^*=0\), gives

\[
R^*Mp=GJp\quad\hbox{for every }p\in P,\qquad
R^*M=GJ,\qquad MR=J^*G.
\tag{AV9}
\]

The last equality is the conjugate transpose of the preceding equality, since \(M,G\) are Hermitian. Multiplying by \(M^{-1}\), then composing with \(J\), proves

\[
R=M^{-1}J^*G,\qquad
I_E=JR=(JM^{-1}J^*)G.
\tag{AV10}
\]

The matrix \(K=JM^{-1}J^*\) is positive definite: \(J^*\) is injective because \(J\) is surjective, and for \(x\ne0\)

\[
x^*Kx=(J^*x)^*M^{-1}(J^*x)>0.
\tag{AV11}
\]

Consequently \(G=K^{-1}\). This proves the inverse quotient identity from the literal section and orthogonality, without presupposing its consequence about the remainder.

\subsubsection{4. Determinant-line sign and metric}\label{endpoint-variation_review--determinant-line-sign-and-metric}

Let \(y_1,\ldots,y_r\) be the increasing-power frame of \(U\), and \(x_1,\ldots,x_q\) the increasing-power remainder frame of \(E\). In the increasing-power frame of \(P\),

\[
[s\ B]=
\begin{pmatrix}I_q&T\\0&U_\chi\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det U_\chi=1.
\tag{AV12}
\]

Indeed, relation column \(j\), starting with \(j=0\), is \(S^j\chi(S)\) and has highest term \(S^{q+j}\) of coefficient one. In the bottom rows corresponding to \(S^q,\ldots,S^N\), no entry occurs below that diagonal. Hence \(U_\chi\) is upper triangular with all diagonal entries one, proving \(\det[s\ B]=1\).

Moving the first \(q\) columns past the last \(r\) columns uses exactly \(qr\) transpositions. The relation-first frame therefore has

\[
L=[B\ s],\qquad \det L=(-1)^{qr}.
\tag{AV13}
\]

The determinant-line map is literally

\[
\begin{aligned}
\det U\otimes\det E&\longrightarrow\det P,\\
(y_1\wedge\cdots\wedge y_r)\otimes(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_q)
&\longmapsto
By_1\wedge\cdots\wedge By_r
\wedge sx_1\wedge\cdots\wedge sx_q .
\end{aligned}
\tag{AV14}
\]

The wedge is nonzero by (AV3), so this is an isomorphism of one-dimensional lines. Replacing \(sx_j\) by \(sx_j+Bz_j\) leaves it unchanged: every term in the difference contains \(r+1\) vectors in the \(r\)-dimensional space \(\operatorname{im}B\). This establishes section independence, while preserving the displayed frame sign.

The change from \(s\) to \(R=s-Bd\) is

\[
[B\ R]=[B\ s]
\begin{pmatrix}I_U&-d\\0&I_E\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
[B\ R]^*M[B\ R]=
\begin{pmatrix}H&0\\0&G\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{AV15}
\]

The right factor in the first equality has determinant one. The second equality is exactly (AV7). Taking determinants retains the orientation scalar explicitly:

\[
\det H\,\det G
=\overline{(-1)^{qr}}\det M\,(-1)^{qr}
=|(-1)^{qr}|^2\det M=\det M.
\tag{AV16}
\]

The squared norm of a wedge is its Gram determinant. Thus (AV16) also proves that (AV14), using the relation metric \(H\) and quotient metric \(G\), is the product metric map. The orientation remains (AV13); its squared absolute value is one in the metric equation. In particular

\[
V(t)=\det G(t)=\frac{\det M(t)}{\det H(t)}.
\tag{AV17}
\]

All original source masses remain in these matrices and determinants.

\subsubsection{5. First variation of the section and Gram}\label{endpoint-variation_review--first-variation-of-the-section-and-gram}

The maps \(B,J,s\) are fixed in \(t\). From \(H=B^*MB\) and \(H^{-1}H=I_U\),

\[
\dot H=B^*\Delta M B,\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}H^{-1}=-H^{-1}\dot H H^{-1}.
\tag{AV18}
\]

Differentiating \(d=H^{-1}B^*Ms\) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot d
&=-H^{-1}(B^*\Delta M B)H^{-1}B^*Ms+H^{-1}B^*\Delta M s\\
&=H^{-1}B^*\Delta M(s-Bd)
=H^{-1}B^*\Delta M R.
\end{aligned}
\tag{AV19}
\]

Consequently, as maps on the original spaces,

\[
\dot R=-BH^{-1}B^*\Delta M R:E\longrightarrow P,\qquad
\operatorname{im}\dot R\subseteq\operatorname{im}B,\qquad J\dot R=0.
\tag{AV20}
\]

Differentiating \(G=R^*MR\) gives all three terms

\[
\dot G=\dot R^*MR+R^*\Delta M R+R^*M\dot R.
\tag{AV21}
\]

The first and third vanish by the relation-valued derivative and \(B^*MR=0\). Therefore

\[
\dot G=R^*\Delta M R.
\tag{AV22}
\]

This is a Hermitian matrix on the unchanged remainder space \(E\).

\subsubsection{6. Second variation of the quotient Gram}\label{endpoint-variation_review--second-variation-of-the-quotient-gram}

The matrix \(\Delta M\) is fixed and Hermitian, so differentiating (AV22) gives

\[
\ddot G=\dot R^*\Delta M R+R^*\Delta M\dot R
=-2R^*\Delta M BH^{-1}B^*\Delta M R.
\tag{AV23}
\]

The two terms are equal because \(H^{-1}\) and \(\Delta M\) are Hermitian. Their sign is established on each actual \(x\in E\) by

\[
x^*\ddot Gx
=-2(B^*\Delta M Rx)^*H^{-1}(B^*\Delta M Rx)
=-2\|H^{-1/2}B^*\Delta M Rx\|^2\le0.
\tag{AV24}
\]

Thus the factor \(2\) in AT7 retains both differentiated occurrences of \(R\).

\subsubsection{7. Both logarithmic derivative formulas}\label{endpoint-variation_review--both-logarithmic-derivative-formulas}

For any positive definite differentiable finite matrix \(A\), multilinearity of the determinant gives \(\det(I+\varepsilon D)=1+\varepsilon\operatorname{Tr}D+O(\varepsilon^2)\): the terms choosing exactly one perturbed column sum to the diagonal trace. Applying this to \(\det(A+\varepsilon\dot A)=\det A\det(I+\varepsilon A^{-1}\dot A)\) therefore proves

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\log\det A=\operatorname{Tr}(A^{-1}\dot A).
\tag{AV25}
\]

Apply (AV25) to (AV17), and separately to \(G\) using (AV22):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt}\log V
&=\operatorname{Tr}(M^{-1}\Delta M)
-\operatorname{Tr}(H^{-1}B^*\Delta M B)\\
&=\operatorname{Tr}(G^{-1}R^*\Delta M R).
\end{aligned}
\tag{AV26}
\]

The traces are on \(P,U,E\), respectively. The equality follows from the proved determinant identity, so it requires no unproved rearrangement across different domains.

Differentiating \(G^{-1}G=I_E\) gives \(\dot G^{-1}=-G^{-1}\dot G G^{-1}\). A second differentiation of the last expression in (AV26), followed by (AV23), now retains both terms:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\log V
&=\operatorname{Tr}(G^{-1}\ddot G)
-\operatorname{Tr}(G^{-1}\dot G G^{-1}\dot G)\\
&=-2\operatorname{Tr}(G^{-1}R^*\Delta M BH^{-1}B^*\Delta M R)
-\operatorname{Tr}(G^{-1}\dot G G^{-1}\dot G).
\end{aligned}
\tag{AV27}
\]

Their exact norm realizations are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr}(G^{-1}R^*\Delta M BH^{-1}B^*\Delta M R)
&=\|H^{-1/2}B^*\Delta M RG^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2,\\
\operatorname{Tr}(G^{-1}\dot G G^{-1}\dot G)
&=\|G^{-1/2}\dot G G^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{AV28}
\]

For the first, multiply \(X^*X\) with \(X=H^{-1/2}B^*\Delta M RG^{-1/2}:E\to U\), and take its trace on \(E\). For the second, \(Y=G^{-1/2}\dot G G^{-1/2}\) is Hermitian, so \(\operatorname{Tr}(Y^*Y)=\operatorname{Tr}(Y^2)\). Cyclicity of the trace on \(E\) gives exactly the two expressions in (AV28). Formula (AV27) is consequently \(-2\|X\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2-\|Y\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\le0\).

Differentiating the first line of (AV26) also gives the compatible full source/relation expression

\[
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\log V
=-\operatorname{Tr}(M^{-1}\Delta M M^{-1}\Delta M)
+\operatorname{Tr}(H^{-1}\dot H H^{-1}\dot H).
\tag{AV29}
\]

Equations (AV27)--(AV28) prove why this particular difference is nonpositive for the exact quotient.

\subsubsection{8. Empty relation domain and equality}\label{endpoint-variation_review--empty-relation-domain-and-equality}

When \(N=q-1\), \(U=\{0\}\), \(P=\mathcal P_{q-1}\), and \(J,s\) identify the same increasing-power frame. The only endomorphism of \(U\) is both its zero and identity map; it is invertible on \(U\), with itself as inverse. Its determinant is \(1\), and its trace is \(0\). The maps \(B:U\to P\) and \(B^*:P\to U\) are the corresponding zero maps. Every composition through \(U\) is the correctly typed zero map on its stated exterior domain and codomain. Thus

\[
R=s,\qquad \dot R=0,\qquad
G=s^*Ms,\qquad \dot G=s^*\Delta M s,\qquad \ddot G=0.
\tag{AV30}
\]

The determinant-line map uses the empty wedge \(1\in\det U=\mathbb C\); its sign is \((-1)^{q\cdot0}=1\). Formula (AV17) is \(\det G=\det M\) in the stated frames. The logarithmic second derivative still retains the inverse-Gram term:

\[
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\log V
=-\|G^{-1/2}\dot G G^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
\tag{AV31}
\]

For either \(r=0\) or \(r>0\), both terms in (AV28) vanish exactly when \(B^*\Delta M R=0\) and \(R^*\Delta M R=0\). The matrix \([B\ R]\) is invertible by (AV8), so its conjugate transpose is invertible. These equations are therefore equivalent to

\[
\Delta M R=0:E\longrightarrow P.
\tag{AV32}
\]

Conversely this identity makes both terms vanish directly. Thus equality in the second-derivative inequality is characterized by the actual Gram increment annihilating the current canonical section, including the empty relation case.
