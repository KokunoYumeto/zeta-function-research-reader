\begin{abstract}
We construct a common algebraic mechanism behind a sequence of integer-valued defects
\[
        -1,-2,-3,\ldots,
\]
which arise in split-zero support semirings, semilocal adele-class crossed products, cyclic orbifold fusion lines, CUE derivative moments, Eisenstein lattice transforms, and elliptic-fibration gauge enhancement.  The primitive object is the negative augmentation, rank, or dimension character
\[
        \delta=-d:D\longrightarrow \ZZ
\]
on a free abelian group of primitive support or sector labels.  For a rank-$r$ primitive source this gives
\[
        D_r=\ZZ^r,\qquad \delta_r(a_1,\ldots,a_r)=-(a_1+\cdots+a_r),
\]
and its kernel is canonically the $A_{r-1}$ root lattice.  Thus the scalar defect $-r$ is the one-dimensional shadow of the $A_{r-1}$ cancellation lattice; the case $r=1$ is the rootless $A_0$ or primitive $U(1)$ layer, while $r=3$ yields the Eisenstein $A_2$ plane.  We prove the corresponding zero taxonomy in the split-zero semiring, identify the finite-prime local boundary extension in the semilocal crossed product and compute its $K$-theoretic boundary map, construct the cyclic and full orbifold-double realizations, derive the block-matrix functor explaining the CUE first-derivative exponent $s^2+2s=\dim\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$, and give the explicit $3\times 3$ Weyl-operator model for the $\ZZ_3$ double whose nonidentity operators span $\mathfrak{sl}_3$.  We also record the $A_2$ Epstein zeta calculation, the $12A_2$--Leech/moonshine bridge, and the F-theory interpretation in which the primitive $-1$ layer is the recurring $U(1)$ rank contribution while $A_{r-1}$ singularities give $SU(r)$ enhancement.  The paper distinguishes theorem-level constructions from conjectural transform identifications, especially between CUE compact Mellin measures and Epstein lattice zeta functions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Purpose and status of the construction}

The constructions below organize a collection of apparently separate phenomena around one elementary but rigid algebraic datum.  Let $D$ be a free abelian group generated by primitive sectors.  In every realization considered here there is a natural dimension, rank, augmentation, or Frobenius-Perron dimension character
\[
        d:D\to \ZZ\quad\text{or}\quad d:D\to\RR
\]
sending a primitive dimension-one sector to $1$.  The defect convention is
\[
        \delta=-d.
\]
Thus each primitive sector contributes $-1$.  The nontrivial information is not the integer alone, but the kernel of the character.  For $r$ primitive sectors the kernel is the $A_{r-1}$ lattice.  This turns the scalar sequence $-1,-2,-3,\ldots$ into the root-lattice tower
\[
        A_0,A_1,A_2,\ldots.
\]

The paper contains three kinds of statements.  First, there are direct theorem-level constructions and computations.  These include the split-zero zero taxonomy, the augmentation kernel calculation, the finite-prime local boundary map, the cyclic group-ring augmentation ideal, the $\ZZ_3$ Weyl-operator matrices, the CUE exponent block decomposition, and the $A_2$ Epstein zeta formula.  Second, there are literature-anchored interpretations, such as the F-theory dictionary $A_{r-1}\mapsto SU(r)$ and Mordell-Weil rank $\mapsto U(1)$ gauge rank, the DVVV orbifold stabilizer labels, and the Miyamoto $\ZZ_3$ simple-current triple.  Third, there are explicitly stated conjectural or functorial bridges, most notably between compact Mellin transform algebras in CUE and Epstein theta/Mellin transforms of root lattices.

No claim below is a proof of RH, BSD, moonshine, or a full Standard Model construction.  The point is narrower and more constructive: a single primitive-defect formalism produces exact algebraic objects which are already known to occur in those settings.

\section{Split-zero support and the zero taxonomy}

\subsection{The split-zero semiring}

\begin{definition}[Split-zero semiring]
Let $R$ be a commutative ring.  Define
\[
        G(R)=R\sqcup\{\tau\}
\]
with operations
\[
        r\oplus s=r+s,\qquad r\oplus\tau=r,\qquad \tau\oplus\tau=\tau,
\]
for $r,s\in R$, and
\[
        rs=rs,\qquad r\tau=\tau r=\tau,
        \qquad \tau^2=\tau.
\]
The element $\tau$ is the semiring zero.  The ordinary ring-zero $0_R\in R\subset G(R)$ is a supported arithmetic zero and is not equal to $\tau$.
\end{definition}

The point of the construction is that the ordinary arithmetic projection collapses two zero-like objects while the support projection separates them.

\begin{definition}[Arithmetic and support projections]
Define
\[
        p_R:G(R)\to R,
        \qquad p_R(\tau)=0_R,\quad p_R(r)=r,
\]
and
\[
        \chi_R:G(R)\to\BB,
        \qquad \chi_R(\tau)=0,
        \quad \chi_R(r)=1\quad(r\in R),
\]
where $\BB=\{0,1\}$ is the Boolean semiring.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Three zero statuses]
There are three different statuses which ordinary ring language collapses.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\tau$ is the unsupported semiring zero.
\item $0_R$ is the supported arithmetic zero.
\item In the coordinate presentation
\[
        G(R)\cong D_R:=\{(0,0)\}\cup(R\times\{1\})\subset R\times\BB,
\]
the points $(r,0)$ with $r\ne0$ are disallowed unsupported nonzero values.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover
\[
        p_R^{-1}(0_R)=\{\tau,0_R\},
\]
while
\[
        \chi_R^{-1}(0)=\{\tau\},\qquad \chi_R^{-1}(1)=R.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The coordinate embedding sends
\[
        \tau\mapsto(0,0),\qquad r\mapsto(r,1).
\]
Thus $0_R$ is $(0,1)$ and is supported.  The arithmetic projection is the first coordinate, so $\tau$ and $0_R$ both map to $0_R$.  The Boolean support projection is the second coordinate, so it separates $\tau$ from every element of $R$, including $0_R$.  Since no pair $(r,0)$ with $r\ne0$ lies in $D_R$, unsupported nonzero values are not part of the semiring.  This proves every assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Support and arithmetic zero primes]
For every nonzero commutative ring $R$, the subset
\[
        \mathfrak s_R=\{\tau\}
\]
is a prime ideal of $G(R)$.  If $R$ is an integral domain, then
\[
        \mathfrak z_R=\{\tau,0_R\}
\]
is also a prime ideal.  Hence in the domain case there is a canonical chain
\[
        \{\tau\}\subset\{\tau,0_R\}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The set $\{\tau\}$ is an ideal because $\tau\oplus\tau=\tau$ and $x\tau=\tau x=\tau$ for all $x\in G(R)$.  If $xy\in\{\tau\}$ and neither factor is $\tau$, then $x,y\in R$ and their product in $G(R)$ is the ordinary product in $R$, hence lies in $R$ and cannot be $\tau$.  Therefore $x\in\{\tau\}$ or $y\in\{\tau\}$, proving primality.

Now suppose $R$ is a domain.  The subset $\{\tau,0_R\}$ is closed under addition and is multiplicatively absorbing.  If $xy\in\{\tau,0_R\}$ and neither factor is $\tau$, then $x,y\in R$ and $xy=0_R$.  Since $R$ is a domain, $x=0_R$ or $y=0_R$.  Thus one factor lies in $\{\tau,0_R\}$.  This proves primality.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The two prime ideals should not be conflated.  The smaller prime $\{\tau\}$ is the support prime.  The larger prime $\{\tau,0_R\}$ is the arithmetic zero prime.  The quotient by support forgets arithmetic value; the quotient by arithmetic zero forgets the distinction between $\tau$ and $0_R$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Support linearization and the first defect functor}

Let $I$ be a finite set.  The support of an element of a free split-zero semimodule $G(R)^I$ is a subset of $I$.  Therefore the support skeleton is a Boolean cube $\mathcal P(I)$.  Linearizing this cube gives the free abelian group on $I$.

\begin{definition}[Support linearization]
Define
\[
        \mathcal L_I:\mathcal P(I)\to\ZZ^I,
        \qquad
        J\mapsto\sum_{j\in J}e_j.
\]
Define the primitive support-defect functional
\[
        \delta_I:\ZZ^I\to\ZZ,
        \qquad
        \delta_I\left(\sum_{i\in I}a_ie_i\right)=-\sum_{i\in I}a_i.
\]
\end{definition}

Thus a support subset of size $r$ contributes defect $-r$:
\[
        \delta_I(\mathcal L_I(J))=-|J|.
\]

\begin{theorem}[The support defect kernel]
If $|I|=r$, then
\[
        \ker(\delta_I)\cong A_{r-1}.
\]
For $r=1$, this is the zero lattice $A_0=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
After choosing an ordering of $I$, identify $\ZZ^I$ with $\ZZ^r$.  Then
\[
        \ker(\delta_I)=\{(a_1,\ldots,a_r):a_1+\cdots+a_r=0\}.
\]
For $1\le i\le r-1$, let
\[
        \alpha_i=e_i-e_{i+1}.
\]
Every $\alpha_i$ lies in the kernel.  Conversely, if $a=(a_1,\\ldots,a_r)$ has coordinate sum zero, define $b_i=a_1+\cdots+a_i$.  Then
\[
        a=\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}b_i(e_i-e_{i+1}).
\]
The Gram matrix of the $\alpha_i$ under the standard inner product is
\[
        \langle\alpha_i,\alpha_j\rangle=\begin{cases}
        2,&i=j,\\
        -1,&|i-j|=1,\\
        0,&|i-j|>1.
        \end{cases}
\]
This is the Cartan matrix of type $A_{r-1}$.  If $r=1$, the kernel is zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The recursive primitive $U(1)$ layer]
The defect source decomposes as
\[
        \ZZ^r=\ZZ e_1\oplus\cdots\oplus\ZZ e_r,
\]
and each summand is a primitive rank-one charge with defect $-1$.  Thus the $A_{r-1}$ root lattice is obtained by imposing cancellation among $r$ repeated $U(1)$-type primitive charges.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each summand $\ZZ e_i$ maps by $\delta_I$ to $\ZZ$ via $e_i\mapsto -1$.  The full kernel consists of all integral linear combinations whose total primitive charge is zero.  The simple roots $e_i-e_{i+1}$ are precisely pairwise cancellations of adjacent primitive rank-one charges.
\end{proof}

This proves the precise sense in which the $-1$ primitive recurs at every defect level.  The $-1$ layer is rootless by itself, but each root is a difference of two such primitive charges.

\section{The augmentation, dimension, and rank character}

\subsection{The existing algebraic object}

The primitive defect is not an invented numerical convention.  It is the negative of a standard character: augmentation, rank, ordinary representation dimension, or Frobenius-Perron dimension.

\begin{definition}[Primitive dimension character]
Let $D$ be a free abelian group on primitive labels $X$.  The augmentation character is
\[
        \epsilon_X:\ZZ[X]\to\ZZ,
        \qquad
        \epsilon_X\left(\sum_{x\in X}a_x[x]\right)=\sum_{x\in X}a_x.
\]
The defect character is
\[
        \delta_X=-\epsilon_X.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
If $D=K_0(C(Y))$ and $[E]$ is a vector bundle class, the dimension character is the rank $\operatorname{rk}(E)$.  If $D=R(G)$ is a representation ring, the dimension character is $\dim$.  If $D=K_0(\mathcal C)$ is the Grothendieck group of a fusion category, the corresponding positive dimension character is $\operatorname{FPdim}$.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Universal primitive defect]
In each of the following categories, the primitive defect is the negative of the canonical dimension/rank character.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item For a finite support set $X$, $\delta=-\epsilon_X$.
\item For complex vector bundles, $\delta=-\operatorname{rk}$.
\item For finite-dimensional group representations, $\delta=-\dim$.
\item For pointed fusion lines, $\delta=-\operatorname{FPdim}$.
\end{enumerate}
If all primitive sectors have dimension one and there are $r$ of them, the defect-free virtual sector is $A_{r-1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In each listed case the indicated dimension/rank character is additive under direct sum and sends a primitive dimension-one object to $1$.  Its negative therefore sends each primitive to $-1$.  For $r$ primitive dimension-one objects, the Grothendieck group is $\ZZ^r$ and the dimension character is the sum of coordinates.  Its kernel is $A_{r-1}$ by the theorem of the previous section.
\end{proof}

This is the actual relation between the integer-valued primitive defect patterns: they are negative dimension characters.  The nontrivial geometry is the virtual dimension-zero lattice.

\subsection{Diagonalization by the cyclic $S$-matrix}

For a cyclic pointed fusion line, the dimension character is the trivial Fourier component.

Let
\[
        C_r=\langle g:g^r=1\rangle,
        \qquad \omega=e^{2\pi i/r}.
\]
The group algebra $\CC[C_r]$ has basis $g^a$, $a\in\ZZ/r\ZZ$.  The Fourier matrix is
\[
        S_{ab}=\frac1{\sqrt r}\omega^{ab},\qquad a,b\in\ZZ/r\ZZ.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Defect as the trivial Fourier projection]
For
\[
        x=\sum_{a=0}^{r-1}c_ag^a\in\CC[C_r],
\]
the augmentation is
\[
        \epsilon(x)=\sum_{a=0}^{r-1}c_a
        =\sqrt r\sum_{a=0}^{r-1}c_a S_{a0}.
\]
Therefore
\[
        \ker(\epsilon)\otimes\CC
\]
is the span of the nontrivial Fourier modes $b=1,\ldots,r-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $S_{a0}=1/\sqrt r$ for all $a$, one has
\[
        \sqrt r\sum_a c_aS_{a0}=\sum_a c_a=\epsilon(x).
\]
The Fourier transform diagonalizes the group algebra.  The trivial Fourier mode is $b=0$; the kernel of the projection to that mode is the span of all nontrivial modes.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[$r=3$]
For $C_3$, the kernel of the augmentation is
\[
        \CC_\omega\oplus \CC_{\omega^2},
\]
the complexification of the $A_2$ lattice.
\end{corollary}

This is the precise sense in which ``negative dimension'' is diagonalized by the fusion $S$-matrix: the defect is the negative projection onto the dimension row; virtual dimension-zero sectors are the nontrivial Fourier modes.

\section{Root lattices, Cartan matrices, and the Standard Model seed}

\subsection{The $A$-series matrices}

For $r\ge1$, the Cartan matrix of $A_{r-1}$ is the $(r-1)\times(r-1)$ matrix
\[
        C(A_{r-1})_{ij}=2\delta_{ij}-\delta_{i,j+1}-\delta_{i+1,j}.
\]
For $r=1$ this is the empty $0\times0$ matrix.  Explicitly:
\[
        A_0: \quad []
\]
\[
        A_1:\quad [2],
\]
\[
        A_2:\quad
        \begin{pmatrix}
        2&-1\\
        -1&2
        \end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
        A_3:\quad
        \begin{pmatrix}
        2&-1&0\\
        -1&2&-1\\
        0&-1&2
        \end{pmatrix}.
\]

The determinant is
\[
        \det C(A_{r-1})=r.
\]
Thus for $A_2$,
\[
        \det\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}=3,
\]
and hence the weight/root quotient has order $3$.  For simply connected $SU(3)$ this quotient is the center:
\[
        P(A_2)/Q(A_2)\cong Z(SU(3))\cong\ZZ/3\ZZ.
\]

\subsection{The rootless primitive and repeated $U(1)$}

The rank-one primitive source
\[
        \ZZ\xrightarrow{-1}\ZZ
\]
has zero kernel.  It is therefore not a nontrivial $SU(1)$ root datum.  It is the primitive abelian charge datum.  This is the correct algebraic shadow of a $U(1)$ charge: one dimension-one sector, no roots, and no cancellation until another primitive sector is supplied.

For $r\ge2$, $A_{r-1}$ is produced by imposing the zero-total-charge condition on $r$ primitive $U(1)$ charges.  Thus $U(1)$ appears recursively: every simple root
\[
        e_i-e_{i+1}
\]
is a cancellation between two primitive $U(1)$-type charges.

\subsection{The Standard Model root seed}

The Standard Model gauge algebra has semisimple root part
\[
        \mathfrak{su}_3\oplus\mathfrak{su}_2
\]
and one abelian factor $\mathfrak u_1$.  At the level of root and primitive-defect data this is
\[
        A_2\oplus A_1\oplus A_0.
\]
Equivalently, before imposing augmentation kernels, the primitive source is
\[
        D_3\oplus D_2\oplus D_1
        =\ZZ^3\oplus\ZZ^2\oplus\ZZ.
\]
The total defect of the sum of all primitive generators is
\[
        -3-2-1=-6,
\]
while the cancellation lattice is
\[
        A_2\oplus A_1\oplus A_0.
\]

\begin{remark}
This is not a derivation of the Standard Model representation content or hypercharge normalization.  It is the exact root/abelian skeleton: $SU(3)$ from $A_2$, $SU(2)$ from $A_1$, and $U(1)$ from the rootless primitive $A_0$ layer.  F-theory supplies a geometric realization of this skeleton through ADE singular fibers and Mordell-Weil rank, discussed later.
\end{remark}

\section{Semilocal boundary maps and the finite-prime local edge}

\subsection{The finite-prime local model}

Let $S$ be a finite set of rational places containing finite primes $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ and the archimedean place.  Put
\[
        \Gamma_S=\ZZ[1/p_1,\ldots,1/p_n]^\times.
\]
The semilocal visible model keeps the generic stratum and the one-zero boundary strata.  The finite-prime boundary at a prime $p$ is modeled locally by a valuation compactification
\[
        \ZZ\cup\{\infty\},
\]
where $m\to+\infty$ corresponds to the $p$-adic coordinate tending to $0$.

Fix $p$ and let $\ell_p=\log p$.  Define
\[
        Y_p=(\ZZ\cup\{\infty\})\times\RR
\]
with the action of $\ZZ$ generated by
\[
        T(m,y)=(m+1,y+\ell_p),
\]
\[
        T(\infty,y)=(\infty,y+\ell_p).
\]
Let
\[
        E_p=C_0(Y_p)\rtimes_T\ZZ,
\]
\[
        I_p=C_0(\ZZ\times\RR)\rtimes_T\ZZ,
\]
and
\[
        Q_p=C_0(\{\infty\}\times\RR)\rtimes_T\ZZ.
\]
Then
\[
        0\to I_p\to E_p\to Q_p\to0
\]
is the finite-prime boundary extension.

\begin{lemma}[Morita equivalences]
One has
\[
        I_p\sim_M C_0(\RR),
        \qquad Q_p\sim_M C(S^1).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On the generic part $\ZZ\times\RR$, the invariant coordinate is
\[
        t=y-m\ell_p.
\]
Indeed,
\[
        (y+\ell_p)-(m+1)\ell_p=y-m\ell_p.
\]
The action is free and proper, and the orbit space is $\RR$.  Green imprimitivity gives
\[
        I_p\sim_M C_0(\RR).
\]
On the boundary $\{\infty\}\times\RR$, the action is translation by $\ell_p$, so the quotient is
\[
        \RR/\ell_p\ZZ\cong S^1.
\]
Again Green imprimitivity gives
\[
        Q_p\sim_M C(S^1).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Finite-prime boundary map]
Under the identifications
\[
        K_0(Q_p)\cong K_0(C(S^1))\cong\ZZ[1]
\]
and
\[
        K_1(I_p)\cong K_1(C_0(\RR))\cong\ZZ,
\]
the connecting map
\[
        \partial_p:K_0(Q_p)\to K_1(I_p)
\]
satisfies
\[
        \partial_p([1])=-1
\]
when counterclockwise winding represents $+1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let
\[
        \phi(t)=\frac1{1+e^t}.
\]
Then
\[
        \lim_{t\to-\infty}\phi(t)=1,
        \qquad
        \lim_{t\to+\infty}\phi(t)=0.
\]
On the generic quotient coordinate $t=y-m\ell_p$, this defines a self-adjoint lift $h$ of the boundary projection $1\in C(S^1)$: at the boundary $m\to+\infty$ with $y$ bounded, $t\to-\infty$ and $h\to1$.

The boundary map sends $[1]$ to the $K_1$-class of
\[
        u(t)=\exp(2\pi i\phi(t)).
\]
As $t$ increases from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, the argument decreases from $2\pi$ to $0$.  Thus the loop winds once clockwise.  With counterclockwise generator $+1$, this class is $-1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Identification with the semilocal crossed-product edge}

\begin{theorem}[Finite local edge equals the semilocal boundary edge]
Let $A_{\varnothing p}$ be the intermediate semilocal crossed-product algebra obtained by adjoining the $p$-boundary stratum to the generic stratum.  After Morita reduction by the remaining free valuation and sign directions, the extension
\[
        0\to A_\varnothing\to A_{\varnothing p}\to A_p\to0
\]
is Morita equivalent to
\[
        0\to I_p\to E_p\to Q_p\to0.
\]
Consequently the component of the global connecting map on the finite-prime generator is
\[
        [1]_p\longmapsto -1.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Modulo $\ZZ_p^\times$, a nonzero $p$-adic coordinate is determined by its valuation $m\in\ZZ$.  The zero coordinate is the endpoint $m=+\infty$.  Thus the local coordinate normal to the $p$-boundary is $\ZZ\cup\{\infty\}$.

The archimedean coordinate is logarithmic, $y=\log|x_\infty|$.  Multiplication by $p$ changes the $p$-adic valuation by $+1$ and changes $y$ by $\log p$.  Therefore the local action is precisely
\[
        (m,y)\mapsto(m+1,y+\log p),
\]
with the same translation on the boundary endpoint.  The other finite-prime valuation coordinates and the sign coordinate act freely and properly; their crossed-product contribution is a compact-operator Morita factor.  Naturality of the six-term exact sequence under Morita equivalence transports the local boundary map to the semilocal edge.  The previous theorem gives the coefficient $-1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Archimedean local model and APS sign}

The archimedean fixed-point local algebra is
\[
        C=C_0(\RR)\rtimes \ZZ/2\ZZ,
\]
where the nontrivial element acts by $x\mapsto -x$.  Evaluation at $0$ gives
\[
        0\to J\to C\to Q\to0,
\]
with
\[
        J=C_0(\RR\setminus\{0\})\rtimes\ZZ/2\ZZ\sim_M C_0(\RR)
\]
and
\[
        Q=C^*(\ZZ/2\ZZ)\cong\CC\oplus\CC.
\]
Let $U$ be the nontrivial group element and
\[
        p_+=\frac{1+U}{2},\qquad p_-=\frac{1-U}{2}.
\]
The standard exponential-lift computation gives
\[
        \partial_\infty([p_+])=-1,
        \qquad
        \partial_\infty([p_-])=-1.
\]
Thus
\[
        \partial_\infty([p_+]+[p_-])=-2,
        \qquad
        \partial_\infty([p_+]-[p_-])=0.
\]

The path model
\[
        F_t=(1-2t)I_2
\]
has two eigenvalues crossing from positive to negative.  The one-dimensional APS operator
\[
        D_F=\frac d{dt}+F_t
\]
has
\[
        \operatorname{Ind}_{APS}(D_F)=2
\]
with the conventional orientation of the interval.  Therefore
\[
        \partial_\infty([p_+]+[p_-])=-\operatorname{Ind}_{APS}(D_F).
\]
This is an orientation-reversed equality of index classes, not a loose analogy.

\subsection{Global component formula}

For
\[
        S=\{p_1,\ldots,p_n,\infty\},
\]
the boundary quotient has primitive $K_0$-generators
\[
        [1]_{p_1},\ldots,[1]_{p_n},[p_+],[p_-].
\]
The componentwise connecting map is
\[
        \delta_0(k_1,\ldots,k_n,n_+,n_-)
        =-
        \left(\sum_{i=1}^n k_i+n_++n_-\right).
\]
In particular, for $S=\{p,\infty\}$,
\[
        \delta_0([1]_p+[p_+]+[p_-])=-3,
\]
and the liftable lattice is
\[
        \ker(\ZZ^3\xrightarrow{-(1,1,1)}\ZZ)\cong A_2.
\]

\section{Completed zeta principal parts and anti-invariant defects}

Let
\[
        u=s(s-1),\qquad v=2s-1.
\]
Then
\[
        u(1-s)=u(s),\qquad v(1-s)=-v(s),
\]
and
\[
        v^2=1+4u.
\]
The quotient coordinate is $u$; $v$ is the anti-invariant lift coordinate.

\begin{lemma}[Principal-part basis]
One has
\[
        \frac1u=\frac1{s-1}-\frac1s,
\]
and
\[
        \frac vu=\frac1{s-1}+\frac1s.
\]
If
\[
        P(s)=\frac a s+\frac b{s-1},
\]
then
\[
        P(s)=A\frac1u+B\frac vu
\]
with
\[
        A=\frac{b-a}{2},
        \qquad
        B=\frac{a+b}{2}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Expanding
\[
        A\left(\frac1{s-1}-\frac1s\right)+B\left(\frac1{s-1}+\frac1s\right)
\]
gives coefficient $-A+B$ at $1/s$ and coefficient $A+B$ at $1/(s-1)$.  Solving
\[
        a=-A+B,
        \qquad b=A+B
\]
gives the displayed formulas.
\end{proof}

Let
\[
        F_t(s)=\zeta(s,1+t)
\]
and
\[
        \Lambda_t(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)F_t(s).
\]
The residues at $0$ and $1$ are
\[
        \Res_{s=0}\Lambda_t=-1-2t,
        \qquad
        \Res_{s=1}\Lambda_t=1.
\]
Therefore the finite principal part is
\[
        \operatorname{PP}_{\{0,1\}}(\Lambda_t)
        =(-1-2t)\frac1s+1\cdot\frac1{s-1}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Quotient/lift decomposition]
For all $t$,
\[
        \operatorname{PP}_{\{0,1\}}(\Lambda_t)
        =(1+t)\frac1u-t\frac vu.
\]
At $t=1$,
\[
        \operatorname{PP}_{\{0,1\}}(\Lambda_1)
        =2\frac1u-\frac vu.
\]
Thus the primitive anti-invariant coefficient is $-1$, and the two endpoint residues of the anti-invariant term are $-1$ and $-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply the preceding lemma with
\[
        a=-1-2t,
        \qquad b=1.
\]
Then
\[
        A=\frac{1-(-1-2t)}2=1+t,
\]
and
\[
        B=\frac{-1-2t+1}{2}=-t.
\]
This gives the formula.  At $t=1$, the anti-invariant term is
\[
        -\frac vu=-\frac1s-\frac1{s-1},
\]
so its residues at $0$ and $1$ are both $-1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Logarithmic Jacobian]
One has
\[
        d\log u=\frac vu\,ds.
\]
Therefore
\[
        \Res_0(d\log u)=1,
        \qquad
        \Res_1(d\log u)=1,
        \qquad
        \Res_\infty(d\log u)=-2.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since
\[
        u=s(s-1),
\]
we have
\[
        du=(2s-1)ds=v\,ds.
\]
Thus $d\log u=du/u=(v/u)ds$.  The finite residues are the orders of the zeros of $u$ at $0$ and $1$, hence $1$ and $1$.  On the Riemann sphere the sum of residues of a meromorphic differential is zero, so the residue at infinity is $-2$.
\end{proof}

On the critical line
\[
        s=\frac12+i\gamma,
\]
we have
\[
        u=-\left(\gamma^2+\frac14\right)\in\RR,
        \qquad
        v=2i\gamma.
\]
Thus $1/u$ is real and $v/u$ is purely imaginary.  The invariant quotient part and anti-invariant lift part are orthogonal over $\CC$ on the critical line.

\section{Cyclic augmentation, Eisenstein $A_2$, and Epstein zeta}

\subsection{The cyclic triplet}

Let
\[
        C_3=\langle g:g^3=1\rangle.
\]
The group ring is
\[
        \ZZ[C_3]=\ZZ\{1,g,g^2\}.
\]
The augmentation is
\[
        \epsilon(a+bg+cg^2)=a+b+c.
\]
The defect is $-\epsilon$.

\begin{theorem}[The $C_3$ augmentation ideal is $A_2$]
The augmentation ideal
\[
        I_{C_3}=\ker(\epsilon)
\]
is the $A_2$ root lattice under the coefficient inner product.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Identify $1,g,g^2$ with $e_0,e_1,e_2$.  Then
\[
        I_{C_3}=\{a e_0+b e_1+c e_2:a+b+c=0\}.
\]
A basis is
\[
        \alpha_1=1-g,
        \qquad
        \alpha_2=g-g^2.
\]
The Gram matrix is
\[
        \langle\alpha_i,\alpha_j\rangle=
        \begin{pmatrix}
        2&-1\\
        -1&2
        \end{pmatrix}_{ij}.
\]
Thus it is $A_2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Eisenstein realization}

Let
\[
        \omega=e^{2\pi i/3},
        \qquad \OO=\ZZ[\omega].
\]
The evaluation map
\[
        \pi_\omega:\ZZ[C_3]\to\ZZ[\omega],
        \qquad g\mapsto\omega,
\]
has kernel generated by $1+g+g^2$.  The augmentation ideal maps to the Eisenstein ideal $(1-\omega)\ZZ[\omega]$.  In simple-root coordinates, the $A_2$ quadratic form is
\[
        Q_{A_2}(m,n)=2(m^2-mn+n^2).
\]
The Eisenstein norm is
\[
        N(m+n\omega)=m^2-mn+n^2.
\]
Thus
\[
        Q_{A_2}(m,n)=2N(m+n\omega).
\]

\begin{theorem}[$A_2$ Epstein zeta]
With the above normalization,
\[
        Z_{A_2}(s)
        =2^{-s}6\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3}).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
        Z_{A_2}(s)=\sum_{(m,n)\ne(0,0)}\left(2(m^2-mn+n^2)\right)^{-s}.
\]
Therefore
\[
        Z_{A_2}(s)=2^{-s}\sum_{0\ne z\in\ZZ[\omega]}N(z)^{-s}.
\]
The unit group of $\ZZ[\omega]$ has order $6$, and $\ZZ[\omega]$ has class number one.  Hence
\[
        \sum_{0\ne z\in\ZZ[\omega]}N(z)^{-s}
        =6\zeta_{\QQ(\sqrt{-3})}(s).
\]
Finally
\[
        \zeta_{\QQ(\sqrt{-3})}(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3}).
\]
Combining these identities gives the formula.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Twelve Eisenstein planes and the Leech lattice}

The Eisenstein-plane note constructs
\[
        L_0=\frac{2}{9}\rho\ZZ[\omega],
        \qquad \rho=e^{\pi i/3},
\]
and proves that $L_0$ is similar to $A_2$.  Twelve copies globalize to the Niemeier lattice
\[
        N(12A_2),
\]
and the Leech lattice is obtained as a $3$-neighbor of $N(12A_2)$.  It further records
\[
        \Theta_\Lambda=E_4^3-720\Delta,
        \qquad
        j=\Theta_\Lambda/\Delta+720.
\]
The same order-three stabilizer at the hexagonal point forces local modular invariants to descend through a cubic coordinate.  In the language developed here, this is the global lattice-theoretic version of the $C_3$ augmentation-kernel mechanism.

\section{Orbifold irreducibles, stabilizers, and Weyl operators}

\subsection{DVVV stabilizer labels}

For a finite group $G$ acting in a holomorphic orbifold theory, the irreducible twisted-sector labels are of the form
\[
        (C,\rho),
\]
where $C$ is a conjugacy class in $G$ and $\rho\in\Irr(Z_G(g_C))$ is an irreducible representation of the centralizer of a representative $g_C\in C$.  Define
\[
        \operatorname{OrbIrr}(G)=\{(C,\rho)\}.
\]
The primitive defect module is
\[
        D_G=\ZZ[\operatorname{OrbIrr}(G)].
\]
There are two natural characters:
\[
        \epsilon^{\mathrm{prim}}\left(\sum a_{C,\rho}[C,\rho]\right)=\sum a_{C,\rho},
\]
and the quantum-dimension character
\[
        d^{\mathrm{qdim}}(C,\rho)=|C|\dim\rho.
\]
The corresponding defects are their negatives.

\subsection{The $S_3$ check}

Let
\[
        S_3=\langle r,s:r^3=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle.
\]
The conjugacy classes are
\[
        C_1=\{1\},\qquad C_s=\{s,sr,sr^2\},\qquad C_r=\{r,r^2\}.
\]
The centralizers are
\[
        Z_{S_3}(1)=S_3,
        \qquad
        Z_{S_3}(s)=\langle s\rangle\cong\ZZ/2\ZZ,
        \qquad
        Z_{S_3}(r)=\langle r\rangle\cong\ZZ/3\ZZ.
\]
Therefore
\[
        |\operatorname{OrbIrr}(S_3)|=3+2+3=8.
\]
The quantum dimensions are
\[
        (1,1,2,3,3,2,2,2).
\]
Their squared sum is
\[
        1^2+1^2+2^2+3^2+3^2+2^2+2^2+2^2=36=|S_3|^2.
\]

\subsection{The cyclic full double and the Weyl-operator algebra}

For $G=C_r$ abelian,
\[
        \operatorname{OrbIrr}(C_r)=C_r\times\widehat C_r.
\]
Choose
\[
        \omega=e^{2\pi i/r}.
\]
Let $V=\CC^r$ with basis $|m\rangle$, $m\in\ZZ/r\ZZ$.  Define
\[
        X|m\rangle=|m+1\rangle,
        \qquad
        Z|m\rangle=\omega^m|m\rangle.
\]
Then
\[
        ZX=\omega XZ.
\]
For $(a,b)\in\ZZ_r^2$, define
\[
        W_{a,b}=X^aZ^b.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Twisted group algebra]
The multiplication rule is
\[
        W_{a,b}W_{c,d}=\omega^{bc}W_{a+c,b+d}.
\]
The commutator is
\[
        [W_{a,b},W_{c,d}]
        =\left(\omega^{bc}-\omega^{ad}\right)W_{a+c,b+d}.
\]
The $r^2$ operators $W_{a,b}$ form a basis of $M_r(\CC)$.  The identity is $W_{0,0}$, and the nonidentity operators span $\mathfrak{sl}_r(\CC)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The relation $ZX=\omega XZ$ gives
\[
        Z^bX^c=\omega^{bc}X^cZ^b.
\]
Thus
\[
        X^aZ^bX^cZ^d=\omega^{bc}X^{a+c}Z^{b+d}.
\]
Switching $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ gives the commutator formula.  The matrices $X^aZ^b$ are linearly independent because they are distinct shift-phase operators; since there are $r^2$ of them, they form a basis of $M_r(\CC)$.  Only $W_{0,0}=I$ has nonzero trace.  Hence the nonidentity span is the traceless subspace $\mathfrak{sl}_r(\CC)$.
\end{proof}

The fusion algebra itself is not $\mathfrak{sl}_r$; it is a commutative semisimple fusion algebra.  The Lie algebra appears after passing to the twisted group algebra determined by the Heisenberg cocycle
\[
        \alpha((a,b),(c,d))=\omega^{bc}
\]
and then taking commutators.

\subsection{Explicit $3\times3$ matrices}

Then

The eight nonidentity Weyl operators are

        \quad XZ^2,

Explicitly,

\end{pmatrix},
\quad

Together these eight matrices form a Weyl basis of $\mathfrak{sl}_3(\CC)$.

\subsection{Where $\tau$ sits in the matrix model}

The zero matrix is not $\tau$.  In the split-zero globalization of the matrix algebra,
\[
        G(M_r(\CC))=M_r(\CC)\sqcup\{\tau\}.
\]
The zero matrix $0_{M_r}$ is supported.  The element $\tau$ is unsupported.  Therefore a zero coefficient of a Weyl operator means a supported arithmetic zero coefficient, while an absent sector is represented at the support level by $\tau$.  Removing the identity operator to pass from $M_r$ to $\mathfrak{sl}_r$ is a trace projection, not the same operation as replacing a sector by $\tau$.

\section{CUE derivative moments and the $\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$ functor}

\subsection{Matrix-size $A_{N-1}$}

Let $U\in U(N)$ have eigenvalues $z_1,\ldots,z_N$.  Weyl integration involves the factor
\[
        \prod_{1\le i<j\le N}(1-z_i/z_j)(1-z_j/z_i).
\]
The roots of $SU(N)$ are
\[
        e_i-e_j,
        \qquad i\ne j.
\]
The character corresponding to $e_i-e_j$ is $z_i/z_j$.  Therefore the CUE Weyl density is the $A_{N-1}$ root product.

\subsection{Exact first derivative moment}

Let
\[
        \Phi_N(z)=\det(zI-U).
\]
Write
\[
        \Phi_N(z)=\sum_{j=0}^N(-1)^j e_j(U)z^{N-j},
\]
where $e_j$ is the character of $\wedge^j\CC^N$.  Then
\[
        \Phi_N'(1)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(-1)^j(N-j)e_j(U).
\]
By Haar orthogonality of irreducible characters,
\[
        \mathbb E[e_j(U)\overline{e_k(U)}]=\delta_{jk}.
\]
Therefore
\[
        M_N(1):=\mathbb E|\Phi_N'(1)|^2
        =\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(N-j)^2
        =\sum_{m=1}^N m^2
        =\frac{N(N+1)(2N+1)}6.
\]
For $N=3$,
\[
        M_3(1)=1+4+9=14.
\]

\subsection{Moment-order exponent}

The CUE derivative moment theorem of Grover--Mezzadri--Simm gives the microscopic exponent
\[
        |\mu|+|\nu|+s^2
\]
when $\ell(\mu)=\ell(\nu)=s$.  For first-derivative $s$-fold moments,
\[
        \mu=\nu=(1^s),
\]
so
\[
        |\mu|=|\nu|=s.
\]
Thus the exponent is
\[
        s^2+2s=(s+1)^2-1=\dim\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}.
\]
This is not merely a numerical coincidence; it comes from a canonical block decomposition.

\begin{theorem}[The CUE exponent functor]
Let $W=\CC^s$.  The map
\[
        \End(W)\oplus W\oplus W^\vee
        \longrightarrow
        \mathfrak{sl}(W\oplus\CC)
\]
\[
        (A,u,\varphi)\longmapsto
        \begin{pmatrix}
        A&u\\
        \varphi&-\tr(A)
        \end{pmatrix}
\]
is a vector-space isomorphism.  The bracket transported from matrix commutator makes this space into $\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Any element of $\mathfrak{sl}(W\oplus\CC)$ has block form
\[
        \begin{pmatrix}
        B&u\\
        \varphi&\lambda
        \end{pmatrix}
\]
with $\tr(B)+\lambda=0$.  Hence $\lambda=-\tr(B)$ and the element is uniquely determined by $(B,u,\varphi)$.  This gives the isomorphism.  The commutator of traceless block matrices is again traceless, so the transported bracket is exactly the Lie bracket of $\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$.
\end{proof}

The CUE terms correspond to the three summands:
\[
        s^2=\dim\End(W),
\]
\[
        s=\dim W,
\]
\[
        s=\dim W^\vee.
\]
Thus the exponent
\[
        s^2+2s
\]
is the dimension of the block Lie algebra
\[
        \End(W)\oplus W\oplus W^\vee\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}.
\]
For $s=2$ this is
\[
        \End(\CC^2)\oplus\CC^2\oplus(\CC^2)^\vee\cong\mathfrak{sl}_3,
\]
the $A_2/SU(3)$ derivative-moment layer.

\subsection{Compact Mellin peeling and quotient obstruction}

For fixed $N$, the compact Mellin framework realizes
\[
        M_N(k)=\int_0^{N^24^{N-1}}t^k\,d\nu_N(t)
\]
for a compactly supported probability measure.  At $N=3$, a phase-cover construction gives Hermitian-square decompositions upstairs, but the quotient variables
\[
        u=|\lambda|^2,
        \qquad v=|c|^2
\]
can fail to carry a real sum-of-squares representation.  The descended polynomial $\Psi_2^\#(u,v)$ has a Gram matrix with a negative principal minor, so it is not an SOS on the base.

This is a quotient/lift obstruction: positivity exists on the phase cover but is not represented by the quotient variables.  It is structurally parallel to the split-zero distinction between supported zero and unsupported absence, but an explicit functor from the CUE phase-cover trace algebra to the split-zero support category is an additional construction, not automatic.

\section{Nielsen geometry, Weyl penalties, and Epstein transforms}

The geometry of quantum computation places a right-invariant metric on $SU(2^n)$ or $U(2^n)$ by decomposing the Hamiltonian Lie algebra into allowed and penalized subspaces.  Abstractly, choose a basis of Hamiltonians $H_j$ and a positive penalty operator $G$ with
\[
        \langle H_i,H_j\rangle_G=\tr(H_iG(H_j)).
\]

For a finite Heisenberg/Weyl basis $W_{a,b}$, one can choose penalty weights diagonal in the Weyl basis.  To connect to an Epstein zeta, pass to an infinite lattice cover $L$ of the phase labels and define a diagonal positive operator on $\ell^2(L\setminus\{0\})$ by
\[
        \mathcal H_Q e_x=Q(x)e_x.
\]
Then its spectral zeta function is
\[
        \zeta_{\mathcal H_Q}(s)=\sum_{0\ne x\in L}Q(x)^{-s}=Z_L(s).
\]

For $L=A_2$ and $Q=Q_{A_2}$, this gives
\[
        \zeta_{\mathcal H_{A_2}}(s)=Z_{A_2}(s)=2^{-s}6\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3}).
\]
Thus the computational-geometry construction is precise: a Weyl-operator or Hamiltonian geometry with Eisenstein $A_2$ penalty spectrum has Epstein zeta equal to the $A_2$ Epstein zeta.  The finite $3\times3$ Weyl matrices are the mod-$3$ quotient of this Eisenstein phase lattice.

\section{Rank-one spectral triples and the primitive $-1$ operation}

The zeta spectral triples of Connes--Consani--Moscovici construct rank-one perturbations
\[
        D^{(\lambda,N)}_{\log}
        =D^{(\lambda)}_{\log}-|D^{(\lambda)}_{\log}\xi\rangle\langle\delta_N|.
\]
Their theorem identifies the regularized determinant with
\[
        \det_{\mathrm{reg}}(D^{(\lambda,N)}_{\log}-z)
        =-i\lambda^{-iz}\widehat\xi(z),
\]
and the self-adjointness of the perturbed operator forces the zeros of $\widehat\xi$ to lie on the real line in the approximating model.

Structurally, a rank-one perturbation is the operator-theoretic version of adding or removing one primitive boundary/evaluation sector.  In the defect language it is a $-1$ operation.  The exact equality is not between the split-zero semiring and the spectral triple; it is between the algebraic form of the operation: one rank-one evaluation correction contributes one primitive defect.

\section{F-theory, Mordell-Weil rank, and BSD}

F-theory geometrizes the type-IIB axio-dilaton
\[
        \tau=C_0+ie^{-\phi}
\]
as the complex modulus of an elliptic curve.  An elliptic fibration has singular fibers.  The ADE type of a singular fiber controls nonabelian gauge enhancement.  In particular,
\[
        A_{r-1}\text{ singularity}\quad\leadsto\quad SU(r)\text{ enhancement}.
\]
This matches the defect-root tower
\[
        -r\leadsto A_{r-1}\leadsto SU(r).
\]
The abelian side is governed by Mordell-Weil rank:
\[
        \operatorname{rk}MW(\pi)\leadsto U(1)^{\operatorname{rk}MW(\pi)}.
\]
Thus the primitive $-1$ layer is the recurring $U(1)$ rank unit, while nonabelian enhancement occurs when primitive units collide into an $A$-type root configuration.

The Birch--Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture relates the Mordell-Weil rank of an arithmetic elliptic curve to the analytic order of vanishing:
\[
        \operatorname{rk}E(K)=\operatorname{ord}_{s=1}L(E,s).
\]
The F-theory and BSD appearances are not identical without specifying an arithmetic elliptic fibration over a number field.  The common object is Mordell-Weil rank.  In one setting it gives $U(1)$ gauge rank; in the other it is predicted by an $L$-function order of vanishing.

\section{Moonshine, Niemeier lattices, and the pure $A$ tower}

The Niemeier lattices of rank $24$ with nontrivial root systems are labelled by root systems of full rank $24$, all irreducible components having the same Coxeter number.  Umbral moonshine assigns finite groups and vector-valued mock modular forms to these Niemeier root systems.  The pure $A$ cases have root systems
\[
        A_{\ell-1}^{\,24/(\ell-1)}.
\]
Each component $A_{\ell-1}$ is the kernel of
\[
        \ZZ^\ell\xrightarrow{-\Sigma}\ZZ.
\]
Therefore the direct sum of all components is
\[
        \bigoplus_{j=1}^{24/(\ell-1)} A_{\ell-1},
\]
with total rank
\[
        \frac{24}{\ell-1}(\ell-1)=24.
\]
For $\ell=3$, this is
\[
        12A_2.
\]
The Eisenstein-Leech note identifies precisely this lattice as the Niemeier root system globalizing twelve Eisenstein planes, with the Leech lattice obtained as a $3$-neighbor.  The same $\ell=3$ layer therefore connects:
\[
        -3,
        \quad A_2,
        \quad \ZZ[\omega],
        \quad 12A_2,
        \quad \Lambda_{\mathrm{Leech}},
        \quad Z_3\text{-orbifold moonshine constructions}.
\]

Miyamoto's $\ZZ_3$-orbifold construction gives a simple-current triple
\[
        W_0,W_3,W_6
\]
with
\[
        W_3\boxtimes W_3=W_6,
        \qquad
        W_3\boxtimes W_6=W_0.

Thus
\[
        \ZZ\{W_0,W_3,W_6\}\cong\ZZ[C_3].
\]
The augmentation kernel is $A_2$, and the regular triple has defect $-3$.

\section{A consolidated theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Primitive defect realization theorem]
There is a single primitive defect mechanism underlying the constructions in this paper.  It is the negative of a canonical dimension, rank, or augmentation character
\[
        \delta=-d.
\]
For $r$ primitive dimension-one sectors,
\[
        D_r=\ZZ^r,
        \qquad
        \delta_r(a_1,\ldots,a_r)=-(a_1+\cdots+a_r),
\]
and
\[
        \ker(\delta_r)=A_{r-1}.
\]
This mechanism is realized by:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item split-zero support linearization $\mathcal P(I)\to\ZZ^I$;
\item semilocal finite and archimedean boundary maps in crossed-product $K$-theory;
\item cyclic simple-current augmentation $\ZZ[C_r]\to\ZZ$;
\item Eisenstein $A_2$ augmentation and its Epstein zeta;
\item CUE Weyl integration through the $A_{N-1}$ Weyl denominator;
\item CUE first-derivative moment order via
\[
        \End(\CC^s)\oplus\CC^s\oplus(\CC^s)^\vee\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1};
\]
\item F-theory enhancement $A_{r-1}\mapsto SU(r)$ and Mordell-Weil rank $\mapsto U(1)$ rank.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The split-zero support realization was proved by support linearization.  The semilocal finite-prime and archimedean boundary maps were computed by exponential lifts.  The cyclic simple-current realization is the augmentation map on $\ZZ[C_r]$.  The Eisenstein $A_2$ realization follows from $C_3$ augmentation and the identification with $\ZZ[\omega]$.  The CUE Weyl denominator is the product over $A_{N-1}$ roots.  The moment-order functor was proved by the block-matrix identification with $\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$.  The F-theory statement is the standard ADE singularity-to-gauge-enhancement dictionary, with Mordell-Weil rank supplying abelian factors.  In each case the primitive character sends each primitive dimension-one sector to $1$, so its negative sends it to $-1$; the rank-$r$ kernel is $A_{r-1}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Conjectural transform bridge}

The following is the natural next theorem to attempt, but it is not yet proved here.

\begin{conjecture}[CUE/Epstein transform functor]
There exists a functor from the phase-cover transform algebra of fixed-$N$ CUE derivative moments to a lattice-shell transform category such that, for the Eisenstein $N=3$ layer, the compact Mellin measure degenerates or linearizes to the $A_2$ lattice-shell measure whose Mellin transform is $Z_{A_2}(s)$.
\end{conjecture}

The evidence is structural:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item CUE $N=3$ Weyl integration uses the $A_2$ root product.
\item The phase-cover construction retains positivity which the quotient loses.
\item The Eisenstein plane is the universal $A_2$ Euclidean lattice.
\item The cyclic $C_3$ augmentation kernel and the $A_2$ lattice are the same integral object.
\end{enumerate}
The missing ingredient is an explicit transform-level comparison between compact pushforward measures and discrete lattice-shell measures.
