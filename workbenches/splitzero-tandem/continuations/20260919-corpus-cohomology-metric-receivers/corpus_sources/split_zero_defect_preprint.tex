\title[Split-zero support and augmentation defects]{Split-zero support, augmentation defects, and A-type realization patterns}
\author{}
\date{April 2026}

\begin{document}
\begin{abstract}
This article isolates a collection of theorem-level constructions around the split-zero globalization semiring, augmentation defects, semilocal crossed products, cyclic orbifold sector systems, CUE derivative moments, and A-type root data.  The central construction is the negative augmentation character
\[
   -\Sigma:\ZZ^r\longrightarrow \ZZ,
       \qquad (a_1,\ldots,a_r)\longmapsto -(a_1+\cdots+a_r),
\]
whose kernel is the root lattice $A_{r-1}$.  We prove that this character is not a formal numerology: it appears as the support-linearization defect of free split-zero semimodules, as the negative rank or dimension character on primitive boundary sectors, as the cyclic simple-current augmentation, as the finite-prime and archimedean boundary map in the visible semilocal crossed product, and as the first obstruction row in several quotient/lift problems.  The $r=3$ case identifies the triplet defect with the Eisenstein $A_2$ plane, its Epstein zeta transform, and the cyclic $\ZZ_3$ simple-current line.  Separately, the full abelian orbifold double $\ZZ_r\times \widehat{\ZZ_r}$ is shown, after choosing the standard Heisenberg cocycle, to realize $M_r(\CC)$ and hence $\mathfrak{sl}_r(\CC)$ by Weyl operators.  We also construct a functorial vector-space explanation of the first-derivative CUE exponent $s^2+2s=\dim\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$, and record the precise compatibility, and non-identity, between these constructions, Epstein transforms, F-theory rank data, and Mordell-Weil/BSD rank statements.  All claims are formulated independently of any unproved Riemann Hypothesis implication.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The purpose of this paper is to extract a coherent theorem package from a family of constructions in which a single integer-valued defect recurs in several different mathematical settings.  The scalar values
\[
        -1,\quad -2,\quad -3,\quad \ldots
\]
are not taken as primitive.  They are shadows of a more structured object: the negative augmentation, rank, or dimension character on a free abelian group of primitive sectors.  If $X$ is a finite primitive sector set, define
\[
       D(X)=\ZZ[X],
       \qquad
       \epsilon_X\Big(\sum_{x\in X} a_x[x]\Big)=\sum_{x\in X}a_x,
       \qquad
       \delta_X=-\epsilon_X.
\]
When $|X|=r$ and a basis is chosen, this is
\[
       \delta_r:\ZZ^r\to\ZZ,
       \qquad
       (a_1,\ldots,a_r)\mapsto-(a_1+\cdots+a_r).
\]
The total primitive class has defect $-r$, and the defect-free lattice is
\[
       \ker(\delta_r)=\{(a_i)\in\ZZ^r:\sum_i a_i=0\}=A_{r-1}.
\]
Thus the integer $-r$ is the scalar boundary of the $A_{r-1}$ cancellation lattice.

The split-zero globalization semiring, developed in the background notes \cite{splitzero,globalization}, provides the support-theoretic origin of this construction.  For a commutative ring $R$ one adjoins an unsupported zero $\tauz$ to the supported arithmetic sheet $R$.  The ordinary ring zero $0_R$ remains supported and is not identified with $\tauz$ inside the semiring.  The arithmetic projection collapses them, but the support character separates them.  This produces a Boolean support cube for free objects, and linearizing that cube gives the augmentation module above.

The same negative augmentation appears in three additional theorem-level ways, with the semilocal crossed-product side modeled on the Connes--Consani visible semilocal calculations \cite{connesconsani,connesconsanicft}.  First, the completed shifted zeta principal part decomposes into an invariant quotient term and an anti-invariant lift term; the latter has primitive coefficient $-1$ and doubled endpoint contribution $-2$.  Second, in the visible semilocal crossed-product model associated with the generic and one-zero strata, the finite-prime local edge has connecting map $[1]\mapsto -1$, while the archimedean fixed-point edge has two primitive sectors $[p_+]$ and $[p_-]$, each mapping to $-1$.  Third, a cyclic simple-current line $\ZZ[C_r]$ has augmentation ideal $A_{r-1}$ and total regular-class defect $-r$.

The $r=3$ case is the first two-dimensional cancellation case.  Its kernel is $A_2$, equivalently the Eisenstein plane.  The corresponding Epstein zeta function is explicitly
\[
       Z_{A_2}(s)=2^{-s}6\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3}),
\]
for the normalization $Q_{A_2}(m,n)=2(m^2-mn+n^2)$.  This connects the triplet defect to the Eisenstein integer lattice, the $\ZZ_3$ simple-current line, and the $12A_2$ Niemeier/Leech construction.

A distinct but compatible enlargement occurs for a full abelian orbifold double.  For $C_r=\ZZ/r\ZZ$, the sector label set of the untwisted-discrete-torsion-free holomorphic orbifold double is $C_r\times\widehat{C_r}$.  With the standard Heisenberg cocycle, this label set is represented by Weyl operators $X^aZ^b$, satisfying $ZX=\omega XZ$.  The resulting twisted group algebra is $M_r(\CC)$ and the nonidentity operators span $\mathfrak{sl}_r(\CC)$.  This is a commutator realization, not an assertion that the fusion product itself is a Lie bracket.

The CUE derivative moment side, in the form of the higher derivative theorem of Grover--Mezzadri--Simm \cite{cue}, supplies two further appearances of A-type structure.  The Weyl integration formula for $U(N)$ carries the $A_{N-1}$ root product.  Independently, for first-derivative $s$-fold CUE moments the large-$N$ exponent is $s^2+2s$, which admits the block Lie algebra realization
\[
       \End(\CC^s)\oplus \CC^s\oplus (\CC^s)^\vee
       \cong \mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}(\CC).
\]
This gives a constructed functorial explanation of the exponent identity
\[
       s^2+2s=(s+1)^2-1=\dim\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}.
\]

The paper is deliberately conservative about claims.  No statement here proves the Riemann Hypothesis.  No statement identifies a fusion algebra with a Lie algebra without passing through the additional projective Weyl-operator construction.  No statement identifies an F-theory compactification with a BSD theorem without specifying an arithmetic elliptic fibration.  The aim is to isolate the precise algebraic machinery that survives proof.

\subsection*{Guide to the main results}

The paper proves the following constructions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item The split-zero semiring has three zero statuses: the unsupported zero $\tauz$, the supported arithmetic zero $0_R$, and the disallowed unsupported nonzero states in the ambient coordinate model $R\times\BB$.
\item The support prime $\{\tauz\}$ is always prime, and $\{\tauz,0_R\}$ is prime when $R$ is a domain.
\item Linearized split-zero support produces $\ZZ^I\xrightarrow{-\Sigma}\ZZ$ and hence the $A_{|I|-1}$ kernel.
\item The quotient coordinate $u=s(s-1)$ and lift coordinate $v=2s-1$ decompose two-endpoint principal parts; the anti-invariant correction coefficient at the endpoint is $-1$.
\item The finite-prime semilocal local edge is identified with an explicit transformation-group compactification, and its boundary map is $[1]\mapsto -1$.
\item The archimedean local edge $C_0(\RR)\rtimes\ZZ/2\ZZ$ has two primitive sectors, each mapping to $-1$.
\item The cyclic augmentation ideal of $\ZZ[C_r]$ is $A_{r-1}$; for $r=3$ it is the Eisenstein $A_2$ lattice and has Epstein zeta $2^{-s}6\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3})$.
\item The full cyclic orbifold double with Heisenberg cocycle realizes $M_r(\CC)$ and $\mathfrak{sl}_r$ by Weyl commutators.
\item The CUE first-derivative exponent is realized by the block decomposition of $\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$.
\item Rank-theoretic bridges to F-theory and BSD are stated only through Mordell-Weil rank and ADE fiber data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The split-zero semiring and its zero taxonomy}

\subsection{Definition and coordinate model}

Let $R$ be a commutative unital ring.  The split-zero semiring associated with $R$ is the set
\[
       G(R)=R\sqcup\{\tauz\}
\]
with operations
\[
       r\oplus s=r+s,
       \qquad
       r\oplus\tauz=r,
       \qquad
       \tauz\oplus\tauz=\tauz,
\]
for $r,s\in R$, and
\[
       rs=rs,
       \qquad
       r\tauz=\tauz r=\tauz,
       \qquad
       \tauz^2=\tauz.
\]
Here $\tauz$ is the semiring additive identity and multiplicative absorber.  The element $0_R\in R\subset G(R)$ remains a supported element.  Thus $\tauz$ and $0_R$ are distinct in $G(R)$.

There are two canonical maps:
\[
       p_R:G(R)\longrightarrow R,
       \qquad
       p_R(\tauz)=0_R,\\quad p_R(r)=r,
\]
called the arithmetic projection, and
\[
       \chi_R:G(R)\longrightarrow \BB,
       \qquad
       \chi_R(\tauz)=0,
       \quad \chi_R(r)=1\quad(r\in R),
\]
called the support character.  Here $\BB=\{0,1\}$ is the Boolean semifield.

The coordinate model is
\[
       D_R:=\{(0,0)\}\cup (R\times\{1\})\subset R\times\BB.
\]
The identification is
\[
       \tauz=(0,0),
       \qquad
       r=(r,1)\quad(r\in R).
\]
Thus
\[
       0_R=(0,1).
\]
The ambient points
\[
       (r,0),\qquad r\ne0,
\]
are deliberately absent.

\begin{proposition}[Zero taxonomy]
Inside the coordinate model $D_R$, there are three distinct zero-status phenomena:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\tauz=(0,0)$ is the unsupported global zero.
\item $0_R=(0,1)$ is the supported arithmetic zero.
\item $(r,0)$ with $r\ne0$ is an unsupported nonzero arithmetic state and is disallowed by the definition of $G(R)$.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover,
\[
       p_R^{-1}(0_R)=\{\tauz,0_R\},
\]
whereas
\[
       \chi_R^{-1}(0)=\{\tauz\},
       \qquad
       \chi_R^{-1}(1)=R.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The coordinate model gives $\tauz=(0,0)$ and $0_R=(0,1)$ directly.  The arithmetic projection is the first coordinate, while the support character is the second coordinate.  Therefore both $\tauz$ and $0_R$ project to $0_R$ under $p_R$, but only $\tauz$ has support coordinate $0$.  Since $D_R$ contains no point $(r,0)$ for $r\ne0$, unsupported nonzero arithmetic states are not elements of the split-zero semiring.  The displayed fiber equalities follow immediately.\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The distinction between $\tauz$ and $0_R$ is not a terminological refinement.  Later constructions use it to separate a zero coefficient, a missing support sector, and a trace-removed identity sector.  These are different operations in the split-zero model.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Prime ideals associated with the two zeros}

\begin{definition}
Define
\[
      \mathfrak s_R:=\{\tauz\},
      \qquad
      \mathfrak z_R:=\{\tauz,0_R\}.
\]
We call $\mathfrak s_R$ the support prime candidate and $\mathfrak z_R$ the supported arithmetic zero prime candidate.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The support prime]
For every nonzero commutative ring $R$, the subset
\[
       \mathfrak s_R=\{\tauz\}
\]
is a prime ideal of $G(R)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It is an ideal because $\tauz\oplus\tauz=\tauz$ and $x\tauz=\tauz x=\tauz$ for all $x\in G(R)$.  Suppose $xy\in\mathfrak s_R$.  Then $xy=\tauz$.  If neither $x$ nor $y$ is $\tauz$, then both lie in $R$, and their product in $G(R)$ is their ordinary product in $R$, hence belongs to $R$, not to $\{\tauz\}$.  Thus at least one of $x,y$ is $\tauz$, so one of $x,y$ lies in $\mathfrak s_R$.  Hence $\mathfrak s_R$ is prime.\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The supported arithmetic zero prime]
If $R$ is an integral domain, then
\[
       \mathfrak z_R=\{\tauz,0_R\}
\]
is a prime ideal of $G(R)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It is an ideal.  Indeed, it is closed under $\oplus$ because
\[
     \tauz\oplus\tauz=\tauz,
     \qquad
     \tauz\oplus 0_R=0_R,
     \qquad
     0_R\oplus0_R=0_R,
\]
and it is multiplicatively absorbing because
\[
     x\tauz=\tauz,
     \qquad
     r0_R=0_R\quad(r\in R).
\]
Now suppose $xy\in\mathfrak z_R$.  If one of $x,y$ is $\tauz$, then one factor is already in $\mathfrak z_R$.  Otherwise $x,y\in R$.  Then $xy\in\{0_R\}$ inside $R$.  Since $R$ is a domain, $xy=0_R$ implies $x=0_R$ or $y=0_R$.  Hence one factor lies in $\mathfrak z_R$.  Therefore $\mathfrak z_R$ is prime.\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
If $R$ is a domain, there is a strict chain of prime ideals
\[
        \{\tauz\}\subset \{\tauz,0_R\}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The inclusion is immediate, and it is strict because $0_R\ne\tauz$ in $G(R)$.\end{proof}

\section{The augmentation defect and the A-type kernel}

\subsection{The universal primitive defect object}

\begin{definition}[Primitive sector module]
Let $X$ be a finite set.  Define
\[
       D(X):=\ZZ[X]
\]
with standard basis $[x]$, $x\in X$.  The augmentation is
\[
       \epsilon_X:D(X)\to\ZZ,
       \qquad
       \epsilon_X\Big(\sum_{x\in X}a_x[x]\Big)=\sum_{x\in X}a_x.
\]
The primitive defect is
\[
       \delta_X:=-\epsilon_X.
\]
The cancellation lattice is
\[
       K(X):=\ker(\delta_X)=\ker(\epsilon_X).
\]
\end{definition}

Thus a primitive sector has defect
\[
       \delta_X([x])=-1,
\]
and the total primitive class
\[
       \one_X:=\sum_{x\in X}[x]
\]
has defect
\[
       \delta_X(\one_X)=-|X|.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Augmentation kernel]
If $|X|=r$, then $K(X)$ is canonically isomorphic, after choosing an ordering of $X$, to the $A_{r-1}$ root lattice.  For $r=1$, this says $K(X)=0=A_0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose an ordering $X=\{1,\ldots,r\}$ and write $e_i=[i]$.  Then
\[
       K(X)=\{(a_1,\ldots,a_r)\in\ZZ^r:a_1+\\cdots+a_r=0\}.
\]
For $1\le i\le r-1$, set
\[
       \alpha_i=e_i-e_{i+1}.
\]
Each $\alpha_i$ has coordinate sum zero.  Conversely, let $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_r)\in K(X)$ and put
\[
       b_i=a_1+\cdots+a_i.
\]
Then
\[
       \sum_{i=1}^{r-1}b_i(e_i-e_{i+1})=(a_1,\ldots,a_r),
\]
because the coefficient of $e_1$ is $b_1=a_1$, the coefficient of $e_j$ for $2\le j\le r-1$ is $-b_{j-1}+b_j=a_j$, and the coefficient of $e_r$ is $-b_{r-1}=a_r$ using $\sum_i a_i=0$.  Thus $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{r-1}$ form a basis of $K(X)$.

With the standard inner product on $\ZZ^r$,
\[
      \langle \alpha_i,\alpha_j\rangle
      =
      \begin{cases}
      2,&i=j,\\
      -1,&|i-j|=1,\\
      0,&|i-j|>1.
      \end{cases}
\]
This is the Cartan matrix of type $A_{r-1}$.\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The scalar ladder]
The primitive total defects satisfy
\[
       -1\leftrightarrow A_0,
       \qquad
       -2\leftrightarrow A_1,
       \qquad
       -3\leftrightarrow A_2,
       \qquad
       -r\leftrightarrow A_{r-1}.
\]
The $-1$ case has no nonzero root lattice but has a nonzero rank-one defect character $\ZZ\to\ZZ$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $r$ primitive sectors, the total class is $(1,\ldots,1)$ and its defect is $-r$.  The kernel is $A_{r-1}$ by the theorem.  For $r=1$ the kernel is zero.\end{proof}

\subsection{The dimension character interpretation}

The same map appears under several standard names.  For a vector bundle, it is rank.  For a finite group representation, it is dimension.  For a pointed fusion category, it is Frobenius-Perron dimension.  In all cases the project defect is the negative of that character.

\begin{definition}[Dimension defect]
Let $D$ be a free abelian group on primitive irreducible classes $e_i$, and suppose a dimension character
\[
      d:D\to\ZZ
\]
satisfies $d(e_i)=1$ for each primitive sector.  Define
\[
      \delta_D=-d.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Primitive defect as negative dimension]
In each of the following settings the primitive defect is the negative of the canonical rank or dimension character:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $K_0(C(Y))$ for connected compact $Y$, with $d=\operatorname{rk}$;
\item $R(G)$ for a finite group $G$, with $d=\dim$;
\item the Grothendieck group of a pointed fusion subcategory, with $d=\FPdim$;
\item the cyclic group ring $\ZZ[C_r]$, with $d$ the augmentation.
\end{enumerate}
In a rank-$r$ primitive dimension-one sector system, the kernel of the defect is $A_{r-1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $K_0(C(Y))$ with $Y$ connected, the rank of a vector bundle is locally constant, hence constant, and sends a line bundle or the trivial rank-one class to $1$.  For a representation ring $R(G)$, the ordinary dimension homomorphism sends a one-dimensional irreducible representation to $1$; in the general representation ring it sends an irreducible $\rho$ to $\dim\rho$.  For a pointed fusion category, every simple invertible object has Frobenius-Perron dimension $1$.  For $\ZZ[C_r]$ with basis $g^i$, the augmentation sends each $g^i$ to $1$.  Therefore in a primitive dimension-one sector system the map is exactly $\epsilon_r$, and the kernel is $A_{r-1}$ by the augmentation-kernel theorem.\end{proof}

\subsection{Rank-three source versus rank-three kernel}

Two rank conventions must be distinguished.  A source of rank $3$ means three primitive sectors:
\[
      \ZZ^3\xrightarrow{-\Sigma}\ZZ,
\]
whose kernel is $A_2$.  A kernel of rank $3$ means a source of rank $4$:
\[
      \ZZ^4\xrightarrow{-\Sigma}\ZZ,
\]
whose kernel is $A_3$.

\begin{proposition}[The first rank-three kernel coincidence]
The rank-three cancellation lattice is
\[
      \ker(\ZZ^4\xrightarrow{-\Sigma}\ZZ)=A_3.
\]
As a root lattice,
\[
      A_3\cong D_3.
\]
Consequently the corresponding compact Lie data may be read either as type $SU(4)$ or type $Spin(6)$, up to the usual finite-cover distinctions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first statement is the augmentation-kernel theorem with $r=4$.  The root systems $A_3$ and $D_3$ are isomorphic: both have rank $3$ and the $D_3$ Dynkin diagram is a three-node chain after removing the redundant notation normally used for $D_n$ with $n\ge4$.  Equivalently, the Lie algebras $\mathfrak{sl}_4(\CC)$ and $\mathfrak{so}_6(\CC)$ are isomorphic.  The compact simply connected groups $SU(4)$ and $Spin(6)$ are therefore isogenous and in fact isomorphic as simply connected compact real forms.\end{proof}

\section{Support linearization from the split-zero semiring}

Let $I$ be a finite set.  A free split-zero object $G(R)^I$ has coordinatewise support.  The support of an element is a subset of $I$.

\begin{definition}[Support linearization]
Define
\[
      \operatorname{Supp}:G(R)^I\to \mathcal P(I)
\]
by
\[
      \operatorname{Supp}((x_i)_{i\in I})=
      \{i\in I: x_i\ne\tauz\}.
\]
Define the linearization map
\[
      \mathcal L_I:\mathcal P(I)\to\ZZ^I,
      \qquad
      J\mapsto \sum_{j\in J}e_j.
\]
The support defect is
\[
      \Delta_I(J):=-|J|.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Split-zero support produces the augmentation defect]
For a finite support set $I$, the composite
\[
       \mathcal P(I)\xrightarrow{\mathcal L_I}\ZZ^I\xrightarrow{-\Sigma}\ZZ
\]
is the support defect
\[
       J\mapsto -|J|.
\]
The linearized defect-free support lattice is
\[
       \ker(-\Sigma)=A_{|I|-1}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $J\subseteq I$,
\[
     (-\Sigma)(\mathcal L_I(J))
       = -\Sigma\Big(\sum_{j\in J}e_j\Big)
       =-|J|.
\]
The kernel statement is exactly the augmentation-kernel theorem with $r=|I|$.\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This proves that the A-type defect tower is not imposed externally on the split-zero construction.  It is obtained by linearizing the Boolean support cube of a free split-zero semimodule.
\end{remark}

\section{Quotient coordinate, anti-invariant lift, and endpoint defects}

\subsection{The quadratic quotient}

Set
\[
       u=s(s-1),
       \qquad
       v=2s-1.
\]
Then
\[
       u(1-s)=u(s),
       \qquad
       v(1-s)=-v(s),
\]
and
\[
       v^2=1+4u.
\]
Thus $\CC(s)=\CC(u)(v)$ is a quadratic extension, with deck involution $s\mapsto1-s$ sending $v$ to $-v$.

\begin{lemma}[Endpoint principal part basis]
The two functions
\[
       \frac1u,
       \qquad
       \frac vu
\]
form a basis for the two-dimensional space of principal parts with at most simple poles at $s=0$ and $s=1$.  Explicitly,
\[
       \frac1u=\frac1{s-1}-\frac1s,
       \qquad
       \frac vu=\frac1{s-1}+\frac1s.
\]
If
\[
       P(s)=\frac a s+\frac b{s-1},
\]
then
\[
       P(s)=A\frac1u+B\frac vu
\]
with
\[
       A=\frac{b-a}{2},
       \qquad
       B=\frac{a+b}{2}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $u=s(s-1)$ and $v=2s-1$,
\[
      \frac1u=\frac1{s(s-1)}=\frac1{s-1}-\frac1s,
\]
and
\[
      \frac vu=\frac{2s-1}{s(s-1)}=\frac1s+\frac1{s-1}.
\]
Now
\[
      A\frac1u+B\frac vu
      =(-A+B)\frac1s+(A+B)\frac1{s-1}.
\]
Equating coefficients gives $a=-A+B$ and $b=A+B$, hence $A=(b-a)/2$ and $B=(a+b)/2$.\end{proof}

\subsection{The completed shifted family}

Let
\[
      F_t(s)=\zeta(s,1+t)
\]
be the Hurwitz shifted family, and define its completed form
\[
      \Lambda_t(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\left(\frac s2\right)F_t(s).
\]
The only facts required are
\[
      F_t(0)=\zeta(0,1+t)=-\frac12-t,
\]
and that $F_t$ has residue $1$ at $s=1$.

\begin{proposition}[Endpoint residues]
The residues of $\Lambda_t$ at $0$ and $1$ are
\[
       \Res_{s=0}\Lambda_t=-1-2t,
       \qquad
       \Res_{s=1}\Lambda_t=1.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At $s=0$,
\[
      \Gamma(s/2)=\frac{2}{s}+O(1),
      \qquad
      \pi^{-s/2}=1+O(s),
\]
so
\[
      \Res_{s=0}\Lambda_t=2F_t(0)=2\left(-\frac12-t\right)=-1-2t.
\]
At $s=1$, the gamma and $\pi$ factors are regular and
\[
      \pi^{-1/2}\Gamma(1/2)=1,
\]
while $F_t$ has residue $1$.  Hence $\Res_{s=1}\Lambda_t=1$.\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Invariant/anti-invariant decomposition]
The endpoint principal part of $\Lambda_t$ is
\[
       \operatorname{PP}_{\{0,1\}}(\Lambda_t)
       =(1+t)\frac1u-t\frac vu.
\]
Consequently the anti-invariant coefficient is $-t$, and at $t=1$ it is $-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The principal part has coefficient
\[
      a=-1-2t
\]
at $1/s$, and coefficient
\[
      b=1
\]
at $1/(s-1)$.  By the preceding basis formula,
\[
      A=\frac{b-a}{2}=\frac{1-(-1-2t)}2=1+t,
\]
and
\[
      B=\frac{a+b}{2}=\frac{-1-2t+1}{2}=-t.
\]
Thus
\[
      \operatorname{PP}_{\{0,1\}}(\Lambda_t)
      =(1+t)\frac1u-t\frac vu.
\]
The term $1/u$ is invariant under $s\mapsto1-s$, while $v/u$ is anti-invariant.  Hence the anti-invariant coefficient is $-t$.\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The doubled endpoint at $t=1$]
At $t=1$,
\[
      \operatorname{PP}_{\{0,1\}}(\Lambda_1)
      =2\frac1u-\frac vu.
\]
Moreover
\[
      -\frac vu=-\frac1s-\frac1{s-1}
\]
has residue $-1$ at each endpoint $0$ and $1$, and total finite endpoint residue $-2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Substitute $t=1$.  The displayed identity for $-v/u$ follows from the previous lemma.  The residues at $0$ and $1$ are both $-1$, so the total is $-2$.\end{proof}

\subsection{The logarithmic Jacobian}

\begin{proposition}[Logarithmic Jacobian]
One has
\[
      d\log u=\frac vu\,ds.
\]
Its residues on $\mathbb P^1$ are
\[
      \Res_{s=0}(d\log u)=1,
      \qquad
      \Res_{s=1}(d\log u)=1,
      \qquad
      \Res_{s=\infty}(d\log u)=-2.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $u=s(s-1)$,
\[
      du=(2s-1)ds=v\,ds.
\]
Thus
\[
      d\log u=\frac{du}{u}=\frac vu\,ds.
\]
The divisor of $u$ on $\mathbb P^1$ has simple zeros at $0$ and $1$ and a double pole at $\infty$.  The logarithmic derivative has residues equal to the orders of zeros and poles.  Hence the residues are $1$, $1$, and $-2$.\end{proof}

\subsection{Critical-line orthogonalization}

If
\[
      s=\frac12+i\gamma,
\]
then
\[
      u=s(s-1)=-\left(\gamma^2+\frac14\right)\in\RR,
      \qquad
      v=2i\gamma\in i\RR.
\]
Thus on the critical line the invariant term $1/u$ is real, and the anti-invariant term $v/u$ is purely imaginary.

\begin{proposition}[Critical-line separation]
On the critical line $\Re(s)=1/2$, the decomposition
\[
       A(u)\frac1u+B(u)\frac vu
\]
separates into a real invariant quotient component and a purely imaginary anti-invariant lift component whenever $A(u)$ and $B(u)$ are real-valued on the real $u$-ray.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On the critical line, $u\in\RR$ and $v\in i\RR$.  Thus $1/u\in\RR$ and $v/u\in i\RR$.  If $A(u),B(u)\in\RR$, the assertion follows.\end{proof}


\section{Stone endpoint algebra and coprime defect kernels}

The split-zero support distinction also has a finite Stone model.  This model records how a non-unit supported sector propagates through multiplication and how idempotence defects become scalar Euler-product obstructions.

Let
\[
       X=\{\xi_1,\xi_*\}
\]
with multiplication
\[
       \xi_1x=x\xi_1=x,
       \qquad
       \xi_*\xi_* = \xi_*.
\]
Let
\[
       u=1_{\xi_1},
       \qquad
       v=1_{\xi_*}
\]
be the characteristic functions.  Define the comultiplication on functions by
\[
       \Delta f(x,y)=f(xy).
\]

\begin{proposition}[Endpoint Boolean comultiplication]
One has
\[
       \Delta(u)=u\otimes u,
\]
and
\[
       \Delta(v)=v\otimes u+u\otimes v+v\otimes v.
\]
Thus
\[
       \Delta(v)-v\otimes v=v\otimes u+u\otimes v.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The product equals $\xi_1$ only when both inputs are $\xi_1$, so $\Delta(u)$ is $1$ exactly on $(\xi_1,\xi_1)$; this is $u\otimes u$.  The product equals $\xi_*$ unless both inputs are $\xi_1$, so $\Delta(v)$ has truth table $(0,1,1,1)$ on
\[
       (\xi_1,\xi_1), (\xi_1,\xi_*), (\xi_*,\xi_1), (\xi_*,\xi_*).
\]
The function $v\otimes u+u\otimes v+v\otimes v$ has the same truth table.  Subtracting $v\otimes v$ leaves the two mixed boundary entries.\end{proof}

For $\lambda\in\CC$, define
\[
       a_\lambda=u+\lambda v.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Idempotence defect]
The comultiplicative defect of $a_\lambda$ is
\[
       \Delta(a_\lambda)-a_\lambda\otimes a_\lambda
       = (\lambda-\lambda^2)v\otimes v.
\]
Thus the scalar idempotence defect is
\[
       \delta(\lambda)=\lambda-\lambda^2.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Using the preceding proposition,
\[
       \Delta(a_\lambda)=u\otimes u+\lambda(v\otimes u+u\otimes v+v\otimes v).
\]
Also
\[
       a_\lambda\otimes a_\lambda
       =u\otimes u+\lambda(v\otimes u+u\otimes v)+\lambda^2v\otimes v.
\]
Subtracting gives $(\lambda-\lambda^2)v\otimes v$.\end{proof}

Now define the Dirichlet series with coefficients
\[
       c_\lambda(1)=1,
       \qquad
       c_\lambda(n)=\lambda\quad(n\ge2),
\]
namely
\[
       Z_\lambda(s)=1+\lambda(\zeta(s)-1)=\lambda\zeta(s)+(1-\lambda).
\]
For coprime $m,n$, define
\[
       \Delta_\lambda(m,n)=c_\lambda(mn)-c_\lambda(m)c_\lambda(n).
\]

\begin{proposition}[Coprime coefficient defect]
For coprime $m,n$,
\[
       \Delta_\lambda(m,n)=0
\]
if at least one of $m,n$ equals $1$, and
\[
       \Delta_\lambda(m,n)=\lambda-\lambda^2
\]
if $m,n>1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $m=1$, then $c_\lambda(mn)=c_\lambda(n)=c_\lambda(1)c_\lambda(n)$ because $c_\lambda(1)=1$.  Similarly if $n=1$.  If $m,n>1$, then $mn>1$, so
\[
       c_\lambda(mn)-c_\lambda(m)c_\lambda(n)=\lambda-\lambda^2.
\]
\end{proof}

Define the interior coprime kernel
\[
       C(s,t)=\sum_{\substack{m,n>1\\(m,n)=1}}m^{-s}n^{-t}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Interior coprime kernel]
For $\Re(s),\Re(t)>1$ and by meromorphic continuation,
\[
       C(s,t)=\frac{\zeta(s)\zeta(t)}{\zeta(s+t)}-\zeta(s)-\zeta(t)+1.
\]
Consequently the two-variable defect Dirichlet series is
\[
       D_\lambda(s,t)=(\lambda-\lambda^2)C(s,t).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The standard coprime sum is
\[
       \sum_{(m,n)=1}m^{-s}n^{-t}=\frac{\zeta(s)\zeta(t)}{\zeta(s+t)}.
\]
Subtract the boundary contributions: $(m=1,n>1)$ contributes $\zeta(t)-1$, $(n=1,m>1)$ contributes $\zeta(s)-1$, and $(m,n)=(1,1)$ contributes $1$.  Hence
\[
       C(s,t)=\frac{\zeta(s)\zeta(t)}{\zeta(s+t)}-(\zeta(t)-1)-(\zeta(s)-1)-1,
\]
which is the displayed formula.  The defect formula follows from the preceding proposition.\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The regularized $-3$ from the doubled Stone defect]
At $\lambda=2$,
\[
       \lambda-\lambda^2=-2.
\]
Using $\zeta(0)=-1/2$, one has
\[
       C(0,0)=\frac32,
\]
and therefore
\[
       D_2(0,0)=-3.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
At $s=t=0$,
\[
       C(0,0)=\frac{\zeta(0)^2}{\zeta(0)}-2\zeta(0)+1=\zeta(0)-2\zeta(0)+1=1-\zeta(0)=\frac32.
\]
Multiplying by $-2$ gives $-3$.\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This $-3$ is not the primitive triplet defect.  It is the product of the doubled Stone idempotence defect $-2$ with the regularized interior coprime mass $3/2$.  It is nevertheless a genuine occurrence of the same integer in the support-refined zeta calculus.
\end{remark}

\section{Semilocal crossed products and boundary defects}

This section gives the local calculation that identifies the finite-prime boundary edge with an actual extension morphism in the visible semilocal crossed-product sequence.  The point is to distinguish the theorem-level local computation from a mere rank-counting consequence.

\subsection{The finite-prime local compactification}

Fix a rational prime $p$ and write
\[
        \ell_p=\log p.
\]
The local finite-prime edge is modeled by
\[
        Y_p=(\ZZ\cup\{\infty\})\times\RR,
\]
where $m\to+\infty$ in $\ZZ$ converges to $\infty$.  Let $T$ be the generator of a $\ZZ$-action given by
\[
        T(m,y)=(m+1,y+\ell_p),
        \qquad
        T(\infty,y)=(\infty,y+\ell_p).
\]
Define
\[
        E_p=C_0(Y_p)\rtimes_T\ZZ.
\]
The generic ideal is
\[
        I_p=C_0(\ZZ\times\RR)\rtimes_T\ZZ,
\]
and the boundary quotient is
\[
        Q_p=C_0(\{\infty\}\times\RR)\rtimes_T\ZZ.
\]
Thus
\[
        0\to I_p\to E_p\to Q_p\to0
\]
is a short exact sequence.

\begin{lemma}[Morita reductions]
There are strong Morita equivalences
\[
        I_p\sim_M C_0(\RR),
        \qquad
        Q_p\sim_M C(S^1).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On $\ZZ\times\RR$, the action is free and proper.  The invariant coordinate is
\[
        t=y-m\ell_p.
\]
Indeed,
\[
        (y+\ell_p)-(m+1)\ell_p=y-m\ell_p.
\]
The orbit space is therefore homeomorphic to $\RR$.  Green imprimitivity gives
\[
        I_p\sim_M C_0(\RR).
\]
On the boundary $\{\infty\}\times\RR$, the action is translation $y\mapsto y+\ell_p$.  The orbit space is
\[
        \RR/\ell_p\ZZ\cong S^1.
\]
Again Green imprimitivity gives
\[
        Q_p\sim_M C(S^1).
\]
\end{proof}

The quotient coordinate on the generic part is
\[
        t=y-m\ell_p.
\]
When $m\to+\infty$ with $y$ bounded, then $t\to-\infty$, and the limiting boundary coordinate is
\[
        \theta=y\pmod{\ell_p\ZZ}=t\pmod{\ell_p\ZZ}.
\]
Thus the boundary circle is attached at the negative end of the generic logarithmic line with phase remembered modulo $\ell_p$.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-prime boundary map]
Under the Morita identifications
\[
        K_0(Q_p)\cong K_0(C(S^1))\cong\ZZ[1],
        \qquad
        K_1(I_p)\cong K_1(C_0(\RR))\cong\ZZ,
\]
where the positive generator of $K_1(C_0(\RR))$ is represented by counterclockwise winding, the connecting homomorphism
\[
        \partial_p:K_0(Q_p)\to K_1(I_p)
\]
satisfies
\[
        \partial_p([1])=-1.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose a continuous function
\[
        \phi:\RR\to[0,1]
\]
such that
\[
        \lim_{t\to-\infty}\phi(t)=1,
        \qquad
        \lim_{t\to+\infty}\phi(t)=0.
\]
For example, $\phi(t)=(1+e^t)^{-1}$.  On the generic part define
\[
        h(m,y)=\phi(y-m\ell_p)=\phi(t),
\]
and on the boundary define
\[
        h(\infty,y)=1.
\]
This is continuous in the compactification because $m\to+\infty$ with $y$ bounded implies $t=y-m\ell_p\to-\infty$, so $\phi(t)\to1$.  The image of $h$ in $Q_p$ is the constant projection $1$.  Hence $h$ is a self-adjoint lift of $[1]\in K_0(Q_p)$.

The connecting map sends $[1]$ to the class of
\[
        u(t)=\exp(2\pi i\phi(t))
\]
in the unitization of $C_0(\RR)$.  As $t$ moves from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, $\phi(t)$ decreases from $1$ to $0$.  Thus $u(t)$ traverses the unit circle once clockwise.  With counterclockwise winding as $+1$, this class is $-1$.\end{proof}

\subsection{Identification with the semilocal finite boundary edge}

Let $S$ be a finite set of rational primes together with $\infty$.  For a finite prime $p\in S$, the $p$-adic coordinate modulo $\ZZ_p^\times$ is its valuation.  Nonzero finite coordinate gives $m\in\ZZ$; the zero coordinate is the limit $m\to+\infty$.  The real coordinate is logarithmic $y=\log |x_\infty|$.

Multiplication by $p$ sends
\[
      (m,y)\mapsto(m+1,y+\log p)
\]
and sends the boundary valuation $\infty$ to itself while translating $y$ by $\log p$.  The remaining finite-prime valuation directions and sign directions act freely and properly and contribute compact-operator Morita factors.

\begin{theorem}[Actual finite edge]
The intermediate semilocal extension that attaches the finite-prime stratum $Z_p$ to the generic stratum $Z_\varnothing$ is Morita equivalent to
\[
        0\to I_p\to E_p\to Q_p\to0.
\]
Consequently the component of the global connecting map on $K_0(A_p)$ sends the rank-one boundary class to $-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
After dividing the finite $p$-adic coordinate by $\ZZ_p^\times$, the coordinate is the valuation compactification $\ZZ\cup\{\infty\}$.  The real logarithmic coordinate is $y$.  Multiplication by $p$ gives exactly the transformation $T$ used above.  The other valuation coordinates and sign coordinate form a free proper transformation group on the local edge; Green imprimitivity replaces them by compact-operator Morita factors.  Hence the semilocal edge extension is Morita equivalent to the model extension.  Naturality of the six-term exact sequence under Morita equivalence identifies the connecting maps.  The preceding theorem computes the model map as $[1]\mapsto -1$.\end{proof}

\subsection{The archimedean local edge}

The archimedean local model is
\[
        C=C_0(\RR)\rtimes\Ztwo,
\]
where the generator acts by $x\mapsto -x$.  Evaluation at the fixed point $0$ gives
\[
        0\to J\to C\to Q\to0,
\]
where
\[
        J=C_0(\RR\setminus\{0\})\rtimes\Ztwo,
        \qquad
        Q=C^*(\Ztwo).
\]
Let $U$ denote the nontrivial group element.  The minimal projections of $Q$ are
\[
        p_+=\frac{1+U}{2},
        \qquad
        p_-=\frac{1-U}{2}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Archimedean boundary map]
Under the standard Morita identification
\[
        K_1(J)\cong K_1(C_0(\RR))\cong\ZZ,
\]
with counterclockwise winding positive, the connecting map satisfies
\[
        \partial_\infty([p_+])=-1,
        \qquad
        \partial_\infty([p_-])=-1.
\]
Consequently
\[
        \partial_\infty([p_+]+[p_-])=-2,
        \qquad
        \partial_\infty([p_+]-[p_-])=0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose an even continuous function $\phi:\RR\to[0,1]$ with $\phi(0)=1$ and $\phi(x)\to0$ as $|x|\to\infty$.  The self-adjoint lifts
\[
        h_+=\phi p_+,
        \qquad
        h_-=\phi p_-
\]
lift $p_+$ and $p_-$.  In the half-line matrix representation of the crossed product, the projections are
\[
        P_+=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
        \qquad
        P_-=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Then
\[
        \exp(2\pi i h_+)=P_-+e^{2\pi i\phi(x)}P_+,
\]
so the determinant is $e^{2\pi i\phi(x)}$.  As $x$ moves from $0$ to $+\infty$, $\phi(x)$ decreases from $1$ to $0$, so the determinant winds once clockwise.  Hence $[p_+]\mapsto -1$.  The same determinant calculation with $P_-$ gives $[p_-]\mapsto -1$.  Additivity gives the remaining statements.\end{proof}

\subsection{Global visible semilocal formula}

Let
\[
       S=\{p_1,\ldots,p_n,\infty\}.
\]
Let $A_S$ denote the visible semilocal crossed product of the generic stratum and all one-zero strata, after quotient by the finite local unit groups.  Let
\[
       B_S=\bigoplus_{i=1}^n A_{p_i}\oplus A_\infty
\]
be the boundary quotient.  The $K_0$-group of $B_S$ has primitive basis
\[
       [1]_{p_1},\ldots,[1]_{p_n},[p_+],[p_-].
\]

\begin{theorem}[Corrected global boundary row]
With the orientation convention used above, the connecting homomorphism
\[
       \delta_0:K_0(B_S)\to K_1(A_\varnothing)\cong\ZZ
\]
is
\[
       \delta_0(k_1,\ldots,k_n,n_+,n_-)
       =-\big(k_1+\cdots+k_n+n_++n_-\big).
\]
Consequently
\[
       K_0(A_S)\cong\ZZ^{n+1},
       \qquad
       K_1(A_S)\cong\ZZ^n.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The finite local edge computation gives
\[
       [1]_{p_i}\mapsto -1
\]
for every finite prime $p_i$.  The archimedean computation gives
\[
       [p_+]\mapsto -1,
       \qquad
       [p_-]\mapsto -1.
\]
The connecting map is additive, hence the displayed row formula.

The six-term exact sequence has the form
\[
       0\to K_0(A_S)\to K_0(B_S)\xrightarrow{\delta_0}K_1(A_\varnothing)	o K_1(A_S)\to K_1(B_S)\to0,
\]
because $K_0(A_\varnothing)=0$.  Here
\[
       K_0(B_S)\cong\ZZ^{n+2},
       \qquad
       K_1(B_S)\cong\ZZ^n,
       \qquad
       K_1(A_\varnothing)\cong\ZZ.
\]
The row vector defining $\delta_0$ is primitive, hence $\delta_0$ is surjective.  Therefore
\[
       K_0(A_S)=\ker\delta_0\cong\ZZ^{n+1}.
\]
Surjectivity also makes the map $K_1(A_\varnothing)\to K_1(A_S)$ zero, so
\[
       K_1(A_S)\cong K_1(B_S)\cong\ZZ^n.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The semilocal triplet]
For $S=\{p,\infty\}$,
\[
       \delta_0([1]_p+[p_+]+[p_-])=-3,
\]
and
\[
       \ker\big(\ZZ^3\xrightarrow{-(1,1,1)}\ZZ\big)\cong A_2.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Substitute $n=1$ and the class $(1,1,1)$ into the global boundary row.  The kernel is the $r=3$ augmentation kernel, hence $A_2$.\end{proof}

\section{Cyclic augmentation, Eisenstein lattices, and Epstein transforms}

\subsection{The cyclic simple-current line}

Let
\[
        C_r=\langle g:g^r=1\rangle.
\]
The group ring $\ZZ[C_r]$ has basis $1,g,\ldots,g^{r-1}$.  Its augmentation is
\[
        \epsilon:\ZZ[C_r]\to\ZZ,
        \qquad
        \epsilon\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a_jg^j\Big)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a_j.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Cyclic augmentation ideal]
The augmentation ideal
\[
        I_{C_r}:=\ker\epsilon
\]
is the $A_{r-1}$ root lattice.  The regular class has defect
\[
        -\epsilon(1+g+\cdots+g^{r-1})=-r.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Identify $g^j$ with the standard basis vector $e_j$ of $\ZZ^r$.  Then $\epsilon$ is coordinate summation.  Its kernel is therefore $A_{r-1}$ by the augmentation-kernel theorem.  The regular class has $r$ basis summands, so its negative augmentation is $-r$.\end{proof}

\subsection{The case $r=3$ and Eisenstein integers}

Let
\[
        \omega=e^{2\pi i/3},
        \qquad
        \OO=\ZZ[\omega].
\]
There is a quotient map
\[
        \ZZ[C_3]\to\ZZ[\omega],
        \qquad
        g\mapsto\omega.
\]
Since
\[
        1+\omega+\omega^2=0,
\]
the quotient is
\[
        \ZZ[C_3]/(1+g+g^2)\cong\ZZ[\omega].
\]
The augmentation ideal
\[
        I_{C_3}=\ker\epsilon
\]
is generated by
\[
        1-g,
        \qquad
        g-g^2,
\]
and maps to the Eisenstein ideal $(1-\omega)\ZZ[\omega]$.

\begin{proposition}[The Eisenstein $A_2$ plane]
The lattice $I_{C_3}$ with the coefficient inner product is $A_2$.  Under the Eisenstein realization, its quadratic form is similar to
\[
        m^2-mn+n^2=N(m+n\omega).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the basis
\[
        \alpha_1=1-g,
        \qquad
        \alpha_2=g-g^2,
\]
the coefficient inner products are
\[
        \langle \alpha_1,\alpha_1\rangle=2,
        \quad
        \langle \alpha_2,\alpha_2\rangle=2,
        \quad
        \langle \alpha_1,\alpha_2\rangle=-1.
\]
This is the $A_2$ Gram matrix.  The norm in $\ZZ[\omega]$ is
\[
        N(m+n\omega)=(m+n\omega)(m+n\bar\omega)=m^2-mn+n^2.
\]
Thus the Eisenstein norm gives the same hexagonal quadratic form up to the conventional factor $2$ used in root-lattice normalization.\end{proof}

\subsection{The $A_2$ Epstein zeta function}

Normalize
\[
        Q_{A_2}(m,n)=2(m^2-mn+n^2).
\]
Define
\[
        Z_{A_2}(s)=\sum_{(m,n)\ne(0,0)}Q_{A_2}(m,n)^{-s}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Eisenstein factorization]
For $\Re(s)>1$,
\[
        Z_{A_2}(s)=2^{-s}6\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3}).
\]
The identity extends by meromorphic continuation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since
\[
        Q_{A_2}(m,n)=2N(m+n\omega),
\]
one has
\[
        Z_{A_2}(s)=2^{-s}\sum_{0\ne z\in\ZZ[\omega]}N(z)^{-s}.
\]
The unit group of $\ZZ[\omega]$ has order $6$.  The ring $\ZZ[\omega]$ has class number one.  Therefore each nonzero ideal is principal and has exactly six generators differing by units.  Hence
\[
        \sum_{0\ne z\in\ZZ[\omega]}N(z)^{-s}
        =6\sum_{\mathfrak a\ne0}N(\mathfrak a)^{-s}
        =6\zeta_{\QQ(\sqrt{-3})}(s).
\]
For the quadratic field $\QQ(\sqrt{-3})$,
\[
        \zeta_{\QQ(\sqrt{-3})}(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3}).
\]
Combining gives the formula.\end{proof}

\section{Orbifold irreducibles, simple-current lines, and Weyl doubles}

\subsection{Irreducible stabilizer labels}

Let $G$ be a finite group.  In a holomorphic orbifold theory, the standard irreducible labels are conjugacy classes together with irreducible representations of centralizers.  Algebraically define
\[
        \operatorname{OrbIrr}(G)=
        \{(C,\rho): C\in\operatorname{Cl}(G),\ \rho\in\Irr(Z_G(g_C))\},
\]
where $g_C\in C$ is a chosen representative.

\begin{definition}[Orbifold defect module]
Define
\[
        D_G=\ZZ[\operatorname{OrbIrr}(G)].
\]
The primitive-count defect is
\[
        \delta_G^{\rm prim}\Big(\sum a_{C,\rho}[C,\rho]\Big)
        =-\sum a_{C,\rho}.
\]
The quantum-dimension defect is
\[
        \delta_G^{\rm qdim}\Big(\sum a_{C,\rho}[C,\rho]\Big)
        =-\sum a_{C,\rho}|C|\dim\rho.
\]
\end{definition}

If $G$ is abelian, every conjugacy class is a singleton and every centralizer is $G$, so
\[
        \operatorname{OrbIrr}(G)=G\times\widehat G.
\]

\subsection{The nonabelian $S_3$ check}

Let
\[
        S_3=\langle r,s:r^3=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle.
\]
The conjugacy classes are
\[
        C_1=\{1\},
        \qquad
        C_s=\{s,sr,sr^2\},
        \qquad
        C_r=\{r,r^2\}.
\]
The centralizers are
\[
        Z_{S_3}(1)=S_3,
        \qquad
        Z_{S_3}(s)=\langle s\rangle\cong C_2,
        \qquad
        Z_{S_3}(r)=\langle r\rangle\cong C_3.
\]
Thus
\[
        \Irr(S_3)=\{\mathbf1,\operatorname{sgn},\operatorname{std}\},
\]
with dimensions $1,1,2$;
\[
        \Irr(C_2)=\{+,-\},
\]
and
\[
        \Irr(C_3)=\{\chi_0,\chi_1,\chi_2\}.
\]

\begin{proposition}[$S_3$ orbifold dimensions]
The $S_3$ orbifold label set has $8$ elements.  Its quantum-dimension vector is
\[
        (1,1,2,3,3,2,2,2).
\]
The squared dimensions sum to
\[
        36=|S_3|^2.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The label count is
\[
        3+2+3=8.
\]
The quantum dimension attached to $(C,\rho)$ is $|C|\dim\rho$.  For $C_1$ this gives $1,1,2$.  For the transposition class $C_s$ of size $3$, the two $C_2$ irreducibles have dimension $1$, giving $3,3$.  For the three-cycle class $C_r$ of size $2$, the three $C_3$ irreducibles have dimension $1$, giving $2,2,2$.  The square sum is
\[
        1+1+4+9+9+4+4+4=36.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Simple-current triples}

Let a cyclic simple-current line be generated by an invertible object $J$ of order $r$.  Then its Grothendieck group is $\ZZ[C_r]$ with basis
\[
        \one,J,J^2,\ldots,J^{r-1}.
\]
Each simple current has Frobenius-Perron dimension $1$, so the defect is negative augmentation.

For $r=3$, a simple-current triple
\[
        W_0,
        \quad W_3,
        \quad W_6
\]
with fusion
\[
        W_3\boxtimes W_3=W_6,
        \qquad
        W_3\boxtimes W_6=W_0
\]
therefore has
\[
        \ZZ\{W_0,W_3,W_6\}\cong\ZZ[C_3]
\]
and
\[
        \ker\epsilon=A_2,
        \qquad
        -\epsilon(W_0+W_3+W_6)=-3.
\]


\subsection{Fourier $S$-matrix and the cyclic augmentation row}

For the cyclic pointed line $C_r$, the Fourier matrix
\[
       S_{ab}=\frac1{\sqrt r}\omega^{ab},
       \qquad
       a,b\in\ZZ/r\ZZ,
\]
diagonalizes the fusion matrices.  If $N_g$ denotes fusion by the generator $g$, then
\[
       N_g S_{\bullet b}=\omega^b S_{\bullet b}.
\]
The augmentation is the projection onto the trivial Fourier row $b=0$.

\begin{proposition}[Augmentation as the trivial Fourier coefficient]
For $x=\sum_a x_a g^a\in\CC[C_r]$,
\[
       \epsilon(x)=\sqrt r\sum_a x_a S_{a0}.
\]
Hence
\[
       \ker\epsilon\otimes\CC=\bigoplus_{b=1}^{r-1}\CC\,S_{\bullet b}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $S_{a0}=1/\sqrt r$ for all $a$,
\[
       \sqrt r\sum_a x_aS_{a0}=\sum_ax_a=\epsilon(x).
\]
The Fourier basis diagonalizes $\CC[C_r]$ and decomposes it into the trivial character line $b=0$ and the nontrivial character lines $b=1,\ldots,r-1$.  The augmentation vanishes exactly on the sum of nontrivial character lines.\end{proof}

For $r=3$, this says
\[
       I_{C_3}\otimes\CC=\CC_\omega\oplus\CC_{\omega^2},
\]
the complexified $A_2$ plane.

\subsection{Cyclic orbifold doubles and Weyl operators}

Let $C_r=\ZZ/r\ZZ$ and let $\widehat{C_r}$ be its character group.  Put
\[
       \omega=e^{2\pi i/r}.
\]
Let $V=\CC^r$ with basis $|m\rangle$, $m\in C_r$.  Define
\[
       X|m\rangle=|m+1\rangle,
       \qquad
       Z|m\rangle=\omega^m|m\rangle.
\]
Then
\[
       ZX=\omega XZ.
\]
For $(a,b)\in C_r\times\widehat{C_r}$ define
\[
       W_{a,b}=X^aZ^b.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Weyl-operator realization]
The operators $W_{a,b}$ form a basis of $M_r(\CC)$ and satisfy
\[
       W_{a,b}W_{c,d}=\omega^{bc}W_{a+c,b+d},
\]
so
\[
       [W_{a,b},W_{c,d}]
       =(\omega^{bc}-\omega^{ad})W_{a+c,b+d}.
\]
Moreover $W_{0,0}=I$, and
\[
       \Span_\CC\{W_{a,b}:(a,b)\ne(0,0)\}=\mathfrak{sl}_r(\CC).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The commutation relation $ZX=\omega XZ$ gives
\[
       Z^bX^c=\omega^{bc}X^cZ^b.
\]
Thus
\[
       W_{a,b}W_{c,d}=X^aZ^bX^cZ^d=\omega^{bc}X^{a+c}Z^{b+d}=\omega^{bc}W_{a+c,b+d}.
\]
Interchanging $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ gives the commutator formula.

The $r^2$ operators are linearly independent: if $a\ne0$, $X^aZ^b$ shifts basis vectors by $a$, and different shifts occupy different matrix diagonals; for $a=0$, the powers $Z^b$ are linearly independent diagonal matrices by the discrete Fourier transform.  Since $\dim M_r(\CC)=r^2$, they form a basis.

The trace of $W_{a,b}$ is zero unless $(a,b)=(0,0)$.  If $a\ne0$, the operator has no diagonal entries.  If $a=0$ but $b\ne0$, then
\[
       \tr(Z^b)=\sum_{m\in C_r}\omega^{bm}=0.
\]
Thus the nonidentity Weyl operators form a basis of the traceless matrices, i.e. $\mathfrak{sl}_r(\CC)$.\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Fusion versus commutator]
The fusion product of a pointed cyclic category is commutative after passing to the group ring.  The Lie algebra above is not the fusion algebra.  It is obtained only after choosing the Heisenberg cocycle
\[
       \alpha((a,b),(c,d))=\omega^{bc}
\]
and taking commutators in the twisted group algebra.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Where the split-zero point enters matrix realizations}

In the matrix algebra $M_r(\CC)$, the zero matrix is a supported arithmetic zero.  It is not the unsupported split-zero point.  The correct globalized matrix semiring is
\[
       G(M_r(\CC))=M_r(\CC)\sqcup\{\tauz\}.
\]
Then
\[
       p(\tauz)=0,
       \qquad
       p(A)=A,
\]
and
\[
       \chi(\tauz)=0,
       \qquad
       \chi(A)=1\quad(A\in M_r(\CC)).
\]
Thus a zero coefficient in a Weyl expansion is not the same as an absent support sector.  Removing $W_{0,0}=I$ to form $\mathfrak{sl}_r$ is the trace-zero projection; it is not the same operation as replacing a sector by $\tauz$.

\section{CUE derivative moments and Lie functors}

\subsection{Weyl denominator and $A_{N-1}$}

Let $U\in U(N)$ have eigenvalues $z_1,\ldots,z_N$.  The CUE Weyl density is
\[
       \prod_{1\le i<j\le N}(1-z_i/z_j)(1-z_j/z_i).
\]

\begin{proposition}[CUE Weyl product]
The CUE Weyl product is the $A_{N-1}$ root product
\[
       \prod_{\alpha\in A_{N-1}^+}(1-z^\alpha)(1-z^{-\alpha}).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The character lattice of the diagonal torus is $\ZZ^N$ with $z^{e_i}=z_i$.  The roots of $SU(N)$ are $e_i-e_j$ for $i\ne j$.  For $i<j$,
\[
       z^{e_i-e_j}=z_i/z_j.
\]
Thus the positive-root product is exactly the displayed CUE Weyl factor.\end{proof}

\subsection{The exact first derivative moment}

Let
\[
       \Phi_N(z)=\det(zI-U).
\]
Define
\[
       M_N(k)=\mathbb E_{U(N)}|\Phi_N'(1)|^{2k}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Exact first moment]
For every $N\ge1$,
\[
       M_N(1)=\sum_{m=1}^N m^2=\frac{N(N+1)(2N+1)}6.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write
\[
       \Phi_N(z)=\sum_{j=0}^N(-1)^j e_j(U)z^{N-j},
\]
where $e_j$ is the $j$-th elementary symmetric character, i.e. the character of $\wedge^j\CC^N$.  Then
\[
       \Phi_N'(1)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(-1)^j(N-j)e_j(U).
\]
The irreducible characters $e_j$ are orthonormal under Haar integration on $U(N)$, so
\[
       M_N(1)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(N-j)^2=\sum_{m=1}^N m^2.
\]
The final closed form is the standard sum of squares formula.\end{proof}

For $N=3$,
\[
       M_3(1)=1^2+2^2+3^2=14.
\]
Also
\[
       \Phi_3'(1)=3-2e_1+e_2.
\]

\subsection{First-derivative moment exponents and $\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$}

For first derivative $s$-fold moments, the CUE exponent is
\[
       s^2+2s.
\]
This admits a functorial Lie-algebra construction.

Let $W=\CC^s$.  Define
\[
       \mathfrak g_s=\End(W)\oplus W\oplus W^\vee.
\]
Map $(A,u,\varphi)$ to the block matrix
\[
       \begin{pmatrix}
       A & u\\
       \varphi & -\tr(A)
       \end{pmatrix}
       \in\mathfrak{sl}(W\oplus\CC).
\]

\begin{theorem}[CUE exponent functor]
The block map is a vector-space isomorphism
\[
       \End(W)\oplus W\oplus W^\vee
       \cong
       \mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}(\CC).
\]
Consequently
\[
       \dim\End(W)+\dim W+\dim W^\vee=s^2+s+s=s^2+2s=\dim\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every element of $\mathfrak{sl}(W\oplus\CC)$ has a block form
\[
       \begin{pmatrix}B&u\\ \varphi&\lambda\end{pmatrix}
\]
with $\tr(B)+\lambda=0$.  Hence $\lambda=-\tr(B)$ and the matrix is uniquely determined by $(B,u,\varphi)\in\End(W)\oplus W\oplus W^\vee$.  This gives the vector-space isomorphism.  Dimension is immediate.\end{proof}

This matches the CUE decomposition
\[
       s^2 \leftrightarrow \ell(\mu)\ell(\nu),
       \qquad
       s\leftrightarrow |\mu|,
       \qquad
       s\leftrightarrow |\nu|,
\]
for $\mu=\nu=(1^s)$.

\subsection{Quotient/lift obstruction in the $N=3$ compact Mellin peel}

At $N=3$, the compact Mellin peeling model \cite{compactmellin} introduces quotient variables
\[
       u=|\lambda|^2,
       \qquad
       v=|c|^2,
\]
and a phase-cover coordinate $Z$ satisfying a quadratic trace relation
\[
       Z^2-R(u,v)Z+P(u,v)=0.
\]
The integer kernels are finite sums of Hermitian squares on the phase cover, but the descended quotient polynomial $\Psi_2^\#(u,v)$ is not a sum of squares over $\RR[u,v]$.

\begin{proposition}[The $N=3$ quotient obstruction]
Assume the descended quadratic kernel is
\[
\Psi_2^\#(u,v)=
\frac{47}{24}+\frac{11}{4}u+\frac{28}{3}v-\frac{11}{8}u^2+4uv+\frac{10}{3}v^2.
\]
Then $\Psi_2^\#$ is not a sum of squares in $\RR[u,v]$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A quadratic polynomial in $u,v$ is a sum of squares only if it has a positive semidefinite Gram matrix with respect to the affine basis $(1,u,v)$.  Here the symmetric Gram matrix is forced to be
\[
       Q=
       \begin{pmatrix}
       47/24&11/8&14/3\\
       11/8&-11/8&2\\
       14/3&2&10/3
       \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its principal minor
\[
       \det\begin{pmatrix}47/24&11/8\\11/8&-11/8\end{pmatrix}
       =-\frac{55}{12}<0.
\]
Hence $Q$ is not positive semidefinite, so no real sum-of-squares representation exists.\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This proves a quotient/lift obstruction: positivity is visible on the phase cover as a Hermitian-square decomposition but is not represented by a real sum of squares after quotienting.  This is structurally parallel to split-zero support descent, but an explicit functor is needed before identifying the two obstructions as the same object.
\end{remark}

\section{Nielsen geometry, Weyl bases, and Epstein penalties}

The Nielsen geometry of quantum computation \cite{nielsen} places a right-invariant metric on a unitary group by penalizing chosen Hamiltonian directions.  The construction below is a precise way to attach an Epstein zeta function to a Weyl-basis penalty spectrum.

\begin{construction}[Weyl-penalty spectral zeta]
Let $L$ be a positive definite lattice with quadratic form $Q$.  Suppose a right-invariant metric on a unitary group has an orthogonal Hamiltonian basis indexed by a finite or countable subset $\mathcal I\subset L\setminus\{0\}$, and suppose the penalty operator is diagonal with eigenvalue $Q(\ell)$ on the basis vector indexed by $\ell$.  Define the penalty spectral zeta function by
\[
       \zeta_{\rm pen}(s)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal I}Q(\ell)^{-s}.
\]
If $\mathcal I=L\setminus\{0\}$, then
\[
       \zeta_{\rm pen}(s)=Z_L(s).
\]
\end{construction}

For $L=A_2$ with $Q=Q_{A_2}$, this gives
\[
       \zeta_{\rm pen}(s)=Z_{A_2}(s)=2^{-s}6\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3})
\]
when all nonzero $A_2$ modes are included.

\begin{remark}
For a finite-dimensional $SU(3)$ Weyl-operator basis there are only eight nonidentity operators.  To obtain the full Epstein zeta one must pass to a lattice-mode or covering construction.  Thus the Epstein zeta is not the spectral zeta of the finite eight-dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ basis without additional lifting.
\end{remark}

\section{Rank dictionaries: F-theory, Mordell-Weil rank, and BSD}

The constructions above have a precise rank-theoretic compatibility with elliptic-fibration physics and arithmetic geometry.  The statement must be kept at the correct level.

For an elliptic fibration \cite{vafa,arithmeticgeometry}
\[
       \pi:X\to B
\]
with section, the Mordell-Weil group $\MW(\pi)$ is the group of rational sections.  In F-theory compactifications, the free rank of $\MW(\pi)$ contributes abelian gauge factors $U(1)^{\rk \MW}$.  Reducible fibers contribute ADE root lattices; in particular an $A_{r-1}$ fiber singularity yields $SU(r)$-type nonabelian enhancement.

\begin{proposition}[Shioda-Tate rank form]
For an elliptic surface with section, one has the rank identity
\[
       \rho(X)=2+\sum_v \rk \Phi_v+\rk \MW(\pi),
\]
where $\Phi_v$ is the root lattice generated by the non-identity components of the reducible fiber at $v$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The trivial lattice is generated by the zero section, the fiber class, and the non-identity components of reducible fibers.  Its rank is
\[
       2+
       \sum_v \rk\Phi_v.
\]
Modulo this trivial lattice, section classes are identified with the Mordell-Weil group modulo torsion by the Shioda map.  Taking ranks yields the displayed identity.\end{proof}

BSD enters through a different but compatible rank statement.  For an elliptic curve $E/\QQ$, the BSD rank conjecture asserts
\[
       \rk E(\QQ)=\operatorname{ord}_{s=1}L(E,s).
\]
This is a theorem in the analytic-rank $0$ and $1$ cases over $\QQ$ by the Gross-Zagier/Kolyvagin circle of results.  Thus the valid bridge is
\[
       \text{Mordell-Weil rank}
       \quad\leftrightarrow\quad
       U(1)\text{ rank in elliptic-fibration geometry},
\]
and, in arithmetic settings,
\[
       \text{Mordell-Weil rank}
       \quad\leftrightarrow\quad
       \text{analytic rank by BSD, conjecturally or in known cases}.
\]
No direct equality between an arbitrary F-theory compactification and a BSD theorem is asserted without specifying an arithmetic elliptic fibration.

\section{Moonshine and Niemeier compatibility}

The cyclic triplet appears naturally in $\ZZ_3$ orbifold constructions of lattice VOAs, especially Miyamoto's order-three orbifold construction \cite{miyamoto}.  If a simple-current triple
\[
       W_0,
       \quad W_3,
       \quad W_6
\]
satisfies
\[
       W_3\boxtimes W_3=W_6,
       \qquad
       W_3\boxtimes W_6=W_0,
\]
then the Grothendieck subgroup it spans is $\ZZ[C_3]$.  Hence the augmentation defect gives
\[
       -\epsilon(W_0+W_3+W_6)=-3,
\]
and the kernel is $A_2$.

The Eisenstein-Leech side supplies the corresponding lattice globalization \cite{eisensteinleech,umbral,linear-codes}: an Eisenstein plane is the $A_2$ local root geometry; twelve such planes form the $12A_2$ Niemeier root system; the Leech lattice is obtained by a $3$-neighbor construction from the $12A_2$ Niemeier lattice.  Thus the chain
\[
       \ZZ[C_3]\to A_2\to 12A_2\to \Lambda_{\rm Leech}
\]
is structurally compatible with the $\ZZ_3$ simple-current triple and with the order-three local cubic coordinate at the hexagonal point.

This does not assert that the augmentation defect alone proves moonshine.  It states the precise algebraic substructure shared by the cyclic orbifold triple, the Eisenstein lattice, the Niemeier $12A_2$ system, and the order-three local modular descent.

\section{Synthesis and non-assertions}

The stable theorem package is the following.

\begin{theorem}[Unified primitive defect theorem]
There is a common primitive defect mechanism behind the split-zero support theory, semilocal boundary maps, cyclic simple-current lines, Weyl-operator doubles, CUE A-type structures, and elliptic-fibration rank data.  It is the negative dimension/rank/augmentation character.

For $r$ primitive dimension-one sectors,
\[
       D_r=\ZZ^r,
       \qquad
       \delta_r(a_1,\ldots,a_r)=-(a_1+\cdots+a_r),
\]
and
\[
       \ker\delta_r=A_{r-1}.
\]
The cases are
\[
       r=1:\\quad -1,
       \quad A_0=0,
       \quad U(1)\text{ primitive charge},
\]
\[
       r=2:
       \quad -2,
       \quad A_1,
       \quad SU(2)\text{ root system},
\]
\[
       r=3:
       \quad -3,
       \quad A_2,
       \quad SU(3),
       \quad C_3,
       \quad \ZZ[\omega],
\]
\[
       r=4:
       \quad -4,
       \quad A_3\cong D_3,
       \quad SU(4)\sim Spin(6).
\]
The split-zero semiring realizes this ladder by support linearization; the semilocal model realizes the primitive finite and archimedean boundary defects; the cyclic simple-current line realizes it by group-ring augmentation; the Eisenstein $A_2$ transform realizes the triplet by an Epstein zeta function; and the CUE first-derivative exponent realizes the full Lie-algebra dimension by the block decomposition of $\mathfrak{sl}_{s+1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The split-zero support statement is Theorem 3.2.  The semilocal finite and archimedean boundary maps are Theorems 5.2 and 5.4.  The cyclic augmentation statement is Theorem 6.1.  The Eisenstein and Epstein statements are Proposition 6.2 and Theorem 6.3.  The CUE block construction is Theorem 8.3.  The identifications of $A_{r-1}$ with the root lattice of $SU(r)$ and $A_3\cong D_3$ are standard root-system computations already proved in Sections 3 and 4.\end{proof}

The following statements are deliberately not made.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item The fusion algebra of an orbifold is not identified with $\mathfrak{sl}_r$.  The Lie algebra arises only after adding a Heisenberg cocycle and taking commutators in the Weyl-operator representation.
\item The CUE $N=3$ quotient/SOS obstruction is not asserted to be literally the $A_2$ lattice.  It is a quotient/lift obstruction compatible with the same support-loss pattern; an explicit functor is required for equality.
\item The Epstein zeta transform is not asserted to be the same transform as the compact CUE Mellin transform.  They are both Mellin transforms of measures, one discrete and one compact, and are connected only after specifying a limiting or functorial bridge.
\item The F-theory/Mordell-Weil/BSD relation is rank-theoretic unless a specific arithmetic elliptic fibration is fixed.
\item No result here proves RH.  The quotient and anti-invariant decomposition of the completed shifted family is a structural descent statement, not a proof of the location of zeros.
\end{enumerate}
