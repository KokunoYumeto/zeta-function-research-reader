\section{Both original spectral poles, with coefficient bounds uniform in the cutoff}
\label{sec:real-pair-uniform-coefficients}

The functions in this section are the actual functions of RLA1--10,
on the neighborhood constructed there from the certified point
RPZ1--19 \cite{RPZ005}.  Their complete sources accompany this
derivation.  The four labels $\mathrm M,\mathrm N,\mathrm T,\mathrm E$
and primary exponents in RLA1 are unchanged.  In particular we retain
the original $p=(z,x)$, $u=1/z$, unit
$a=\alpha_{\rm unit}(1+ix)$, spectral coordinate $t$, and
$\omega(t)=-i\arctan t$ throughout.  The exact maps
$p=\mathcal P_{\rm roots}(\alpha,\beta)$ in RLA9 recover both
original parameters whenever root coordinates are used below.
This continuation concerns that same geometric family; it makes no
identification with an arithmetic zeta quartet.

The complete factorization proved in RLA5--10 is
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 g(t,p)=e^{-4\omega(t)}\prod_{j=1}^4 f_j(\omega(t),p)
       =U(t,p)(t-\alpha(p))(t-\beta(p)),\qquad F(t,p)=U(t,p)^{-1},\\
 U=e^{-4\omega}U_+U_-,\qquad U(0,p_*)=-b,\quad
 b=|G_\xi(0,p_*)|^2>0,\qquad G=f_1f_2,\\
 U_+(t,p)=\int_0^1\partial_t[G(\omega(\cdot),p)]
        (\alpha+\theta(t-\alpha))\,d\theta,
 \end{gathered}\tag{RUC1}
\]
and $U_-$ is the same integral for $G^\#(\omega(\cdot),p)$ at
the root $\beta$.  The original center exponential and all unit
coefficients remain in $U$.  The integral divisions and the
existence of the two roots are proved in RLA5--6 by contraction
and integration of the complete analytic functions.

\subsection{A single parameter neighborhood and its actual contour constants}

There is a positive dyadic $R<1/2$ such that, for
$r_0=R/4$, the full functions and the inverse root map are
holomorphic on a neighborhood of
\[
 \{|t|\le2R,\ |\alpha|\le r_0,\ |\beta|\le r_0\},\qquad
 |U(t,\mathcal P_{\rm roots}(\alpha,\beta))+b|\le b/2.
 \tag{RUC2}
\]
To verify existence, RLA9 first gives an open neighborhood of the
origin in the two root coordinates.  Composing its holomorphic
inverse with RLA6 gives a holomorphic function of $(t,\alpha,\beta)$
equal to $-b$ at the origin.  Continuity supplies a polydisc on
which the displayed difference is strictly less than $b/2$.
Every sufficiently small dyadic $R$ has its displayed closed
polydisc inside that open set.  Fix any one such $R$ once and
for all.  This is a proved choice of a neighborhood of the actual
functions; it is not an extra nonvanishing hypothesis on freely
assigned coefficients.

Let $\mathcal K=\mathcal P_{\rm roots}
(\{|\alpha|,|\beta|\le r_0\})$.  It is a compact neighborhood of
the point in its original parameter space.  Define the actual
contour maximum and the following completely specified constants:
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 M=\max_{p\in\mathcal K,\ |\zeta|=R}|F(\zeta,p)|\le2/b,
 \qquad m=2/(3b),\qquad |F(t,p)|\ge m\quad(|t|\le2R),\\
 C_0=\frac{MR}{R-r_0},\qquad C_1=\frac{MR}{(R-r_0)^2},\qquad
 C=\frac{M}{(R-r_0)^2},\qquad A=C_0+2r_0C_1.
 \end{gathered}\tag{RUC3}
\]
The inequalities for $F$ follow directly from
$b/2\le|U|\le3b/2$.  No parameter or cutoff dependence is hidden
in $M$: it is the maximum of the stated actual function on the
specified fixed compact contour family.  The constants $C_0,C_1$
bound respectively $|F(a,p)|$ and $|\partial_tF(a,p)|$ for
$|a|\le r_0$.  Indeed Cauchy's integral formula and its derivative
give denominators $\zeta-a$ and $(\zeta-a)^2$, respectively;
the contour length is $2\pi R$ and their moduli are at least
$R-r_0$ and $(R-r_0)^2$.

\subsection{Exact division with both residues and a uniform analytic remainder}

For $\alpha\ne\beta$ define the degree-one interpolation polynomial
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 L(t,p)&=\frac{F(\alpha,p)(t-\beta)-F(\beta,p)(t-\alpha)}
                     {\alpha-\beta}=\ell_0+\ell_1t,\\
 \ell_1&=\frac{F(\alpha,p)-F(\beta,p)}{\alpha-\beta},\qquad
 \ell_0=F(\alpha,p)-\alpha\ell_1.
 \end{aligned}\tag{RUC4}
\]
At $\alpha=\beta=a$ use exactly
$L(t,p)=F(a,p)+\partial_tF(a,p)(t-a)$.
All these functions extend holomorphically across the diagonal:
the divided difference in RUC4 equals
$\int_0^1\partial_tF(\beta+\theta(\alpha-\beta),p)\,d\theta$.
Every segment lies in $|t|\le r_0$.
Thus $|\ell_1|\le C_1$ and
$|\ell_0|\le C_0+r_0C_1$, uniformly on $\mathcal K$.

The exact decomposition, on the entire neighborhood, is
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \frac1{g(t,p)}&=\frac{L(t,p)}{(t-\alpha)(t-\beta)}+H(t,p),\\
 H(t,p)&=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}
 \frac{F(\zeta,p)}{(\zeta-\alpha)(\zeta-\beta)(\zeta-t)}\,d\zeta
                    \quad(|t|<R).
 \end{aligned}\tag{RUC5}
\]
The equality of meromorphic functions includes parameters where a
root is zero.  To prove it first take $t,\alpha,\beta$ distinct.
Partial fractions in $\zeta$ and Cauchy's integral formula evaluate
the integral as
\[
 \frac{F(t,p)}{(t-\alpha)(t-\beta)}
 +\frac{F(\alpha,p)}{(\alpha-t)(\alpha-\beta)}
 +\frac{F(\beta,p)}{(\beta-t)(\beta-\alpha)}.
\]
Rearranging gives RUC5 with the polynomial RUC4.  The contour
denominators stay nonzero on $|\zeta|=R$, so the integral is
holomorphic in $t,\alpha,\beta$ and the parameters, including
their coincidences.  The divided-difference integral gives the
same continuation of $L$.  Taking limits therefore proves RUC5
also at coincidences, without dividing by a vanishing separation.
Equivalently the integral is the actual quotient
$(F-L)/((t-\alpha)(t-\beta))$ at its removable points.

The principal part itself is, exactly,
\[
 \begin{cases}
 \displaystyle
 \frac{F(\alpha,p)}{\alpha-\beta}\frac1{t-\alpha}
 +\frac{F(\beta,p)}{\beta-\alpha}\frac1{t-\beta},&\alpha\ne\beta,\\[6pt]
 \displaystyle \frac{F(a,p)}{(t-a)^2}
              +\frac{\partial_tF(a,p)}{t-a},&\alpha=\beta=a.
 \end{cases}\tag{RUC6}
\]
The double-pole term cannot lose its nonzero coefficient, because
$F(a,p)\ne0$.  Its simple-pole coefficient is the actual derivative
of the full unit reciprocal, not a coefficient that may be omitted.

Write $H(t,p)=\sum_{n\ge0}r_n(p)t^n$.
Expanding $1/(\zeta-t)$ in its uniformly convergent geometric
series on any $|t|\le R'<R$, and integrating each term, proves
\[
 r_n(p)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}
     \frac{F(\zeta,p)}{(\zeta-\alpha)(\zeta-\beta)\zeta^{n+1}}\,d\zeta,
 \qquad |r_n(p)|\le C R^{-n}\quad(n\ge0,\ p\in\mathcal K).
 \tag{RUC7}
\]
This estimate has one fixed radius and one fixed constant for
every coefficient and every parameter in the neighborhood.
It is stronger in its quantifiers than a remainder for one fixed
coefficient as the parameter tends to the base point.

\subsection{Every original inverse coefficient, including root collision}

For the old fixed domain the inverse exists exactly off
$\alpha\beta=0$.  At such a parameter let
$h_n(p)=[t^n]g(t,p)^{-1}$.  The complete formula is
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 h_n(p)&=P_n(p)+r_n(p),\\
 P_n(p)&=\begin{cases}
 \displaystyle\frac{F(\beta,p)\beta^{-n-1}
                   -F(\alpha,p)\alpha^{-n-1}}{\alpha-\beta},
                         &\alpha\ne\beta,\\[6pt]
 (n+1)F(a,p)a^{-n-2}-\partial_tF(a,p)a^{-n-1},
                         &\alpha=\beta=a\ne0.
 \end{cases}
 \end{aligned}\tag{RUC8}
\]
Geometric expansion of each simple pole in RUC6 proves the first
case.  Differentiation of $(t-a)^{-1}$ with respect to $a$ gives
the coefficient $(n+1)a^{-n-2}$ of the double pole, proving the
second, including both signs.  The same second expression is the
negative derivative of $F(a,p)a^{-n-1}$, which proves directly
that it is the diagonal limit of the first expression.

Let $r=\min\{|\alpha|,|\beta|\}>0$.  Without any division by
the separation of the roots, the finite geometric-product formula
gives the uniform coefficient bound
\[
 |P_n(p)|\le A(n+1)r^{-n-2},\qquad
 |h_n(p)|\le A(n+1)r^{-n-2}+CR^{-n}.
 \tag{RUC9}
\]
For its proof the coefficient of $t^n$ in $q^{-1}$, where
$q=(t-\alpha)(t-\beta)$, is
$\sum_{j=0}^n\alpha^{-j-1}\beta^{-(n-j)-1}$.
Its modulus is at most $(n+1)r^{-n-2}$, and the coefficient of
$t^{n-1}$ is at most $nr^{-n-1}$, with zero value at $n=0$.
Multiply by $\ell_0+\ell_1t$ and use RUC4's bounds, $r\le r_0$,
and the definition of $A$.  This proof works unchanged at equal
roots and retains the full unit in the interpolation coefficients.

The following exact adjacent identities are valid for every $n\ge1$:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \alpha P_n-P_{n-1}&=F(\beta,p)\beta^{-n-1},\\
 \beta P_n-P_{n-1}&=F(\alpha,p)\alpha^{-n-1}.
 \end{aligned}\tag{RUC10}
\]
They follow by substituting the first formula in RUC8 and
cancelling its two terms.  Substitution of the second formula
gives the same identities at equal roots, with the unit derivative
terms cancelling exactly.  Thus no two adjacent principal
coefficients vanish at a parameter where the old inverse exists.
For the exact coefficients, RUC7 gives, for example,
\[
 |\alpha h_n-h_{n-1}-F(\beta,p)\beta^{-n-1}|
       \le C(|\alpha|+R)R^{-n}.
 \tag{RUC11}
\]
The other identity has the corresponding $\beta$ in this bound.
Select the one whose right-hand root has modulus $r$.
Its left-hand linear combination has modulus at most
$(1+r_0)\max\{|h_n|,|h_{n-1}|\}$.
It follows, uniformly throughout the punctured neighborhood, that
\[
 \max\{|h_n|,|h_{n-1}|\}
 \ge\frac{m r^{-n-1}-C(r_0+R)R^{-n}}{1+r_0}.
 \tag{RUC12}
\]
In particular define the explicit integer
\[
 n_1=\max\left\{1,
  \left\lceil\frac{\log^+(2C(r_0+R)r_0/m)}{\log(R/r_0)}\right\rceil
              \right\},\qquad \log^+y=\max\{0,\log y\}.
\]
For all $n\ge n_1$ the error term in RUC12 is at most half of
its first term, because its relative bound is
$C(r_0+R)r_0(r_0/R)^n/m$.  Consequently
\[
 \max\{|h_n(p)|,|h_{n-1}(p)|\}
       \ge\frac{m}{2(1+r_0)}r^{-n-1}\quad(n\ge n_1).
 \tag{RUC13}
\]
There is no exclusion of small root separation or of phase
cancellation in this estimate.

\subsection{The exact real phase cancellations and their limits}

For a real parameter, RLA10 proves
$\beta=-\bar\alpha$ and $F(-\bar t,p)=\overline{F(t,p)}$.
If the roots are distinct and nonzero, write their actual values
$\alpha=r e^{i\phi}$ and $F(\alpha,p)=|F(\alpha,p)|e^{i\psi}$;
here $\cos\phi\ne0$, and $\beta=-r e^{-i\phi}$.
Then RUC8 becomes the exact parity formula
\[
 P_n=\begin{cases}
 \displaystyle-\frac{|F(\alpha,p)|}{r^{n+2}\cos\phi}
          \cos(\psi-(n+1)\phi),&n\text{ even},\\[5pt]
 \displaystyle-\frac{i|F(\alpha,p)|}{r^{n+2}\cos\phi}
          \sin(\psi-(n+1)\phi),&n\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}\tag{RUC14}
\]
Both the numerator amplitude and phase are those of the full
actual $F$.  Thus the exact criterion for cancellation of the
two root contributions is
\[
 P_n=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \left(\frac\alpha\beta\right)^{n+1}
       =\frac{F(\alpha,p)}{\overline{F(\alpha,p)}}.
 \tag{RUC15}
\]
This follows also directly from equating the two terms in RUC8.
If $\alpha/\beta$ has infinite multiplicative order, at most one
nonnegative integer $n$ can satisfy it: two solutions would give
a nonzero integer power of this ratio equal to one.  If its order
is the integer $q\ge2$, either there is no solution or the
solutions are exactly one residue class for $n+1$ modulo $q$.
Indeed multiplication by a fixed solution reduces the equation
to $(\alpha/\beta)^{n-n_0}=1$.  The ratio cannot have order one
in the distinct-root case.  These assertions classify actual
phase cancellation; they do not assert that an independently
prescribed value of the unit phase is realized.

If the two real-slice roots coincide, $\alpha=\beta=a\ne0$ is
purely imaginary.  Reflection gives $F(a,p)\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$
and $\partial_tF(a,p)\in i\mathbb R$.  Formula RUC8 gives
\[
 P_n=F(a,p)a^{-n-2}
           \left(n+1-\frac{a\partial_tF(a,p)}{F(a,p)}\right).
 \tag{RUC16}
\]
The real constant in the last quotient can equal $n+1$ for at
most one nonnegative integer.  Hence the double-pole case also
has at most one cancelled principal coefficient.
Whenever a principal coefficient cancels, the complete coefficient
equals the analytic remainder and satisfies $|h_n|\le CR^{-n}$.
It need not be zero.  Conversely RUC13 proves that such
cancellation cannot persist in two adjacent full coefficients
at the exponential scale of the actual smaller root.  This is a
proved limitation on cancellation, not an assumption of generic phase.

\subsection{A second-order determinant identity and its explicit error}

For every $n\ge0$ and every parameter off the zero branches,
\[
 P_nP_{n+2}-P_{n+1}^2
       =-\frac{F(\alpha,p)F(\beta,p)}{(\alpha\beta)^{n+3}}.
 \tag{RUC17}
\]
For distinct roots write
$P_n=A_\alpha\alpha^{-n}+A_\beta\beta^{-n}$, where
$A_\alpha=-F(\alpha,p)/(\alpha(\alpha-\beta))$ and
$A_\beta=F(\beta,p)/(\beta(\alpha-\beta))$.
Expanding the determinant cancels both pure square terms and
leaves
$A_\alpha A_\beta(\alpha\beta)^{-n}(\alpha^{-1}-\beta^{-1})^2$,
which is the displayed expression.  The diagonal limit exists
by RUC8, giving the same identity at equal nonzero roots; direct
substitution of its affine factor in $n$ gives it as well.

Let $\mathcal E_n$ denote
\[
 \mathcal E_n=C\bigl(|P_n|R^{-n-2}
          +2|P_{n+1}|R^{-n-1}+|P_{n+2}|R^{-n}\bigr)
                    +2C^2R^{-2n-2}.
 \tag{RUC18}
\]
Expansion of $h_j=P_j+r_j$ and the triangle inequality prove
\[
 \left|h_nh_{n+2}-h_{n+1}^2
       +\frac{F(\alpha,p)F(\beta,p)}{(\alpha\beta)^{n+3}}\right|
       \le\mathcal E_n.
 \tag{RUC19}
\]
This is valid for all $n$ and every such complex parameter,
including collision of the two nonzero roots.  The stated
error uses actual $P_j$, so no root separation factor has been
discarded or hidden.

On the real slice both root moduli equal $r$.  Put
$q_0=r_0/R=1/4$, $a_0=\log(R/r_0)>0$ and
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 Q&=\frac{4CA r_0^2}{m^2}\left(3+\frac2{a_0\exp(1)}\right)
             +\frac{2C^2r_0^4q_0^2}{m^2},\\
 n_2&=\max\left\{0,
              \left\lceil\frac{2\log^+(2Q)}{a_0}\right\rceil\right\}.
 \end{aligned}\tag{RUC20}
\]
RUC9 bounds the error relative to the modulus of the principal
determinant by
\[
 \frac{\mathcal E_n}{|F(\alpha,p)|^2r^{-2n-6}}
 \le\frac{4CA r_0^2}{m^2}(n+3)q_0^n
                  +\frac{2C^2r_0^4}{m^2}q_0^{2n+2}
 \le Q e^{-a_0n/2}.
\]
For the first inequality, the three mixed errors have respective
factors $(n+1)r^2(r/R)^{n+2}$,
$2(n+2)r^2(r/R)^{n+1}$ and $(n+3)r^2(r/R)^n$ after division;
the quadratic error has factor $2C^2r^4(r/R)^{2n+2}$.
Replace $r$ by its upper bound $r_0$ and use $|F|\ge m$.
For the second inequality,
$(n+3)e^{-a_0n/2}\le3+2/(a_0\exp(1))$ follows by maximizing
$x e^{-a_0x/2}$ on $x\ge0$; the other exponential decreases
at least as fast.  The definition of $n_2$ now proves
\[
 |h_nh_{n+2}-h_{n+1}^2|
       \ge\tfrac12|F(\alpha,p)|^2r^{-2n-6}
       \ge\tfrac12m^2r^{-2n-6}\quad(n\ge n_2).
 \tag{RUC21}
\]
The same $n_2$ works for every nonzero real parameter in the
fixed neighborhood, even as it approaches either root collision
or the certified point.  It is an integer independent of the cutoff.

\subsection{The finite weighted consequences, with all original constants}

For $D\ge0$, let $\rho_j=\sqrt{j!/(s/2)_j}$ with
$s\in\{1,a_{\rm amp}\}$ and $a_{\rm amp}=4\ell+1\ge9$.
The original mass $(2\pi)^{s/2}$ and centers
$a_{\rm amp}/2$, $a_{\rm amp}/2-4$ are those of WCF1--6
\cite{WCF}; no measure is changed.  In its orthonormal frames,
the old inverse is exactly
$B_{D,s}^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(\rho)^{-1}T_D(h)
\operatorname{diag}(\rho)$.
An upper diagonal of gap $n$ has norm
\[
 w^+_{D,n,s}=\max_{0\le i\le D-n}\frac{\rho_{i+n}}{\rho_i}
 =\begin{cases}\rho_n,&s=1,\\
                 \rho_D/\rho_{D-n},&s=a_{\rm amp},
   \end{cases}\qquad 0\le n\le D.
 \tag{RUC22}
\]
Its nonzero columns have distinct orthogonal images, so its norm
is the maximum displayed ratio.  Squaring the ratio gives the
product of $(i+j+1)/(i+j+s/2)$ for $0\le j<n$.
Each factor decreases with $i$ at $s=1$ and increases with $i$
at $s=a_{\rm amp}$, proving the endpoint formulas without a
replacement of the weights.

Define
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal B_{D,s}(p)&=\sum_{n=0}^D w^+_{D,n,s}
           \{A(n+1)r^{-n-2}+CR^{-n}\},\\
 \mathcal R_{D,s}&=C\sum_{n=0}^D w^+_{D,n,s}R^{-n},\qquad
 \underline\rho_{D,s}=\min\{\rho_D,\rho_{D-1}\}\quad(D\ge1).
 \end{aligned}\tag{RUC23}
\]
The triangle inequality for the exact weighted upper diagonals
and RUC9 prove $\|B_{D,s}^{-1}\|\le\mathcal B_{D,s}(p)$ and
that the analytic-remainder matrix has norm at most
$\mathcal R_{D,s}$, for every $D$.  The entries in row zero and
columns $D,D-1$, together with RUC13, give the uniform lower bound
\[
 \|B_{D,s}^{-1}\|
 \ge\frac{m\underline\rho_{D,s}}{2(1+r_0)\rho_0}
          r^{-D-1}\quad(D\ge n_1).
 \tag{RUC24}
\]
Consequently, for each fixed nonzero parameter and fixed original
$s$, phase cancellation cannot remove exponential growth with
the cutoff: in fact
$\lim_{D\to\infty}\|B_{D,s}^{-1}\|^{1/D}=r^{-1}>1$.
For completeness the subexponential nature of the fixed-$s$
weights follows directly from
$\log\rho_D=-\frac12\sum_{j=1}^D\log(1+(s/2-1)/j)$.
After finitely many terms, the absolute logarithm of each factor
is at most $2|s/2-1|/j$, by integrating $1/(1+t)$ between zero
and that number.  Hence $|\log\rho_D|=O_s(1+\log D)$.
The finite sums in RUC23 then have the same $D$th-root upper
rate $r^{-1}$, since $r<R<1$ and their remaining factors are
polynomial in $D$ for fixed $s$.  The lower bound gives the
matching rate.  If $s$ changes with $D$, RUC22--24 remain the
exact bounds; no subexponential assertion about that change is
inferred from this fixed-$s$ argument.

On the real slice and for $D\ge n_2+2$, the minor in rows $0,1$
and columns $D-1,D$ of the exact inverse has determinant
\[
 \frac{\rho_{D-1}\rho_D}{\rho_0\rho_1}
                (h_{D-1}^2-h_Dh_{D-2}).
\]
The exterior norm dominates the absolute value of each matrix
entry in orthonormal exterior frames.  RUC21 therefore proves
\[
 \|\bigwedge^2B_{D,s}^{-1}\|
 \ge\frac{m^2\rho_{D-1}\rho_D}{2\rho_0\rho_1}
                  r^{-2D-2}.
 \tag{RUC25}
\]
For fixed $s$, RUC23 and the inequality
$\|\bigwedge^2B^{-1}\|\le\|B^{-1}\|^2$ give the exact
$D$th-root rate $r^{-2}$ on this real slice, including every
phase-cancelling subsequence and the double-root case.
For every exterior rank $1\le k\le D+1$, the complete finite
upper bound $\|\bigwedge^k B^{-1}\|\le\mathcal B_{D,s}^k$
also follows.  At the top rank its exact value is
$|g_0(p)|^{-D-1}$, because the diagonal of $B$ is $g_0$.
The complementary exterior identity
$\|\bigwedge^kB^{-1}\|=
\|\bigwedge^{D+1-k}B\|/|g_0|^{D+1}$
retains every original forward coefficient.  These finite
formulas do not assert an all-rank asymptotic obtained by
discarding the analytic remainder.

\subsection{The full-matrix pole terms and their lower-triangular correction}

Formula RUC8 for $P_n$ extends to every integer $n$ when the roots
are nonzero.  The full matrix
$(P_{j-i}\rho_j/\rho_i)_{0\le i,j\le D}$ has rank at most two.
For distinct roots it is exactly the sum of the two outer products
\[
 A_\alpha\bigl(\alpha^i/\rho_i\bigr)_i
                  \bigl(\rho_j\alpha^{-j}\bigr)_j^{\mathsf T}
 +A_\beta\bigl(\beta^i/\rho_i\bigr)_i
                  \bigl(\rho_j\beta^{-j}\bigr)_j^{\mathsf T},
 \tag{RUC26}
\]
with the actual amplitudes in the proof of RUC17.
At equal roots the expression RUC8 is affine in $j-i$ times
$a^{i-j}$, so its columns lie in the span of
$(a^i/\rho_i)_i$ and $(ia^i/\rho_i)_i$; this proves rank at most
two without taking an unbounded separated-pole representation.
The rank assertion does not apply directly to its upper-triangular
part.  The difference is a required lower-triangular correction,
whose complete entries at gap $l=i-j\ge1$ are $-P_{-l}\rho_j/\rho_i$.
They have the exact coefficient formula and uniform bound
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 P_{-l}&=-\int_0^1
   \left.\partial_z\{F(z,p)z^{l-1}\}\right|_{
                  z=\beta+\theta(\alpha-\beta)}\,d\theta,\\
 |P_{-l}|&\le C_1r_0^{l-1}+(l-1)C_0r_0^{l-2}.
 \end{aligned}\tag{RUC27}
\]
For $l=1$ the second term is defined to be zero.
The first formula is precisely the negative divided difference
of $F(z,p)z^{l-1}$, and includes its derivative at equal roots.
The segment bound follows by differentiation and RUC3; the
polynomial makes this integral regular even if the segment passes
through zero.  The exact norm of the lower shift weight is
$\max_{0\le j\le D-l}\rho_j/\rho_{j+l}$, equal to
$\rho_{D-l}/\rho_D$ at $s=1$ and $\rho_0/\rho_l$ at
$s=a_{\rm amp}$, by the same product argument as RUC22.
Thus its full finite correction norm is at most the sum of
these weights times the right side of RUC27 for $1\le l\le D$.
Together with $\mathcal R_{D,s}$ this is an exact rank-two-plus-
remainder decomposition of the weighted inverse, retaining every
lower shift.  It supplies finite constants for further exterior
estimates rather than replacing an upper-triangular pole matrix
by a rank-two matrix without its correction.
To record its full exterior consequence define
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 w^-_{D,l,s}&=\max_{0\le j\le D-l}\frac{\rho_j}{\rho_{j+l}},\\
 \mathcal E_{D,s}^{\rm mat}
 &=\mathcal R_{D,s}+
   \sum_{l=1}^D w^-_{D,l,s}
        \{C_1r_0^{l-1}+(l-1)C_0r_0^{l-2}\}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
As before the second summand inside braces is zero at $l=1$.
For every $1\le k\le D+1$, at every off-branch parameter in the
fixed neighborhood, one has the complete finite bound
\[
 \|\bigwedge^k B_{D,s}^{-1}\|
 \le \mathcal B_{D,s}(p)^{\min(k,2)}
      \min\{\mathcal B_{D,s}(p),\mathcal E_{D,s}^{\rm mat}\}
                                      ^{\max(k-2,0)}.
 \tag{RUC28}
\]
Indeed the rank-two full pole matrix has a kernel of codimension
at most two.  On that kernel the full inverse equals the remainder,
whose norm is at most $\mathcal E_{D,s}^{\rm mat}$.  Diagonalizing
the positive matrix of the inverse squared norm proves that its
third and subsequent singular values are at most this bound: if
three were larger, their three-dimensional singular-vector space
would intersect that kernel nontrivially, contradicting the norm
bound there.  Every singular value is also at most
$\mathcal B_{D,s}(p)$ by RUC23.  Multiplying the largest $k$
singular values proves RUC28, including dimensions one and two.
The radius is fixed and smaller than one, so the analytic part
$\mathcal R_{D,s}$ is not claimed bounded as $D$ tends to infinity.

\subsection{The actual order-one and order-two domains}

At a point of either single zero branch, let $c\ne0$ denote its
remaining root; it satisfies $|c|\le r_0$.  The actual order is
one and the reciprocal of the actual quotient symbol is
$t/g=F(t,p)/(t-c)$.  On their intersection the actual order is
two and $t^2/g=F(t,p)$.  Their complete coefficients are
\[
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 v&[t^n](t^v/g)&\text{uniform analytic coefficient error}\\\hline
 1&-F(c,p)c^{-n-1}+s_n(p)&
 |s_n(p)|\le M(R-r_0)^{-1}R^{-n}\\[2pt]
 2&[t^n]F(t,p)&|[t^n]F(t,p)|\le MR^{-n}.
 \end{array}\tag{RUC29}
\]
For $v=1$, divide $F(t,p)-F(c,p)$ by $t-c$ using
$\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}
F(\zeta,p)/((\zeta-c)(\zeta-t))\,d\zeta$.
The two-point partial fraction identity and Cauchy's formula
prove the division, and coefficient extraction gives the stated
bound.  The $v=2$ case is Cauchy's coefficient integral itself.
The original weighted inverse on these actual strata has entries
\[
 (\mathcal A_{D,s,p}^{-1})_{ij}
     =[t^{j-i}](t^v/g)\,\frac{\rho_j}{\rho_{i+v}}\quad(j\ge i),
 \tag{RUC30}
\]
with zero entries otherwise, by multiplying the weighted reciprocal
from WCF6 and RLA30.  Its source and target remain the original
$\mathcal P_{D+v}/\mathcal P_{v-1}$ and $\mathcal P_D$, with
the unchanged Gamma norms.  Summing the exact diagonal weight
maxima against RUC29 gives finite norm and exterior bounds on
these domains as well.  The old inverse has no Taylor coefficient
at zero-root parameters and has not been extended there.  All
strata are connected by the original maps and the complete unit,
not by replacing their domains or leading moments.
