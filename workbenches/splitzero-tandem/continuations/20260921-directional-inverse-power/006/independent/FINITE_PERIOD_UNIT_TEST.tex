\section{A complete real-unit test at an actual finite original period}
The objects here are the original PCL1--10 period coefficients,
the OFM1--11 factors and moments, and the original unit of PZ1--3
\cite{PCLOriginal,NC,Cumulative,PeriodZeroOriginal}.
Keep $0<\delta<1/2$, $\gamma>2$, $z=1/u\in\mathbb R$,
$\beta=2(\gamma^2-\delta^2)$, $\eta=(\delta^2+\gamma^2)^2$,
and $k=2\delta\gamma$. Thus
$\eta-\beta^2/4=k^2>0$. The actual unit is
$a=\alpha+i\chi\ne0$, with $\alpha,\chi\in\mathbb R$.
Its value is not assigned in this calculation. The tests below can be
evaluated at that value; elimination over the real projective phase
describes all possible phase values without identifying any of them
with the actual unit.

Retain the original root multiplication matrix $T$ and quadratic matrix
$Q$ of OPB7. Define
\[
 K=\frac{T^2+\beta I/2}{k},\qquad
 K^2=-I,\qquad
 W_a=\alpha I+\chi K,\qquad
 W_a^{-1}=\frac{\alpha I-\chi K}{\alpha^2+\chi^2}.
 \tag{FUT1}
\]
The matrix $K$ evaluates to $i,-i,-i,i$ at the original ordered roots.
Consequently $VW_a=UV$ with precisely
$U=\operatorname{diag}(a,\bar a,\bar a,a)$.
The identity $T^4+\beta T^2+\eta I=0$ gives $K^2=-I$;
multiplication then proves the displayed full inverse.

For the actual real matrix $R=\mathcal R(z)$, define its four exact
Fourier projectors and their quadratic phase columns by
\[
 \begin{split}
 E_b(z)&=R^{-1}e_be_b^{\mathsf T}R,\qquad 1\le b\le4,\\
 h_{b,0}&=E_be_0,\quad
 h_{b,1}=E_bKe_0-KE_be_0,\quad
 h_{b,2}=-KE_bKe_0.
 \end{split}\tag{FUT2}
\]
Here $e_b$ in the projector denotes the $b$th Fourier basis vector;
$e_0=(1,0,0,0)^{\mathsf T}$ denotes the original constant-polynomial
coefficient. All entries of $R$ are the entire PCL2 series, and
$R^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}R$ since $\det R=1$.
For each residue $\ell=0,1,2,3,4$ and $r=0,1,2,3,4$ set
\[
 C_{\ell r}(z)=
 \sum_{\substack{1\le b,d\le4\\b+d\equiv\ell\ (5)}}
 \ \sum_{\substack{0\le v,w\le2\\v+w=r}}
       h_{b,v}^{\mathsf T}Qh_{d,w},\qquad
 A_i(\alpha,\chi;z)=
 \sum_{r=0}^4(C_{ir}-C_{4r})\alpha^{4-r}\chi^r,
 \quad 0\le i\le3.
 \tag{FUT3}
\]
These are finite homogeneous real quartics whose coefficients are
explicit expressions in the actual original $R,V,D$ data. Ordered
pairs retain both off-diagonal quadratic contributions.
No moment or period entry is replaced by a free coefficient.

For $q=\zeta_5^{-j}$, the exact original factor at zero is
\[
 \boxed{\quad
 f_j(0,z)=\frac{4i\delta\gamma}{(\alpha^2+\chi^2)^2}
          \sum_{i=0}^3A_i(\alpha,\chi;z)q^i.
 \quad}\tag{FUT4}
\]
To prove this, expand
$R^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(q,q^2,q^3,q^4)R=\sum_bq^bE_b$.
By FUT1 the numerator coefficient vector is
$\sum_bq^b(\alpha^2h_{b,0}+\alpha\chi h_{b,1}+\chi^2h_{b,2})$.
The identity $Q_0(Vp)=4i\delta\gamma p^{\mathsf T}Qp$ from OPB8
gives FUT3's sum. Group exponents modulo five and then use
$q^4=-1-q-q^2-q^3$; the coefficients become exactly $A_i$.
This proves FUT4 through the original coordinate maps and restores
the complete unit denominator.

\subsection{Two real quartics for each original conjugate factor pair}
Put $s=\sqrt5$, and define the following four real homogeneous quartics:
\[
 \begin{split}
 P_1&=4A_0+(s-1)A_1-(s+1)(A_2+A_3),\\
 J_1&=2A_1+(s-1)(A_2-A_3),\\
 P_2&=4A_0-(s+1)A_1+(s-1)(A_2+A_3),\\
 J_2&=(s-1)A_1-2A_2+2A_3.
 \end{split}\tag{FUT5}
\]
They give the full real equations
\[
 \begin{split}
 f_1(0,z)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ f_4(0,z)=0
       &\ \Longleftrightarrow\ P_1=J_1=0,\\
 f_2(0,z)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ f_3(0,z)=0
       &\ \Longleftrightarrow\ P_2=J_2=0.
 \end{split}\tag{FUT6}
\]
Indeed let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/5}$ and $S=\sin(2\pi/5)>0$.
Writing $N(q)=\sum_iA_iq^i$, the exact trigonometric values give
$N(\zeta)=P_1/4+iSJ_1/2$ and
$N(\zeta^2)=P_2/4+iSJ_2/2$.
The other two values are their conjugates since every $A_i$ is real.
The nonzero prefactor in FUT4 proves all equivalences in FUT6.
In particular the complete original order-zero value vanishes at the
actual unit precisely when
\[
 E_A(0,z)=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 (P_1(\alpha,\chi;z),J_1(\alpha,\chi;z))=(0,0)
 \ \hbox{or}\ 
 (P_2(\alpha,\chi;z),J_2(\alpha,\chi;z))=(0,0).
 \tag{FUT7}
\]
All four original labels remain; the pairing is the proved conjugation
identity, not removal of their factors or multiplicities.

These quartic coefficients are real even entire functions of $z$.
For the proof use $E_{\rm par}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1,-1)$.
PCL22 gives $R(-z)=E_{\rm par}R(z)E_{\rm par}$;
$K$ commutes with $E_{\rm par}$, $E_{\rm par}e_0=e_0$, and
$E_{\rm par}^{\mathsf T}QE_{\rm par}=Q$.
Hence $h_{b,v}(-z)=E_{\rm par}h_{b,v}(z)$ and every term in FUT3
is even. Reality and entire convergence follow directly from PCL2
and its adjugate, proving the assertion for every actual real period.

\subsection{Exact phase elimination, including the real-root issue}
For either $b=1$ or $b=2$ write
\[
 P_b(\alpha,\chi)=\sum_{r=0}^4p_r\alpha^{4-r}\chi^r,
 \quad J_b(\alpha,\chi)=\sum_{r=0}^4j_r\alpha^{4-r}\chi^r,
 \quad c_{rs}=p_rj_s-p_sj_r.
 \tag{FUT8}
\]
All coefficients here are the specified actual entries of FUT3--5.
The complete quartic B\'ezout matrix is
\[
 \mathcal B_b=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 c_{10}&c_{20}&c_{30}&c_{40}\\
 c_{20}&c_{30}+c_{21}&c_{40}+c_{31}&c_{41}\\
 c_{30}&c_{40}+c_{31}&c_{41}+c_{32}&c_{42}\\
 c_{40}&c_{41}&c_{42}&c_{43}
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \mathscr R_b(z)=\det\mathcal B_b(z).
 \tag{FUT9}
\]
This is an explicitly computed finite determinant in the original
period entries, not a determinant with unspecified amplitudes.
Let $p(X)=\sum p_rX^r$ and $j(X)=\sum j_rX^r$.
Expansion of all ten pairs $0\le s<r\le4$ proves
\[
 (1,X,X^2,X^3)\mathcal B_b(1,Y,Y^2,Y^3)^{\mathsf T}
       =\frac{p(X)j(Y)-p(Y)j(X)}{X-Y}.
 \tag{FUT10}
\]
For example a pair $(r,s)$ contributes
$c_{rs}\sum_{v=0}^{r-s-1}X^{r-1-v}Y^{s+v}$,
which lists every entry of FUT9.

The determinant $\mathscr R_b$ is the classical homogeneous B\'ezout
resultant of the two binary quartics. Here is its full vanishing proof.
When $p_4\ne0$ and $p$ has distinct roots $r_1,\ldots,r_4$,
evaluate FUT10 at all pairs of those roots. The resulting diagonal
entries are $p'(r_i)j(r_i)$, and the evaluation Vandermonde has
square determinant $\prod_{i<j}(r_j-r_i)^2$. Consequently
\[
 \mathscr R_b=p_4^4\prod_{i=1}^4j(r_i).
 \tag{FUT11}
\]
Both sides are polynomial identities in the coefficients when the
right side is expanded symmetrically. They therefore extend to repeated
roots and lower degrees. The homogeneous factorization into four
linear factors shows that this polynomial vanishes exactly when the
two binary quartics have a common complex projective zero, including
the zero-polynomial cases. This also proves directly that each
$\mathscr R_b$ is an even entire function of the actual inverse period.

To decide \emph{real} phases, the resultant alone is insufficient.
The following finite test resolves that issue as well. The phase
$[\alpha:\chi]=[0:1]$ is a zero precisely when
\[
                         p_4=j_4=0.
 \tag{FUT12}
\]
For every other phase retain its actual scale and write
$x=\chi/\alpha$, so $P_b=\alpha^4p(x)$ and
$J_b=\alpha^4j(x)$. This chart does not set the actual value of
$\alpha$ or $a$. If both $p,j$ vanish identically, every real phase
in this chart is a zero. Otherwise compute their last nonzero Euclidean
remainder $g(X)=g_dX^d+\cdots+g_0$, retaining its leading coefficient.
For a zero input polynomial use the other polynomial as $g$.
The division identities, reversed from the last remainder, prove
\[
             p(x)=j(x)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g(x)=0.
 \tag{FUT13}
\]
There are at most five Euclidean divisions because the initial degrees
are at most four. For $d=0$ there is no root in this chart.
For $1\le d\le4$ form the full companion multiplication matrix
\[
 T_g=\begin{pmatrix}
 0&0&\cdots&0&-g_0/g_d\\
 1&0&\cdots&0&-g_1/g_d\\
 0&1&\cdots&0&-g_2/g_d\\
 \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
 0&0&\cdots&1&-g_{d-1}/g_d
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \mathcal H_g=(\operatorname{Tr}(T_g^{r+s}))_{0\le r,s<d}.
 \tag{FUT14}
\]
The degree-one case is the single entry $-g_0/g_1$ in $T_g$.
All divisions displayed are by the already nonzero $g_d$; no original
coefficient or unit is changed. The number of distinct real roots of
$g$ is exactly the signature of this real symmetric matrix:
\[
   \#\{x\in\mathbb R:g(x)=0\}
          =n_+(\mathcal H_g)-n_-(\mathcal H_g).
 \tag{FUT15}
\]
This is Hermite's classical trace-form criterion. Its proof here also
covers multiple roots. Factor the real polynomial into its real linear
and irreducible real quadratic factors, with their actual positive
multiplicities. The Chinese remainder map decomposes
$\mathbb R[X]/(g)$ into the corresponding real local algebras.
At a real root of multiplicity $m$, multiplication has trace $m$
times the residue value; its trace pairing has one positive square
and the nilpotent ideal as radical. At a conjugate pair of multiplicity
$m$, the residue field is $\mathbb C$ and the real trace pairing is
$2m\operatorname{Re}(uv)$, with one positive and one negative square;
the nilpotent ideal again contributes only radical directions.
The trace pairing in the original power basis is exactly FUT14.
Adding these signatures proves FUT15, with no square-free assumption.

Equations FUT12--15 are therefore a complete necessary-and-sufficient
finite real-phase test at each actual real $z$: add the possible
projective endpoint to the number from FUT15, or use the identically
zero case stated above. In particular $\mathscr R_1\mathscr R_2\ne0$
excludes every phase immediately. If that product vanishes, FUT12--15
still decide whether any real phase survives rather than treating a
complex common root as an actual unit phase. Evaluation at the actual
unit always remains the exact two-equation test FUT6--7.

\subsection{Exact local obstructions for real phases which vary with the period}
There is a stronger result than the earlier fixed-phase PZ/PZX cutoffs.
For each fixed original quartet there exists $\epsilon>0$ such that
\[
 \boxed{\quad
 0<|z|<\epsilon,\ z\in\mathbb R
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 f_j(0,z)\ne0\quad
 \text{for every }a=\alpha+i\chi\ne0
 \text{ and every }j=1,2,3,4.
 \quad}\tag{FUT16}
\]
The proof retains phases which vary with $z$; this is why it is not a
repetition of the pointwise phase statement in PZ11 or PZX.

Here are the exact coefficient identities needed for the proof.
Use $R_n$ from the original PCL2 series and the inverse coefficients
$I_n,G_n$ of OPB9. In particular $G_n$ is the actual coefficient of
$R(z)^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(q,q^2,q^3,q^4)R(z)$.
For the first real phase chart $x=\chi/\alpha$ define
\[
 p_{n,0}=G_ne_0,\quad p_{n,1}=G_nKe_0-KG_ne_0,\quad
 p_{n,2}=-KG_nKe_0,\qquad
 N_{r,n}=\sum_{v=0}^n\ \sum_{\substack{0\le b,d\le2\\b+d=r}}
                 p_{v,b}^{\mathsf T}Qp_{n-v,d}.
 \tag{FUT17}
\]
The complete original function in this chart is
$f_j(0,z)=4i\delta\gamma(1+x^2)^{-2}
\sum_{r=0}^4\sum_{n\ge0}N_{r,n}x^rz^n$.
This formula follows from FUT1 by expanding the actual numerator;
it is convergent and includes every coefficient.
The boundary root at $x=0$ is simple, since
\[
 N_{0,0}=0,\qquad N_{1,0}=\frac{2(q^4-q^2)}k\ne0.
 \tag{FUT18}
\]
The analytic implicit construction used in OPB18 therefore gives the
unique nearby complex root, even in $z$,
$x(z)=a_2z^2+a_4z^4+O(z^6)$. Its coefficients are the finite exact
expressions
\[
 a_2=-\frac{N_{0,2}}{N_{1,0}}
 =\frac{k\beta^2(\beta^2-9\eta)q^2(q^2+3q+1)}{162(q+1)},
 \qquad
 a_4=-\frac{N_{0,4}+N_{1,2}a_2+N_{2,0}a_2^2}{N_{1,0}}.
 \tag{FUT19}
\]
All $N_{r,n}$ in this line are obtained from $R_0,\ldots,R_4$ by
the finite sums FUT17 and OPB9. The first coefficient is real:
$q^2(q^2+3q+1)/(q+1)=2q^3/(q+1)-1$, and
$\overline{q^3/(q+1)}=q^3/(q+1)$ by $q^5=1$.

The fourth coefficient has the following exact nonzero conjugation
defect. Substitution of the PCL2 coefficients into FUT17--19 gives
\[
 \begin{split}
 a_4-\bar a_4
 &=\frac{\beta^3k(\beta^2-9\eta)}{1102248}
   \left[U_0(q^3-q^2)+V_0(1+2q+q^2+q^3)\right],\\
 U_0&=82\beta^4-2856\beta^2\eta+15120\eta^2,\qquad
 V_0=89\beta^4-168\beta^2\eta.
 \end{split}\tag{FUT20}
\]
This equality can be checked without any phase limit: construct
$I_n=-I_0\sum_{v=1}^nR_vI_{n-v}$ for $n\le4$, then
$G_n=\sum_{v=0}^nI_v\operatorname{diag}(q,q^2,q^3,q^4)R_{n-v}$,
insert them in FUT17--19, and reduce only the exact identities
$q^4+q^3+q^2+q+1=0$ and $k^2=\eta-\beta^2/4$.
The result is the displayed polynomial; these finite formulas specify
every entry and denominator in the calculation.

We prove that FUT20 never vanishes on the original domain.
Let $h=(\sqrt5-1)/2$, so $3/5<h<2/3$, and let
$t=\beta^2/\eta\in(0,4)$ denote the exact coefficient ratio used
only to assess its sign. At $q=\zeta$ the bracket in FUT20 equals
$2i\sin(2\pi/5)(V_0-hU_0)$, while at $q=\zeta^2$ it equals
$2i\sin(2\pi/5)(U_0+hV_0)$. The other two values are the conjugates.
The complete two real factors, including their $\eta^2$, are
\[
 \begin{split}
 \frac{V_0-hU_0}{\eta^2}
 &=(89-82h)t^2+(-168+2856h)t-15120h<0,\\
 \frac{U_0+hV_0}{\eta^2}
 &=(82+89h)t^2+(-2856-168h)t+15120>0.
 \end{split}\tag{FUT21}
\]
For the first quadratic its derivative is positive on $[0,4]$,
and its endpoint value is $752-5008h<0$.
For the second its derivative is at most its value at $4$,
$-2200+544h<0$, and its endpoint value is $5008+752h>0$.
These derivative statements follow directly from $3/5<h<2/3$.
The prefactor in FUT20 is nonzero. Thus $a_4$ is not real and
$x(z)$ is nonreal for every sufficiently small nonzero real $z$.

At the other boundary phase put $c=\beta/(6k)$ and retain the actual
chart $e=\alpha/\chi+c$, where $\chi\ne0$; thus
$a=\chi(-c+e+i)$. Define $W_0=-cI+K$, $\bar W_0=-cI-K$ and
\[
 \begin{split}
 p'_{n,0}&=\bar W_0G_nW_0e_0,\\
 p'_{n,1}&=G_nW_0e_0+\bar W_0G_ne_0,\qquad
 p'_{n,2}=G_ne_0,
 \end{split}\tag{FUT22}
\]
and $N'_{r,n}$ by the same full double sum as FUT17.
The original function is
$4i\delta\gamma(1+(-c+e)^2)^{-2}\sum N'_{r,n}e^rz^n$.
Its boundary root is simple:
$N'_{1,0}=2(1+c^2)q^3(q^3-q)/k\ne0$.
Hence its unique nearby complex root is
$e(z)=b_2z^2+O(z^4)$, with $b_2=-N'_{0,2}/N'_{1,0}$.
The same finite PCL2 coefficient calculation, here requiring only
$R_0,R_1,R_2$, gives
\[
 b_2-\bar b_2=
 \frac{\beta^4k(\beta^2-9\eta)(4\beta^2-27\eta)}
      {6561(\beta^2-4\eta)(2\beta^2-9\eta)}
       (11q^3-3q^2+8q+4)\ne0.
 \tag{FUT23}
\]
For the nonvanishing, write the last polynomial as
$7(q^3-q^2)+4(1+2q+q^2+q^3)$.
Its two relevant imaginary factors are $4-7h<0$ and $7+4h>0$,
with their conjugates for the other two roots. Every real scalar
factor is nonzero since $\beta>0$, $k>0$ and $\beta^2<4\eta$.
It follows that $e(z)$ too is nonreal for all sufficiently small
nonzero real $z$.

To finish the uniform assertion FUT16, the exact boundary calculation
OPB3--6 shows that the only real projective zeros of any factor are
$\chi=0$ and $\alpha+c\chi=0$. They are the two simple phase roots
just treated. Choose disjoint projective neighborhoods small enough
that the corresponding complex analytic zero is unique for each of
the four factors. FUT20--23 exclude real zeros in those neighborhoods
for small nonzero real $z$. On the complement, compactness of the
real projective line and continuity of the original phase quotient
give a strictly positive minimum of all four boundary absolute values.
The PCL2 entire series converges uniformly there for $z$ in a small
closed disk, so all four values remain nonzero on that complement.
Take the minimum of these finitely many positive radii.
This proves FUT16 uniformly over every actual unit phase and scale.
It also verifies the five-orbit condition there directly. If the
$j$th conjugated quadric were proportional to the original $Q_0$,
evaluation at $\boldsymbol1$ would give $f_j(0,z)=0$, since
$Q_0(\boldsymbol1)=0$. This contradicts FUT16 for every $j=1,2,3,4$.
Two distinct orbit members could be proportional only if conjugating
by the inverse of one of their powers made a nontrivial orbit member
proportional to $Q_0$, which was just excluded. The original invertible
period matrices therefore give five distinct quadric factors on this
region. The original conductor has $v=0$ there; no missing bound is
assumed and no RH conclusion is inferred.

\subsection{Every real-unit factor is genuinely nonquadratic in the inverse period}
The first nonzero real-unit period coefficient is the existing PZX
coefficient OPB15. Its next even coefficient is also nonzero for every
original quartet and all four factors. This supplies an exact input
for the independent entire-function analysis, without claiming that
any resulting complex zero is in the admitted five-orbit domain.

For the complete real-unit function, set
$d_j(z)=f_j(0,z)/(4i\delta\gamma)$; this is only an explicitly
restored nonzero scalar in the coefficient formula. All its coefficients
are $[z^n]d_j=\sum_{v=0}^n(G_ve_0)^{\mathsf T}Q(G_{n-v}e_0)$,
with the original $G_n$ above. For $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/5}$, substitution
of $R_0,\ldots,R_4$ gives the following exact imaginary parts:
\[
 \begin{split}
 \operatorname{Im}\left(
   \frac{-1653372\,[z^4]d_1}{\beta(\beta^2-9\eta)}\right)
 &=\eta^3\sin(2\pi/5)F_1(t),\\
 \operatorname{Im}\left(
   \frac{1653372\,[z^4]d_2}{\beta(\beta^2-9\eta)}\right)
 &=\eta^3\sin(2\pi/5)F_2(t),
 \end{split}\tag{FUT24}
\]
where $t=\beta^2/\eta$, $h=(\sqrt5-1)/2$, and the full polynomials are
\[
 \begin{split}
 F_1(t)&=(169+48h)t^3-(2583+378h)t^2
                 +(11340-2835h)t+25515h,\\
 F_2(t)&=(48-169h)t^3+(-378+2583h)t^2
                 -(2835+11340h)t+25515.
 \end{split}\tag{FUT25}
\]
Their exact degree-three Bernstein coefficients on $[0,4]$ are
\[
 \begin{split}
 B_1&=(25515h,\ 15120+21735h,\ 16464+15939h,\ 14848+11199h),\\
 B_2&=(25515,\ 21735-15120h,\ 15939-16464h,\ 11199-14848h),\\
 F_j(t)&=\sum_{r=0}^3(B_j)_r\binom3r(t/4)^r(1-t/4)^{3-r}.
 \end{split}\tag{FUT26}
\]
Expansion verifies these identities. Every entry of $B_1$ is positive.
Every entry of $B_2$ is positive already with $h<2/3$; the last three
are greater respectively than $11655$, $4963$, and $3901/3$.
The Bernstein basis is nonnegative and sums to one, so both $F_j$
are strictly positive throughout $[0,4]$.
FUT24 therefore proves $[z^4]d_1,[z^4]d_2\ne0$.
The exact original conjugation gives $d_{5-j}(z)=\overline{d_j(z)}$
for real $z$, and hence the same result for $j=3,4$.
All four real-unit factors have both a nonzero second and a nonzero
fourth inverse-period coefficient. No sampled value is used in this
nonvanishing proof.
