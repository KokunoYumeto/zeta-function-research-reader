\section{The complete arithmetic signed order and its algebra conductor}
This section independently verifies equations (9)--(31) of the preserved
user-supplied defining-prime continuation
\cite[eqs.\ (9)--(31)]{DefiningPrimeReceived}. Its original signed algebra,
residue convention, and inverse factor coordinates are RD1--8 and FS13
of the cumulative manuscript, retaining the human polynomial, ES-reader,
and residue provenance \cite{ES488,Tao,JouveRV}. The original ES marking is retained as
$(\mathrm M,\mathrm N,\mathrm T,\mathrm E)=(p,x,y,z)$ in this section;
this is a declared marking and does not identify a historical operator
named $A$ with the formal label $\mathrm N$. The four labels and both
signs at each label are always retained. The algebra conductor calculated
below is an ideal of a finite arithmetic algebra. Its exact transport to
the original weighted linear conductor is supplied at the end; those two
uses of the word conductor are not identified by an assertion.

Let $p$ be a rational prime with $p\equiv1\pmod {12}$ and let $x,y,z$
be positive integers satisfying $4/p=1/x+1/y+1/z$. Put
\begin{equation}
 r_0=p,\quad r_1=x,\quad r_2=y,\quad r_3=z,\qquad
 S=p+x+y+z,\quad f(r)=\prod_{i=0}^3(r-r_i),\quad
 A=-S^{-1},\quad h=Af=Ar^4+r^3+Br^2+Cr+D.
 \tag{ISR1}
\end{equation}
All coefficients in ISR1 are literal coefficients of the supplied
quartic. In particular its cubic coefficient equals one.

\subsection{All actual witness valuation patterns}
The four integers $r_i$ are pairwise distinct. To prove distinctness of
two denominators, suppose $y=z$ and set $d=4x-p>0$. The reciprocal
equation gives $dy=2px$, hence $2dy=p(d+p)$ and $d\mid p^2$.
But $d\equiv-p\equiv3\pmod4$, whereas all positive divisors of $p^2$
are $1\pmod4$. If $x=p$, the equation gives
$(3y-p)(3z-p)=p^2$. Its two factors are positive and $2\pmod3$;
every positive divisor of $p^2$ is $1\pmod3$. These contradictions,
and permutation of the denominator labels, prove the assertion.

The exact equation $4xyz=p(xy+xz+yz)$ implies that at least one
denominator is divisible by $p$. All three cannot be, since writing
them $pX,pY,pZ$ would give $4=1/X+1/Y+1/Z\le3$. If only $z$ is
divisible, valuation comparison gives $z=pZ$ with $Z$ a unit, and
division of the equation by $p$ gives
\begin{equation}
 4xyZ=xy+pZ(x+y),\qquad Z\equiv1/4\pmod p,
 \qquad v_p(p-z)=1.                                      \tag{ISR2}
\end{equation}
For two divisible denominators write their valuations $a,b\ge1$.
If $a<b$, then the right side of the exact equation has valuation
$a+1$, so $a+b=a+1$, a contradiction to $b>a\ge1$.
Thus $a=b$. If $a=b\ge2$, positivity gives
$p/4<x\le p^2/(4p-2)<(p+1)/4$, an interval containing no integer
because $p\equiv1\pmod4$. Therefore, after declaring the denominator
permutation, the only two cases are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{ll}
 \text{one divisible:}&z=pZ,\ p\nmid xyZ;\\
 \text{two divisible:}&y=pY,\ z=pZ,\ p\nmid xYZ,
       \quad Y+Z\equiv4YZ\pmod p.
 \end{array}                                             \tag{ISR3}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Integral closure, every inclusion entry, and residue duality}
First suppose $p\nmid S$. Set $R=\mathbb Z_p$, $K=\mathbb Q_p$ and
\begin{equation}
 E=R[r,\sigma]/(f(r),\sigma^2-Af'(r)),\qquad
 D_i=Af'(r_i),\quad n_{ij}=v_p(r_i-r_j),\quad
 b_i=\sum_{j\ne i}n_{ij},\quad
 m_i=\lfloor b_i/2\rfloor,\quad \epsilon_i=b_i-2m_i,\quad
 u_i=p^{-b_i}D_i\in R^\times.                            \tag{ISR4}
\end{equation}
The ordered basis of $E$ is $(1,r,r^2,r^3,\sigma,\sigma r,
\sigma r^2,\sigma r^3)$. Both relations are monic in the eliminated
variable, so successive polynomial division proves this is a free
basis, without a reduced-fibre assumption. Since the $r_i$ are distinct,
evaluation and labelled Lagrange interpolation give the generic
product and its full integral closure
\begin{equation}
 E_K=\prod_{i=0}^3 K[\sigma_i]/(\sigma_i^2-D_i),\qquad
 O=\prod_{i=0}^3 O_i,\qquad
 O_i=R[\tau_i]/(\tau_i^2-p^{\epsilon_i}u_i),\qquad
 \sigma_i=p^{m_i}\tau_i.                                \tag{ISR5}
\end{equation}
Here a quadratic algebra is allowed to split. To prove that $O_i$ is
the entire integral closure, every element of its generic algebra has
a unique expression $a+b\tau_i$, including in the split case. If it
is integral, its conjugate is integral; its trace $2a$ is in $R$.
Since $p\ne2$, $a\in R$. Then $(b\tau_i)^2=b^2p^{\epsilon_i}u_i$
is integral in $K$, so $2v_p(b)+\epsilon_i\ge0$ and $b\in R$.
Conversely every $a,b\in R$ give an integral element, using the monic
quadratic relation. This proves maximality in both cases. The case
$\epsilon_i=1$ is ramified: a square in $K$ cannot have odd valuation,
and $\tau_i$ has valuation $1/2$. The case $\epsilon_i=0$ is split
when $u_i$ is a residue square and is an unramified quadratic field
when it is a residue nonsquare. Indeed a residue root lifts uniquely
by the recurrence $a_{n+1}=a_n+p^n d_n$ solving
$2a_n d_n=(u_i-a_n^2)/p^n\pmod p$; a residue nonsquare gives a
degree-two residue field in the displayed maximal ring.

Use the normalization basis $(1_0,1_1,1_2,1_3,\tau_0,\tau_1,
\tau_2,\tau_3)$, so that its unit is $(1,1,1,1,0,0,0,0)$.
With $V=(r_i^j)_{0\le i,j\le3}$ the complete inclusion and inverse are
\begin{equation}
 C_p=\operatorname{diag}(V,\operatorname{diag}(p^{m_i})V),\qquad
 C_p^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(V^{-1},
                      V^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(p^{-m_i})),
 \quad (V^{-1})_{ji}=[r^j]\frac{f_i(r)}{f'(r_i)},\quad
 f_i(r)=\prod_{j\ne i}(r-r_j).                           \tag{ISR6}
\end{equation}
Direct evaluation proves both inverse compositions over $K$.
For $N=\sum_{i<j}n_{ij}$, its determinant and index are
\begin{equation}
 \det C_p=(\det V)^2p^{\sum m_i},\qquad
 L:=\operatorname{length}_R(O/E)
       =2N+\sum_i m_i=3N-\tfrac12\sum_i\epsilon_i.
                                                               \tag{ISR7}
\end{equation}
The determinant formula uses precisely the chosen root order, with
$\det V=\prod_{i<j}(r_j-r_i)$, and $\sum b_i=2N$.

Write a generic element as $R(r)+\sigma Q(r)$. The original RD
residue is
\begin{equation}
 \Lambda(R+\sigma Q)=\frac{[r^3](Q\bmod f)}{A}
    =\sum_i\frac{Q(r_i)}{D_i};\qquad

\end{equation}

\end{equation}
For a proof of integral self-duality that does not appeal to a
Gorenstein theorem, write $f=r^4+a_3r^3+a_2r^2+a_1r+a_0$.

Inserting these seven values proves both matrix products. Since $A$
is a unit, $B_E$ and its inverse are over $R$. Thus
$E^\#=\{z:\Lambda(zE)\subset R\}=E$.

\end{equation}

Conversely $zO\subset E$ implies $\Lambda(zO)\subset R$ by testing

\subsection{All invariant factors, including closer pairs}
For a matrix over $R$, the sum of its first $j$ Smith exponents is
the least valuation of a nonzero $j$-minor. This follows from invertible
row and column operations: the ideals generated by all $j$-minors
are unchanged, and a diagonal matrix with divisibility-ordered entries
has least product its first $j$ entries. The diagonal reduction exists
by repeatedly choosing an entry of minimum valuation and clearing its
row and column; all other entries are its multiples in a DVR. This
also proves the criterion used here without leaving a classification
step implicit.

Residue clusters whose differences are units separate over $R$: the
resultant of their monic polynomials is a product of unit differences.
The Sylvester determinant gives polynomials whose combination is a
unit, so their ideals are comaximal and the root algebra splits.
This split acts on both parity copies. A two-root block with gap
valuation $n$ has even Smith exponents $0,n$, by subtracting its
first row and then its constant multiple. Both derivative valuations
are $n$ when all external differences are units. Its odd exponents
are therefore $\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n+\lfloor n/2\rfloor$.
In the one-divisible case ISR2 separates $(p,z)$ from $(x,y)$, so
for $n=v_p(x-y)\ge0$ the eight exponents, as a multiset, are
\begin{equation}
 \{0,0,1,1\}\ \cup\
 \{0,\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n,n+\lfloor n/2\rfloor\},\qquad
 L=2+3n-(n\bmod2).                                      \tag{ISR16}
\end{equation}
The second block is allowed to have $n=0$, in which case it is the
sum of two separated singleton blocks and the formula remains literal.

In the two-divisible case the three clustered roots are $p,pY,pZ$.
The relation in ISR3 prevents $1,Y,Z$ from all coinciding: that would
give $2=4\pmod p$, impossible. If $Y=Z\pmod p$ they both equal
$1/2$; if $Y=1$, then $Z=1/3$, and conversely. Thus one has either
three differences of valuation one, or exactly one closer pair. Put
$n$ equal to the largest valuation and $m=\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor$.
After a row permutation the valuations are $(1,1,n)$ and derivative
valuations $(2,n+1,n+1)$, with row multipliers $(1,m,m)$.
The ordinary three-root Vandermonde has successive minimum minor
valuations $0,1,n+2$, hence exponents $0,1,n+1$.
For the odd weighted block its one-minor minimum is $1$.
The two-minors in columns $1,r$ have valuations $m+2,m+2,2m+n$;
all other two-minors have at least these valuations because the
corresponding determinant is a root difference times an integral
symmetric polynomial. Since $m+n\ge2$, their minimum is $m+2$.
The determinant has valuation $n+2+1+2m=n+2m+3$. Thus the odd
exponents are $1,m+1,n+m+1$. Adding the isolated root's two unit
factors proves the entire list
\begin{equation}
 0,0,0,1,1,m+1,n+1,n+m+1,\qquad
 L=3n+6-((n+1)\bmod2).                                 \tag{ISR17}
\end{equation}
For $n=1$ the list is $0,0,0,1,1,2,2,3$ and all quadratic
branches have even derivative valuation: the normalized fields are
unramified or split although the integral fibre has a nonreduced
six-dimensional cluster.

\subsection{The exact nilpotent statement and exterior cofactor field}
Let $m\ge2$ and let $k$ be a field. In the algebra

\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{rank}M_\sigma=m+1,\quad
 \operatorname{rank}M_{\sigma^2}=2,\quad
 \operatorname{rank}M_{\sigma^3}=1,\quad M_{\sigma^4}=0;
 \quad\text{Jordan lengths }4,\underbrace{2,\ldots,2}_{m-2}.
                                                               \tag{ISR29}
\end{equation}
Indeed $M_\sigma(R+\sigma Q)=u(0)r^{m-1}Q+\sigma R$ has
independent images of dimensions one and $m$. Multiplication by

$\sigma r^{m-1}$; multiplication by $\sigma^3$ has just the second.

Our examples have $p=1201$ and $m=2,3$; both requirements hold.
