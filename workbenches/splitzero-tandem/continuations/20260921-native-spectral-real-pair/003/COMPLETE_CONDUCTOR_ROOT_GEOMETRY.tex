\title{The roots of the complete conductor polynomial}
\author{Split-Zero research programme}
\date{21 September 2026}
\begin{document}\maketitle
The complete conductor relation produces a polynomial $d_A=\UA1$.
Its roots control the multiplication quotient connected to the actual
relation quotient by KF52--55. We prove quantitative root bounds in the
original physical coordinate, retaining every conductor coefficient.
Only $O_A(q^{1/8})$ roots can lie beyond $q^{7/8}/8$; every root is
$O_A(q)$; and their reciprocal $q$-scale limit is the actual exponential
symbol. These statements supply root geometry for that connecting map.
Its complete transformed metric remains part of the map.

\section{The original polynomial and its reciprocal expression}
Keep the simple-quartet domain $k\ge9$, $k\equiv1\pmod4$,
$0<\delta<1/2$, $\gamma>2$, and the fixed actual conductor coefficients
$a_{rs}$, $0\le r,s\le8$. Put
\[
\begin{gathered}
q=(k+1)^2,\quad q'=(k-7)^2,\quad c=k/2,\quad c'=c-4,\\
\beta_{ab}=c+(2a-k)\delta+i(2b-k)\gamma,\\
\beta'_{ab}=c'+(2a-k+8)\delta+i(2b-k+8)\gamma,\\
\chi(S)=\prod_{a,b=0}^k(S-\beta_{ab}),\qquad
\chi'(S')=\prod_{a,b=0}^{k-8}(S'-\beta'_{ab}),\\
b_{rs}=4+(2r-8)\delta+i(2s-8)\gamma,\qquad
\TA p(S')=\sum_{r,s}a_{rs}p(S'+b_{rs}).
\end{gathered}\tag{OR1}
\]
Here $\chi'$ is the named lower root polynomial, not a derivative.
Write
\[
E_A(w)=\sum_{r,s}a_{rs}e^{b_{rs}w},\quad
\mu_j=\sum_{r,s}a_{rs}b_{rs}^j,\quad
v=\operatorname{ord}_0E_A,\quad g=q-q'-v.
\tag{OR2}
\]
The distinct shifts give $0\le v\le80$ and $\mu_v\ne0$: otherwise
the first 81 moments would annihilate the invertible Vandermonde
matrix of those shifts, forcing every coefficient to be zero.
The original conductor is nonzero. The derivative expansion gives
$\deg\TA p=\deg p-v$ for $\deg p\ge v$ and leading coefficient
$\mu_v\binom{\deg p}{v}$ times that of $p$.

For each lower root, $\beta'_{ab}+b_{rs}=\beta_{a+r,b+s}$.
Thus $\chi'$ divides every shifted $\chi(S'+b_{rs})$, with full
quotient
\[
d_{rs}(S')=\prod_{\substack{0\le a,b\le k\\
(a,b)\notin\{r,\ldots,r+k-8\}\times\{s,\ldots,s+k-8\}}}
(S'+b_{rs}-\beta_{ab}).
\]
Consequently
\[
d_A(S')=\frac{\TA\chi(S')}{\chi'(S')}=\sum_{r,s}a_{rs}d_{rs}(S'),
\quad \deg d_A=g,\quad
[S'^g]d_A=\mu_v\binom qv.
\tag{OR3}
\]
This is precisely KF41 and KF52, proved again here at $h=1$.

Retain the physical coordinate $S'=c'+iy$. Set
\[
y_\alpha=\frac{\beta_\alpha-c'}i,\quad
D=\sqrt{\delta^2+\gamma^2},\quad B_k=4+kD,\quad
B=\max_{a_{rs}\ne0}|b_{rs}|,\quad A_1=\sum_{r,s}|a_{rs}|.
\tag{OR4}
\]
Both upper and lower roots in this physical coordinate have modulus
at most $B_k$. The auxiliary variable $z=q/y$ is the specified
reciprocal map, with inverse $y=q/z$; it does not replace the
physical coordinate in the source or metric. On $|z|<q/B_k$ put
\[
\begin{split}
G_k(z)&=\frac{\TA\chi(c'+iq/z)}{\chi(c'+iq/z)}
=\sum_{r,s}a_{rs}\prod_{\alpha=1}^q
 \left(1+\frac{-ib_{rs}z}{q-y_\alpha z}\right),\\
F_k(z)&=z^{-v}G_k(z).
\end{split}\tag{OR5}
\]
The apparent singularity at zero is removable and
\[
F_k(0)=(-i)^v\mu_v\frac{\binom qv}{q^v}.
\tag{OR6}
\]
Indeed the polynomial degree calculation in OR3 gives
$G_k(z)=\mu_v\binom qv(iq/z)^{-v}+O(z^{v+1})$.
The denominators in OR5 are nonzero in its disk, proving
holomorphy there. For $q\ge2v$, with the convention $v=0$ allowed,
\[
|F_k(0)|\ge a_*:=\frac{|\mu_v|}{2^v v!}>0.
\tag{OR7}
\]
This follows from $\binom qv/q^v=(v!)^{-1}
\prod_{j=0}^{v-1}(1-j/q)$, each factor at least $1/2$.

\section{A finite count of distant roots}
For $4B_kR\le q$, the circle $|z|=2R$ has
$|q-y_\alpha z|\ge q/2$. Therefore
\[
\max_{|z|=2R}|F_k(z)|
 \le \frac{A_1e^{4BR}}{(2R)^v}.
\tag{OR8}
\]
In fact each factor in OR5 has modulus at most
$1+2B|z|/q$, and their product is at most $e^{2B|z|}$.

We use the following finite zero count, with its proof included.
For a holomorphic $f$ on a neighborhood of $|z|\le2R$,
$f(0)\ne0$, the number of its zeros in $|z|\le R$, counted with
multiplicity, satisfies
\[
n_f(R)\log2\le
\log\max_{|z|=2R}|f(z)|-\log|f(0)|.
\tag{OR9}
\]
To prove it first choose a circle of radius $t$ without a boundary
zero. Factor all zeros $\zeta_j$ inside that circle:
$f(z)=h(z)\prod_j(z-\zeta_j)$ with $h$ zero-free there.
The real part of a holomorphic logarithm of $h$ has its mean value
at zero. Also
\[
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log|te^{i\theta}-\zeta|\,d\theta=\log t
\quad(|\zeta|<t),
\]
by the convergent series for $\log(1-\zeta e^{-i\theta}/t)$,
whose nonconstant Fourier terms have zero mean. Subtracting the
value at zero gives $\sum_j\log(t/|\zeta_j|)$, bounded by the
logarithmic maximum minus $\log|f(0)|$.
Let $t$ tend to $2R$ through circles without boundary zeros.
Each zero in $|z|\le R$ contributes at least $\log2$ in the limit.
This proves OR9, including boundary zeros. It is the needed form
of Jensen's classical formula \cite{Jensen}; the harmonic mean
and Cauchy formulas are \cite[1.9.31, 1.9.33]{DLMF1}.

Let $n_A(t)$ count roots of $d_A(c'+iy)$ with $|y|\ge t$,
with their full multiplicities. Under $q\ge2v$ and $4B_kR\le q$,
the original $\chi$ and $\chi'$ have no zeros where $|y|\ge q/R$.
OR3 and OR5 therefore give the exact multiplicity-preserving
bijection $y\mapsto q/y$ between those roots of $d_A$ and the
zeros of $F_k$ in $0<|z|\le R$. It follows that
\[
n_A(q/R)\le
\frac{\log(A_1/a_*)+4BR-v\log(2R)}{\log2}.
\tag{OR10}
\]
A convenient weaker bound for $R\ge1/2$ is
\[
n_A(q/R)\le
\frac{\lp(A_1/a_*)+4BR}{\log2}.
\tag{OR11}
\]
Every displayed guard is finite and part of the assertion.

In particular, impose
\[
q\ge2v,\qquad 32B_kq^{1/8}\le q.
\tag{OR12}
\]
Using $R=8q^{1/8}$ gives
\[
\boxed{n_A(q^{7/8}/8)\le
\frac{\lp(A_1/a_*)+32Bq^{1/8}}{\log2}
=O_A(q^{1/8})=o(k).}
\tag{OR13}
\]
All other roots lie in the disk of radius $q^{7/8}/8$.
The constants depend on the unchanged actual conductor; no
uniformity in a varying period or vanishing $\mu_v$ is asserted.
For fixed original data the guards hold eventually because
$B_k=O(k)$ and $q\asymp k^2$.

\section{Every root has a finite linear bound in q}
The absence of zeros near $z=0$ can also be quantified. Define
\[
R_*=\min\left\{1,\frac{9}{4(D+1)}\right\},\quad
M_*=\frac{A_1e^{2BR_*}}{R_*^v},\quad
r_* = R_*\min\left\{\frac12,\frac{a_*}{4M_*}\right\}.
\tag{OR14}
\]
These constants are strictly positive and independent of $k$.
For $k\ge9$, $B_kR_*\le q/2$: it suffices to bound
$B_k\le k(D+4/9)$ and $q\ge k^2$ and use
$R_*\le9/[4(D+1)]$.
Thus the proof of OR8 on the radius $R_*$ gives
$\max_{|z|=R_*}|F_k(z)|\le M_*$.
Cauchy's coefficient formula then gives, for $r<R_*$,
\[
|F_k(z)-F_k(0)|\le M_*\frac{r}{R_*-r}
\quad(|z|\le r).
\]
At $r=r_*$ the right side is at most $a_*/2$.
For $q\ge2v$ there is no zero in $|z|\le r_*$.
Since $q/r_*>B_k$, OR3--5 imply
\[
\boxed{d_A(c'+iy)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ |y|<q/r_*.}
\tag{OR15}
\]
Zeros with $y=0$ satisfy the bound directly. No root of $\chi'$
has been cancelled or lost in this argument.

A further aggregate estimate retains the entire root list. Put
\[
C_0=\frac{\lp(A_1/a_*)+v\lp(1/(2r_*))}{\log2},\qquad
C_1=\frac{4B}{\log2},\qquad T=q/r_*.
\]
For $4B_k\le t\le T$, OR10 with $R=q/t$ gives
$n_A(t)\le C_0+C_1q/t$. Integrating the counting function yields
\[
\boxed{\sum_{\zeta:\ d_A(c'+i\zeta)=0}|\zeta|
\le 4gB_k+C_0T+C_1q\lp\frac{T}{4B_k}
=O_A(q\log(q+2)).}
\tag{OR16}
\]
When $T<4B_k$ the first term already bounds the sum; otherwise split
the integral at $4B_k$ and use OR15. If $n=q/2$, the monic polynomial
in the dimensionless variable $w=y/n$ has coefficient norm
\[
\left\|\prod_\zeta(w-\zeta/n)\right\|_1
\le\prod_\zeta(1+|\zeta|/n),\qquad
\log\left\|\prod_\zeta(w-\zeta/n)\right\|_1
\le\frac1n\sum_\zeta|\zeta|=O_A(\log(q+2)).
\tag{OR17}
\]
The leading coefficient of the actual polynomial has not vanished:
\[
d_A(c'+iny)=\mu_v\binom qv\,i^g n^g
                 \prod_\zeta(y-\zeta/n).
\]
OR17 concerns the explicitly displayed monic factor only.

\section{The exact symbol governing the roots at q-scale}
Fix $R>0$. On $|z|\le R$ impose
$B_kR\le q/2$ and $q\ge4BR$. For
$w_{\alpha,rs}=-ib_{rs}z/(q-y_\alpha z)$ one has
$|w_{\alpha,rs}|\le1/2$. The convergent logarithm gives
\[
\left|\sum_\alpha\log(1+w_{\alpha,rs})+ib_{rs}z\right|
\le e_k(R):=\frac{2BB_kR^2+4B^2R^2}{q}.
\tag{OR18}
\]
Indeed
\[
\left|\sum_\alpha\frac1{q-y_\alpha z}-1\right|
\le\frac{B_kR}{q-B_kR}\le\frac{2B_kR}{q},
\]
and $|\log(1+w)-w|\le |w|^2$ for $|w|\le1/2$.
Summing the latter bound over $q$ factors proves OR18.
Exponentiation and $|e^u-1|\le e^{|u|}|u|$ give
\[
\max_{|z|\le R}|G_k(z)-E_A(-iz)|
\le A_1e^{BR+e_k(R)}e_k(R).
\tag{OR19}
\]
Both terms have a zero of order at least $v$ at zero. Applying
the maximum principle to their difference divided by $z^v$
on a larger disk of radius $2R$ gives the explicit bound
\[
\max_{|z|\le R}\left|F_k(z)-\frac{E_A(-iz)}{z^v}\right|
\le\frac{A_1e^{2BR+e_k(2R)}e_k(2R)}{(2R)^v},
\tag{OR20}
\]
when the guards for $2R$ hold. Thus this difference is
$O_{A,R}(1/k)$ on every fixed disk, including its removable value
at zero.

For any bounded region with piecewise smooth boundary, containing
no zero of $E_A(-iz)/z^v$ on its boundary, OR20 preserves the full
number of zeros for all sufficiently large $k$. Here is the
counting argument. If the boundary minimum is $b>0$ and the
right side of OR20 is smaller than $b$, the homotopy
$f+t(F_k-f)$ is nonzero on that boundary, where $f=E_A(-iz)/z^v$.
Its boundary winding number is constant in $t$. By factoring
each isolated zero and integrating the logarithmic derivative,
this winding number counts all interior zeros with multiplicity.
Consequently every nonzero zero $z_0$ of $E_A(-iz)$ of multiplicity
$j$ has exactly $j$ corresponding roots, in arbitrarily small
isolating disks for $z_0$, satisfying
\[
\boxed{\frac{y_\nu(k)}q\longrightarrow \frac1{z_0}.}
\tag{OR21}
\]
Conversely each nonzero finite accumulation point of $y_\nu(k)/q$
is the reciprocal of a nonzero symbol zero: use OR20 at the
reciprocal point, which must have limiting function value zero.
This is a proved correspondence on the actual symbol's zero set;
it does not presume a particular additional zero or identify
these conductor roots with zeta zeros.

\section{Return to the full conductor quotient}
At $N=q-1,q,2q-1,2q$ set $L=N-q=-1,0,q-1,q$.
The exact target is
\[
\begin{gathered}
Y_N=\Pp'_{L+g}/\UA\Pp_L,\qquad
\UA h=\sum_{r,s}a_{rs}d_{rs}(S')h(S'+b_{rs}),\\
\|\eta\|^2=\int_\mathbb R|\chi'(c'+iy)\eta(c'+iy)|^2
 \frac{|\Gamma(1/4+iy/2)|^2}{2\pi}\,dy .
\end{gathered}
\tag{OR22}
\]
Euclidean polynomial division defines the unique maps
$\UA h=d_A\mathcal C_Lh+\mathcal R_Lh$ with
$\deg\mathcal R_Lh<g$. Their exact leading terms give
\[
\det\mathcal C_L=\prod_{j=0}^L
  \frac{\binom{q+j}{v}}{\binom qv},\qquad
\mathcal F_L=\mathcal R_L\mathcal C_L^{-1}.
\]
In the decomposition $r+d_Aw$, $\deg r<g$, the map
\[
\mathcal J_L(r+d_Aw)=r-\mathcal F_Lw+d_Aw
\tag{OR23}
\]
fixes the actual low representative frame and sends
$\UA\Pp_L$ to $d_A\Pp_L$. Its inverse has the plus sign.
It is an isometry only when the target metric is
\[
H_N^{\rm target}=(\mathcal J_L^{-1})^*H_N\mathcal J_L^{-1}.
\tag{OR24}
\]
This follows by substituting $\mathcal J_Lx$ in the target
quadratic form; no smallness of $\mathcal F_L$ is used.
At $L=-1$ the map is the identity.

For the nonexceptional roots in OR13, the scaled bound satisfies
\[
\frac{q^{7/8}/8}{q/2}=\frac14q^{-1/8}
\le \frac12(q/2)^{-1/8}.
\tag{OR25}
\]
This is exactly the root-radius guard in OOQ13 with $n=q/2$
and $\rho=n^{-1/8}$. The roots outside that disk are still
present, with the explicit count OR13 and full bound OR15.
Neither deleting their directions from the actual kernel nor
discarding OR24 follows from their count. The metric comparison
through OR24, on the unchanged frame
$I_K=V^{-1}\pi^T$ for $Z=[M_A,S_kZ_k]$, remains to be evaluated.
KF49 has already bounded the preceding low-degree and conductor
transport costs by $o(kq)$. OR13--21 evaluate the new root
geometry needed for the next part of that same calculation.
