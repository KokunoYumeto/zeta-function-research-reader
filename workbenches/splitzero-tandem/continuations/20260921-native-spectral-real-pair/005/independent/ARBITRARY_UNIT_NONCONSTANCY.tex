\section{Every original unit gives nonconstant factors and nonzero finite complex zeros}
Retain the complete original data of PCL1--10, OFM1--11 and FUT1--4
\cite{PCLOriginal,NC,Cumulative,PeriodZeroOriginal}. In particular
$0<\delta<1/2$, $\gamma>2$, $a=\alpha+i\chi\ne0$ with real
$\alpha,\chi$, $\beta=2(\gamma^2-\delta^2)>0$,
$\eta=(\delta^2+\gamma^2)^2$, and $k=2\delta\gamma>0$.
Write
\[
 F_j(z)=f_j(0,z)
 =Q_0\!\left(U^{-1}V\mathcal R(z)^{-1}D_5^{-j}
                  \mathcal R(z)V^{-1}U\boldsymbol1\right),
 \qquad j=1,2,3,4.
 \tag{ANP1}
\]
Here $z=1/u$ is the actual inverse original period, not the
exponential-curve variable. The functions in ANP1 are even entire.
We prove the following assertions for every allowed original unit:
\[
 \boxed{\quad
 ([z^2]F_j,[z^4]F_j)\ne(0,0),\qquad
 F_j\text{ has a zero }z_j\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}.
 \quad}\tag{ANP2}
\]
Thus there are no real unit phases for which all nonconstant
inverse-period coefficients vanish. This assertion does not select
the unit or any coefficient independently of its original period.

\subsection{The complete second and fourth coefficients with the original unit}
The following auxiliary coefficient variables retain an exact inverse
map to the unit and its denominator:
\[
 x=\frac\eta{\beta^2},\quad
 p=\alpha+\frac{\beta\chi}{2k},\quad b=\frac{\beta\chi}{k},
 \quad
 \alpha=p-b/2,\quad\chi=kb/\beta,\quad
 \alpha^2+\chi^2=p^2-pb+xb^2.
 \tag{ANP3}
\]
The allowed original domain gives $x>1/4$; in fact
$1/4<x<289/900$ because $0<\delta^2/\gamma^2<1/16$.
No original coordinate, unit scale, or denominator is discarded.
For $q=\zeta_5^{-j}$ define the polynomials $H_2(y,x,q)$ and
$H_4(y,x,q)$ by the full coefficient table at the end of this section:
$H_n=\sum_{r=0}^4h_{n,r}(x,q)y^r$.
The complete exact original coefficients are
\[
 \boxed{\quad
 [z^n]F_j(z)=
 \frac{4i\delta\gamma\,\beta^{5n/2-1}}
      {(\alpha^2+\chi^2)^2}
       \sum_{r=0}^4h_{n,r}(x,q)p^rb^{4-r},
       \qquad n=2,4.
 \quad}\tag{ANP4}
\]
For $b\ne0$ the numerator sum is $b^4H_n(p/b,x,q)$.
For $b=0$ it is $p^4h_{n,4}(x,q)$; this endpoint is retained
throughout. All four factors use their own original fifth root.

Here is a finite derivation of every table entry, also supplying an
independent way to reproduce the full coefficients. Use the PCL2
series, its inverse coefficients $I_n$, and the original $G_n$
defined in OPB9, through $n=4$. In the original coefficient basis put
\[
 T=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&-\eta\\1&0&0&0\\0&1&0&-\beta\\0&0&1&0\end{pmatrix},
 \quad W=pI+(b/\beta)T^2,\quad
 \bar W=(p-b)I-(b/\beta)T^2,
 \quad v_n=\bar W G_n W e_0.
 \tag{ANP5}
\]
The original unit is exactly multiplication by $W$, and
$\bar WW=(\alpha^2+\chi^2)I$, using
$T^4+\beta T^2+\eta I=0$. Thus
\[
 [z^n]F_j=
 \frac{4i\delta\gamma}{(\alpha^2+\chi^2)^2}
              \sum_{r=0}^n v_r^{\mathsf T}Qv_{n-r}.
 \tag{ANP6}
\]
This proves the table by finite multiplication, retaining every
ordered quadratic contribution and every original unit entry.
For the extracted power of $\beta$ in ANP4, a PCL2 entry has
the exact weighted degree $5n+r+1-a$ in the centered-root scale.
More explicitly, for $S=\operatorname{diag}(1,\beta^{1/2},\beta,\beta^{3/2})$,
its coefficient identity is
$R_n(\beta,\beta^2x)=\beta^{5n/2}S^{-1}R_n(1,x)S$.
The same identity holds for $G_n$; also
$T(\beta,\beta^2x)^2=\beta S^{-1}T(1,x)^2S$ and

Inserting these identities into ANP6 gives exactly

These are identities between the full matrices with their inverse
maps; the original $\beta,\eta,a$ are present on the left and
are restored with all their factors in ANP4.

\subsection{Exact elimination of simultaneous coefficient vanishing}
Write $H_n=\sum_{i=0}^3A_{n,i}(y,x)q^i$ using the table's
reduction modulo $q^4+q^3+q^2+q+1$. Put $s=\sqrt5$ and
\[
 P_n=4A_{n,0}+(s-1)A_{n,1}-(s+1)(A_{n,2}+A_{n,3}),
 \quad
 J_n=2A_{n,1}+(s-1)(A_{n,2}-A_{n,3}).
 \tag{ANP7}
\]
For real $x,y$ these are four times the real part and a nonzero
real multiple of the imaginary part at $q=e^{2\pi i/5}$,
as proved in FUT5--6. The same vanishing test applies at its conjugate.
Thus simultaneous coefficient vanishing for that original factor pair
would imply
$P_2=J_2=P_4=J_4=0$.

The complete degree-four resultant in $y$ is
\[
 \operatorname{Res}_y(P_2,J_2)
 =C(x-1/4)(x-1/9)^2\mathfrak F(x),\qquad
 C=-\frac{492160000000}{59049}
      +\frac{24448000000}{6561}\sqrt5,
 \tag{ANP8}
\]
where $\mathfrak F$ is monic of degree $19$ over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$.
It is specified completely either by this exact determinant identity
and ANP7 or by its coefficient table below.
The identity is obtained by the eight-by-eight Sylvester determinant
of the two displayed quartics; exact polynomial division leaves the
degree-nineteen factor and the nonzero constant $C$ shown.
The factors $x-1/4$ and $x-1/9$ cannot vanish on the original domain.

The following small cofactor matrix makes the residual phase equation
an explicit consequence of $P_2=J_2=0$. Form the $6$ by $7$ matrix
$M$ whose rows are the coefficients in descending powers
$y^6,\ldots,y^0$ of
\[
               y^2P_2,\ yP_2,\ P_2,\ y^2J_2,\ yJ_2,\ J_2.
 \tag{ANP9}
\]
Let $M_{0:4}$ denote its first five columns, and put
\[
 A(x)=\det[M_{0:4}\mid M_5],\qquad
 B(x)=\det[M_{0:4}\mid M_6].
 \tag{ANP10}
\]
Their degrees are $14$ and $15$ respectively.
Laplace expansion along the last column gives the same coefficients
for the linear combination of the six row polynomials in both
determinants. The coefficients of $y^6,\ldots,y^2$ vanish because
the corresponding determinant has a repeated column. Hence
$A(x)y+B(x)$ is a polynomial combination of $P_2,J_2$.
In particular every common zero of those original quartics satisfies
$A(x)y+B(x)=0$, including specializations of $x$.

for every original unit, including both endpoints of every phase chart.

\subsection{Nonzero finite complex zeros for all original units}
The FPZ25 entire-function estimate applies to the original arbitrary
unit without a changed constant. In the matrix norm used there,
$\|U\|_\infty=|a|$ and $\|U^{-1}\|_\infty=|a|^{-1}$, since
all four diagonal entries have the same modulus. Their product is one.
With the same actual $v_V=\|V\|_\infty$, $v_I=\|V^{-1}\|_\infty$,
$C_R,T_R$ and $H(r)=2C_R(1+T_Rr)e^{T_Rr}$ as FPZ25, ANP1 gives
\[
 |F_j(z)|\le2(96v_Vv_I)^2H(|z|)^8
          \le A(1+|z|)^8e^{B|z|},
 \quad
 A=2(96v_Vv_I)^2(2C_R)^8\max(1,T_R)^8,\quad B=8T_R.
 \tag{ANP16}
\]
All phase entries remain in the actual function; the displayed
equality of norms only proves this uniform growth estimate.

Off the two phase lines in ANP15, the exact boundary value is nonzero.
If $F_j$ were zero-free, it would have an entire logarithm $h$,
obtained by integrating $F_j'/F_j$ on the simply connected plane.
The growth estimate bounds $\operatorname{Re}h$ on $|z|=R$ by
$M_R=A_0+BR+8\log(1+R)$. Its power-series coefficients satisfy
$|h_n|\le2(M_R-\operatorname{Re}h(0))/R^n$ by the Fourier and
mean-value calculation in FPZ26. Letting $R\to\infty$ gives
$h_n=0$ for $n\ge2$. Since $F_j$ is even, the identity
$e^{2h_1z}=1$ for every $z$ forces $h_1=0$ by differentiation.
Thus a zero-free $F_j$ would be constant. This contradicts the first
assertion of ANP2, already proved by complete coefficient elimination.
It therefore has a zero, and that zero is nonzero because $F_j(0)\ne0$.

On either boundary phase, the zero at the origin alone would not
prove a finite original-period zero. ANP15 resolves that point.
If there were no nonzero zero, then $F_j(z)/z^2$ would be an even
entire zero-free function with nonzero value at the origin and the
same type of growth bound. The preceding logarithm argument would
make it constant. That would contradict $[z^4]F_j\ne0$ in ANP15.
Hence a nonzero zero exists on those phases too.
This proves the second assertion of ANP2 for each of the four
original factors, on the full original parameter and unit domain.

Every such zero gives a finite nonzero original period $u_j=1/z_j$.
Evenness retains the companion zero $-z_j$ of the same factor;
OFM9 retains the exact conjugate-factor correspondence.
This existence proof alone does not prove that an arbitrarily chosen
zero lies in the five-orbit locus, nor that its symbol order is one.
Those stronger conclusions at the particular certified point in
FPZ4--14 remain the conclusions of that full certificate.
