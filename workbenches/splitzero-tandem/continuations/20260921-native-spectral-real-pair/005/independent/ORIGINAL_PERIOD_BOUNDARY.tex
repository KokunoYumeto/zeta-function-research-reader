\section{The original exponential-curve orders at the inverse-period boundary}
This calculation uses the literal coefficient family PCL1--10 and the
original factor and moment definitions OFM1--11
\cite{PCLOriginal,NC,Cumulative}. The inverse original period is denoted
by $z=1/u$; the exponential-curve variable is denoted by $\xi$.
The value $z=0$ is a coefficient boundary at infinite original period.
It is not a finite original period, and none of the orders below asserts
a finite-period singularity. The finite-period order-two calculation in
the existing PZX1--6 and PZU1--11 is a separate earlier calculation
\cite{PeriodZeroOriginal}.

Retain $0<\delta<1/2$, $\gamma>2$, the ordered roots
$w=(\delta+i\gamma,\delta-i\gamma,-\delta+i\gamma,-\delta-i\gamma)$,
$\rho_a=1/2+w_a$, $V_{ak}=w_a^k$ for $0\le k\le3$, and
\[
 \beta=2(\gamma^2-\delta^2),\quad
 \eta=(\delta^2+\gamma^2)^2,\quad c=\frac{\beta}{12\delta\gamma},
 \quad a=\alpha+i\chi\ne0,\quad
 U=\operatorname{diag}(a,\bar a,\bar a,a).
 \tag{OPB1}
\]
Here $\alpha,\chi$ are real. For each $j=1,2,3,4$ set
$q=q_j=\zeta_5^{-j}$; every occurrence of $q$ below is to be evaluated
at that factor's own fifth root. The original functions are
\[
 \begin{split}
 M_j(z)&=U^{-1}V\mathcal R(z)^{-1}
        \operatorname{diag}(q,q^2,q^3,q^4)\mathcal R(z)V^{-1}U,\\
 t_a(\xi)&=e^{\rho_a\xi},\qquad
 f_j(\xi,z)=Q_0(M_j(z)t(\xi)),\quad Q_0(t)=t_1t_4-t_2t_3,\\
 E_A(\xi,z)&=\prod_{j=1}^4f_j(\xi,z),\qquad
 \mu_m(z)=\partial_\xi^m E_A(0,z).
 \end{split}\tag{OPB2}
\]
These are the original maps, including the full unit $a$.

\subsection{All unit phases at the boundary}
The exact interpolation of the original unit values is
\[
 V^{-1}U\boldsymbol1=
 \left(\alpha+\frac{\beta\chi}{4\delta\gamma},\,0,
              \frac{\chi}{2\delta\gamma},\,0\right)^{\mathsf T}.
 \tag{OPB3}
\]
Indeed $w_1^2=w_4^2=-\beta/2+2i\delta\gamma$ and
$w_2^2=w_3^2=-\beta/2-2i\delta\gamma$. Evaluation of OPB3 at
these four roots gives exactly $(a,\bar a,\bar a,a)$.
Using the complete $J_\infty=\mathcal R(0)$ in PCL4, the two
opposite-root values of $M_j(0)\boldsymbol1$ are respectively
\[
 v_+=\frac{q(\alpha+c\chi)+q^3(-c\chi+i\chi)}{\alpha+i\chi},
 \qquad
 v_-=\frac{q(\alpha+c\chi)+q^3(-c\chi-i\chi)}{\alpha-i\chi}.
 \tag{OPB4}
\]
The four values are $(v_+,v_-,v_-,v_+)$ in their original order.
Subtracting their two products therefore gives
\[
 f_j(0,0)=
 \frac{4i\chi(\alpha+c\chi)(q^3-q)
 [\alpha(\alpha+c\chi)q+\chi(\chi-c\alpha)q^3]}
 {(\alpha^2+\chi^2)^2}.
 \tag{OPB5}
\]
For completeness, the bracket never vanishes. Its two coefficients
$A=\alpha(\alpha+c\chi)$ and $B=\chi(\chi-c\alpha)$ are real;
$q^2$ is nonreal, so $A+Bq^2=0$ would force $A=B=0$.
If $\chi=0$, then $A=\alpha^2>0$. If $\chi\ne0$ and $B=0$,
then $\chi=c\alpha$ and $A=(1+c^2)\alpha^2>0$.
Thus no allowed $(\alpha,\chi)$ makes both coefficients zero.
Also $q^3\ne q$. Consequently all four boundary constants vanish
precisely on the two distinct phase lines
\[
             \chi=0\qquad\hbox{or}\qquad\alpha+c\chi=0.
 \tag{OPB6}
\]
These are exactly the exceptional phase values in the earlier PZ7
\cite{PeriodZeroOriginal}. To verify the identification with that source,
put $X_0=(\delta+i\gamma)^2$, $Y_0=(\delta-i\gamma)^2$,
$T_0=X_0/Y_0$, $A_e=(2T_0-1)/(3(T_0-1))$ and
$B_e=(2-T_0)/(3(T_0-1))$. Then
\[
 \chi=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ a/\bar a=1,\qquad
 \alpha+c\chi=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\
 \frac a{\bar a}=\frac{c-i}{c+i}
       =\frac{2X_0-Y_0}{2Y_0-X_0}=\frac{A_e}{B_e}.
 \tag{OPB6a}
\]
The first equivalence is $a-\bar a=2i\chi$.
For the second, cross multiplication with $a=\alpha+i\chi$ gives
$\alpha=-c\chi$. The middle equality follows from
$X_0=-\beta/2+2i\delta\gamma$ and
$Y_0=-\beta/2-2i\delta\gamma$. All denominators are nonzero,
since $\delta\gamma>0$ and $c>0$.
Away from those lines each $n_j=\operatorname{ord}_{\xi=0}f_j(\xi,0)$
is zero and the total order is $v=0$. The leading moment there is
the product of the four explicit nonzero values OPB5.

\subsection{An exact coefficient calculation retaining the complete curve}
Introduce the multiplication matrix of the centered root in its original
coefficient order, and the original quadratic coefficient matrix,
\[
 T=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&-\eta\\1&0&0&0\\0&1&0&-\beta\\0&0&1&0\end{pmatrix},
 \quad
 Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1&0\\0&-1&0&\beta\\1&0&-\beta&0\\
                    0&\beta&0&\eta-\beta^2\end{pmatrix}.
 \tag{OPB7}
\]
Thus $T^4+\beta T^2+\eta I=0$. Direct evaluation at all four roots gives
\[
 V\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{T^me_0}{m!}\xi^m=(e^{w_a\xi})_a,
 \qquad Q_0(Vp)=4i\delta\gamma\,p^{\mathsf T}Qp.
 \tag{OPB8}
\]
The first identity is the convergent matrix exponential evaluated at
each eigenvalue, with its original coefficient vectors. To verify the
second, write $p(w)=p_0+p_1w+p_2w^2+p_3w^3$ and expand
$p(w)p(-w)$. The coefficients of $w^2,w^4,w^6$ are
$2p_0p_2-p_1^2$, $p_2^2-2p_1p_3$, and $-p_3^2$.
The differences of those powers between $w_1$ and $w_2$ are
$4i\delta\gamma$, $-4i\delta\gamma\beta$, and
$4i\delta\gamma(\beta^2-\eta)$, proving every entry of $Q$.

Write $\mathcal R(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}R_nz^n$ with the literal PCL2
coefficients, and define the coefficients of its entire inverse by
\[
 I_0=J_\infty^{-1},\qquad
 I_n=-J_\infty^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^nR_k I_{n-k},\quad
 G_n=\sum_{k=0}^n I_k\operatorname{diag}(q,q^2,q^3,q^4)R_{n-k}.
 \tag{OPB9}
\]
Multiplication of the two convergent series proves the inverse recursion
and gives every coefficient of the original conjugated matrix.
In particular
\[
 R_1=\begin{pmatrix}
 0&\beta^3/27-\beta\eta/3&0&-11\beta^4/324+\beta^2\eta/3-\eta^2/2\\
 -\beta^2/9+\eta&0&7\beta^3/81-2\beta\eta/3&0\\
 0&-\beta^2/6+\eta&0&4\beta^3/27-\beta\eta\\
 \beta/3&0&-2\beta^2/9+\eta&0
 \end{pmatrix}.
 \tag{OPB10}
\]
All second and later $R_n$ are the explicit finite sums PCL2; they are
not free parameters. For any coefficient matrices $B_n$ used below, put
\[
 \mathcal C_{m,n}(B)=
 \sum_{k=0}^n\sum_{h=0}^m
 \frac{(B_k T^he_0)^{\mathsf T}Q(B_{n-k}T^{m-h}e_0)}{h!(m-h)!}.
 \tag{OPB11}
\]
Equations OPB8--9 prove the full convergent identity
$f_j(\xi,z)=4i\delta\gamma e^\xi
\sum_{m,n\ge0}\mathcal C_{m,n}(B)\xi^mz^n$
when $B_n$ is the appropriate exact coefficient conjugation.
The factor $4i\delta\gamma e^\xi$ is retained in every result below;
neither the original roots nor the original norm is changed.

\subsection{The real-unit phase: order sixteen and its four-factor unfolding}
On $\chi=0$, the nonzero scalar $\alpha$ cancels with its inverse
inside the specified conjugated map OPB2, so $B_n=G_n$ exactly.
The even and odd coefficient blocks of $G_0$ act by
\[
 \begin{split}
 (p_0,p_2)&\longmapsto
 \bigl(qp_0+\tfrac\beta3(q^3-q)p_2,\,q^3p_2\bigr),\\
 (p_1,p_3)&\longmapsto
 \bigl(q^2p_1+\tfrac{2\beta}3(q^4-q^2)p_3,\,q^4p_3\bigr).
 \end{split}\tag{OPB12}
\]
Substituting $e_0,T e_0,T^2e_0/2,T^3e_0/6,T^4e_0/24$
in OPB11 yields
\[
 \mathcal C_{0,0}=\mathcal C_{1,0}=\mathcal C_{2,0}
 =\mathcal C_{3,0}=0,\quad
 A_j:=\mathcal C_{4,0}=\frac{\beta(q^6-q^4)}{36}.
 \tag{OPB13}
\]
One can see the fourth coefficient explicitly without series elimination:
the even-square contribution, divided by $w_1^2-w_2^2$, is
$\beta(-q^6/12-q^4/4)$, and the subtracted odd-square contribution
is $\beta(-q^6/9-2q^4/9)$. Their difference is OPB13.
Since $q^2\ne1$ and $\beta>0$, each $A_j\ne0$. It follows that
\[
 n_1=n_2=n_3=n_4=4,\quad v=16,\quad
 f_j^{(4)}(0,0)=\frac83i\delta\gamma\beta(q^6-q^4),\quad
 \boxed{\mu_{16}(0)=16!\,5\left(\frac{\delta\gamma\beta}{9}\right)^4>0.}
 \tag{OPB14}
\]
Indeed $\prod_{j=1}^4(q_j^6-q_j^4)=5$, since $q_j^6-q_j^4
=q_j^{-1}(q_j^2-1)$, the $q_j^2$ permute the four nontrivial fifth
roots, and $\prod(q_j-1)=5$. Multiplication of the four complete
Taylor series gives the factorial and the leading moment displayed.

The first mixed coefficients, computed from the same original $R_1,R_2$
through OPB9--11, are
\[
 \begin{split}
 C_j:=\mathcal C_{1,1}
 &=\frac{\beta^2q^3(q-1)(2q^2+2q+1)}9,\\
 D_j:=\mathcal C_{0,2}
 &=-\frac{\beta^2q^4(\beta^2-9\eta)(q-1)(q^2+3q+1)}{81}.
 \end{split}\tag{OPB15}
\]
This reproduces the existing real-unit inverse-period coefficient while
also computing its full coupling to the exponential-curve coordinate.
Neither coefficient vanishes: the roots of $2q^2+2q+1$ have modulus
$1/\sqrt2$; the roots of $q^2+3q+1$ are real and negative;
$\beta^2<4\eta<9\eta$; and a nontrivial fifth root is neither real
nor $1$. PCL22--23 with the literal $t(\xi)$ give
\[
 f_j(\xi,-z)=e^{2\xi}f_j(-\xi,z).
 \tag{OPB16}
\]
For the proof, opposite-root permutation sends $t(\xi)$ to
$e^\xi t(-\xi)$, commutes with $U$, and preserves $Q_0$.
Consequently $\mathcal C_{m,n}=0$ when $m+n$ is odd. The complete
local expansion is thus
\[
 f_j(\xi,z)=4i\delta\gamma e^\xi
 \left(A_j\xi^4+C_jz\xi+D_jz^2+
       \sum_{\substack{m,n\ge0\\m+3n>4\\(m,n)\ne(0,2)}}
           \mathcal C_{m,n}(G)\xi^mz^n\right).
 \tag{OPB17}
\]
Every coefficient in this convergent remainder is defined by the finite
formula OPB11; coefficients of odd total degree vanish as just proved.

Here is the exact branch consequence. There is one holomorphic branch
$\xi_j(z)$ and three holomorphic branches on the actual three-sheeted
parameter $z=w^3$ satisfying
\[
 \begin{split}
 \xi_j(z)&=-D_jC_j^{-1}z+O(z^3),\\
 \xi_{j,\ell}(w)&=h_{j,\ell}w+O(w^3),\qquad
 h_{j,\ell}^3=-C_j/A_j,\quad \ell=1,2,3.
 \end{split}\tag{OPB18}
\]
To prove the first claim, substitute $\xi=zh$ into the full analytic
series, divide by $z^2$, and extend at $z=0$. Its value there is
$D_j+C_jh$, with nonzero derivative $C_j$. For the other branches
substitute $\xi=wh,z=w^3$ and divide by $w^4$. The extension at zero
is $A_jh^4+C_jh$; its derivatives at its three nonzero roots equal
$-3C_j\ne0$. The roots are distinct because $-C_j/A_j\ne0$.
Both extended equations are even in their parameter by OPB16, so their
unique holomorphic solutions $h$ are even, proving the stated errors.
For existence and uniqueness, in either equation $F(h,t)=0$ at a simple
root $(h_0,0)$, the map $h\mapsto h-F(h,t)/F_h(h_0,0)$ is, on a
sufficiently small closed disk about $h_0$, a contraction with derivative
of modulus at most $1/2$ and maps that disk into itself. Iteration
converges uniformly for small $t$, yielding the holomorphic solution;
the contraction proves uniqueness. Finally, on a fixed small circle in
$\xi$, the limit function has exactly its order-four zero inside.
Uniform convergence in $z$ preserves the number of zeros by the argument
principle, and the four branches just constructed account for them all.
These are zeros in the exponential-curve variable near the coefficient
boundary, not an assertion that $f_j(0,z)$ vanishes for a finite period.

\subsection{The second phase: order eight with its exact mixed coefficient form}
On $\alpha+c\chi=0$ we necessarily have $\chi\ne0$. The original
unit interpolates as $\chi(w^2+\beta/3)/(2\delta\gamma)$.
Define its coefficient multiplication matrix
\[
 W=T^2+\tfrac\beta3 I=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \beta/3&0&-\eta&0\\0&\beta/3&0&-\eta\\
 1&0&-2\beta/3&0\\0&1&0&-2\beta/3
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad H_n=W^{-1}G_nW.
 \tag{OPB19}
\]
The cancelled scalar is exactly $\chi/(2\delta\gamma)$ in the
original conjugation and its inverse, with no other unit change.
The eigenvalue product of $W$ in each opposite-root pair is
$N=\eta-2\beta^2/9>\eta/9>0$, so $\det W=N^2$ and $W$ is
invertible. Equations OPB8--11 apply with $B_n=H_n$.
Put
\[
 X=\beta^4-27\beta^2\eta+81\eta^2,\quad
 Y=3\beta^4-27\beta^2\eta+81\eta^2,\quad
 K=2\beta^2-9\eta.
 \tag{OPB20}
\]
The boundary coefficients are
\[
 \mathcal C_{0,0}(H)=\mathcal C_{1,0}(H)=0,\qquad
 A'_j:=\mathcal C_{2,0}(H)
 =-\frac{q^4(q^2-1)(Xq^2+Y)}{K^2}.
 \tag{OPB21}
\]
Here is a direct coefficient verification. Set $s=w^2$, so
$s^2+\beta s+\eta=0$, and $h=\beta/3$. The first three
transformed exponential derivatives, divided by the original unit, are
\[
 b_0=q^3,\quad
 b_1=w\left[q^4+\frac{h(q^4-q^2)}{s+h}\right],\quad
 b_2=\frac{q(2h^2-\eta)}{s+h}-2hq^3.
 \tag{OPB22}
\]
Since $(s+h)^{-1}=(-s-2h)/N$, reduce
$q^3b_2-b_1^2$ using $s^2=-\beta s-\eta$.
The coefficient of $s$ is exactly $A'_j$ in OPB21; subtracting its
two values multiplies by $s_+-s_-=4i\delta\gamma$, which restores
the original factor. The numerator cannot vanish: $q^2$ is nonreal,
$X,Y$ are real, and $Y-X=2\beta^4>0$, so $Xq^2+Y\ne0$.
The other displayed factors and the denominator are also nonzero.
Consequently the complete orders and leading moment are
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 n_1=n_2=n_3=n_4=2,\quad v=8,\qquad
 f_j^{(2)}(0,0)=8i\delta\gamma A'_j,\\
 \boxed{\mu_8(0)=8!\,
 \frac{5(4\delta\gamma)^4
 (Y^4-XY^3+X^2Y^2-X^3Y+X^4)}{K^8}>0.}
 \end{gathered}\tag{OPB23}
\]
Multiplication over all four fifth roots gives
$\prod(q^2-1)=5$ and
$\prod(Xq^2+Y)=Y^4-XY^3+X^2Y^2-X^3Y+X^4$.
The latter is a product of two strictly positive conjugate-pair
absolute squares by the already proved nonvanishing. This proves the
sign and the factorial in OPB23 without discarding any factor.

For full reproducibility the mixed coefficients at this phase are
\[
 \begin{split}
 C'_j&=\frac{q^4(q-1)}{54K^2}\left[
 2\beta^6(6q^3+q^2+15q+13)
 -\beta^4\eta(432q^3+189q^2+405q+369)\right.\\
 &\hspace{42mm}\left.
 +(1944\beta^2\eta^2-4374\eta^3)(2q^3+q^2+q+1)\right],\\
 D'_j&=-\frac{q^4(\beta^2-9\eta)(q-1)}{1458K^2}\left[
 2\beta^6(9q^3-6q^2+25q+17)
 -27\beta^4\eta(24q^3-3q^2+19q+15)\right.\\
 &\hspace{42mm}\left.
 +(1944\beta^2\eta^2-4374\eta^3)(3q^3+q+1)\right].
 \end{split}\tag{OPB24}
\]
These are respectively $\mathcal C_{1,1}(H)$ and
$\mathcal C_{0,2}(H)$, obtained by substituting OPB9--10 and OPB19
into the finite formula OPB11. Thus the original function is exactly
\[
 f_j(\xi,z)=4i\delta\gamma e^\xi
 \left(A'_j\xi^2+C'_jz\xi+D'_jz^2+
       \sum_{m+n\ge4}\mathcal C_{m,n}(H)\xi^mz^n\right).
 \tag{OPB25}
\]
The sum is convergent with all odd total degrees zero by OPB16.
OPB5, OPB14 and OPB23 exhaust every allowed original unit phase at
this coefficient boundary. They do not replace the actual period
moments at $z\ne0$ by their boundary values.

\subsection{Independent check of the four-factor coefficient entering the inverse}
This last calculation verifies the coefficient algebra used for the
fixed original cutoff; it does not change either weighted space.
On the real-unit phase put
\[
 L=\frac{125\beta^8(\beta^2-9\eta)^4}{81^4},\qquad
 t_0=\frac9{\beta^2-9\eta}.
 \tag{OPB26}
\]
For $k=0,1,2,3$, direct multiplication of the four explicit factors
in OPB15 gives
\[
 [X^k]\prod_{j=1}^4(D_j+C_jX)
       =L(1,-2t_0,2t_0^2,-t_0^3)_k.
 \tag{OPB27}
\]
This identity follows in $\mathbb Q(\beta,\eta)[q]/
(q^4+q^3+q^2+q+1)$ by substituting $q,q^2,q^3,q^4$ in
OPB15 and multiplying. Every coefficient is explicitly determined
by that finite product, so no amplitude assumption enters.
The reciprocal coefficients through degree three are
$(1,2t_0,2t_0^2,t_0^3)$: multiplying the two degree-three
polynomials gives $1$ in degrees zero through three. Finally
\[
 H(t)=\begin{pmatrix}
 1&2t&2t^2&t^3\\0&1&2t&2t^2\\0&0&1&2t\\0&0&0&1
 \end{pmatrix},\quad
 \det H(t)_{\{0,\ldots,k-1\},\{4-k,\ldots,3\}}
       =(t^3,2t^4,t^3,1)_k.
 \tag{OPB28}
\]
Expansion of the four displayed corner determinants proves OPB28.
The original substitution $\omega=-i\arctan t$, every Gamma ratio,
and every metric must be retained when applying these coefficient
identities to the original weighted inverse.
