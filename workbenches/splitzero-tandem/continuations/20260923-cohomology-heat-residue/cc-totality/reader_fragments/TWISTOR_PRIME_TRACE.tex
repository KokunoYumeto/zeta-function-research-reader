\section{Exact prime traces and determinants of the odd twistor receiver}
\label{sec:twistor-prime-trace}

The input dynamics is due to Alain Connes and Caterina Consani,
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00299v1}{\emph{The Absolute Twistor Line
and the Geometry of $\overline{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z}$}},
Proposition \texttt{prop:twistor\_frobenius}; its original author file
\texttt{CC.tex}, lines 1159--1191, gives the two odd residue classes
and their compatibility with the real structure. The source's spatial
coproduct restriction is at lines 462--465. The maps used here are the
explicit receiving maps (TOB1)--(TOB11), constructed from the original
companion in
\href{https://github.com/KokunoYumeto/zeta-function-research-reader/blob/3c78cfbd9e194d35117b33277c421c6564ce3dbf/workbenches/splitzero-tandem/continuations/20260923-full-prime-support/033/MIXED_PRIME_ENERGY_AND_BOUNDARY.tex}{033,
SBT1--SBT23}. The calculation concerns one fixed prime $p$. No product
over primes is introduced.

\subsection{The complete cyclic receiver and its labelled traces}

Retain $k,\delta,\gamma,q,N,E,G_N$ and all original Lagrange labels
$(a,b)$ from (TOB6)--(TOB7). In particular
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT1}
 \omega_{ab}=p^{(2a-k)\delta}e^{i(2b-k)\gamma\log p},
 \qquad 0\le a,b\le k,\qquad q=(k+1)^2.
\end{equation}
For a positive odd integer $d$, put $\chi=\chi_d=(-1)^{(d-1)/2}$,
$A=\chi F_p^\chi$, and
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT2}
 C=C_d:E^{\oplus d}\longrightarrow E^{\oplus d},\quad
 C(v_0,\ldots,v_{d-1})=(Av_{d-1},v_0,\ldots,v_{d-2}),
 \qquad \widetilde C=p^{k/2}C.
\end{equation}
The metric is the unchanged direct sum of $d$ copies of $G_N$.
Trace and determinant below are the ordinary trace and determinant
of these actual finite-dimensional complex-linear operators; their
definitions do not replace this metric or its defects.
Let $P_{ab}^{(d)}=\operatorname{diag}(P_{ab},\ldots,P_{ab})$.

For every integer $n$, including negative integers, the exact formula is
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT3}
 \operatorname{tr}(C^n)=
 \begin{cases}
 0,&d\nmid n,\\
 d\chi^m\displaystyle\sum_{a,b=0}^k\omega_{ab}^{\chi m},&n=dm.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For $n=0$ the second line means $dq$. The stronger labelled identity is
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT4}
 \operatorname{tr}(P_{ab}^{(d)}C^n)=
 \begin{cases}0,&d\nmid n,\\d\chi^m\omega_{ab}^{\chi m},&n=dm.
 \end{cases}
 \qquad
 \operatorname{tr}(P_{ab}^{(d)}\widetilde C^n)
   =p^{kn/2}\operatorname{tr}(P_{ab}^{(d)}C^n).
\end{equation}
To prove these statements, let $\iota_r:E\to E^{\oplus d}$ be the
inclusion into block $r$ and let $\pi_r$ be its projection. Iteration
of (TPT2) sends every block to the block whose index is increased by
$n$ modulo $d$. If $d\nmid n$, no diagonal block is present, so the
trace is zero. If $n=dm$, the exact identity
$C^d=\operatorname{diag}(A,\ldots,A)$ gives $A^m$ in every diagonal
block. This also holds for negative $m$, because $A$ and $C$ are
invertible. Finally $P_{ab}A^m=\chi^m\omega_{ab}^{\chi m}P_{ab}$
and $\operatorname{tr}P_{ab}=1$. This proves (TPT3)--(TPT4) without
an orthogonality assumption on the Lagrange basis.

For later comparison define the original, fully labelled power sum
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT5}
 t_p(m)=\operatorname{tr}(F_p^m)
   =\sum_{a=0}^k\sum_{b=0}^k
       p^{m(2a-k)\delta}e^{im(2b-k)\gamma\log p}
       \qquad(m\in\mathbb Z).
\end{equation}
Thus the surviving trace at time $n=dm$ samples original exponent
$\chi m=\chi n/d$, not exponent $n$. Its multiplicity is exactly $d$.
For the weighted receiver the same trace is
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT6}
 \operatorname{tr}(\widetilde C^{dm})
       =d\chi^m p^{kdm/2}t_p(\chi m)
       =d\chi^m p^{k(d-\chi)m/2}
                         \operatorname{tr}(\widetilde F_p^{\chi m}),
 \qquad \widetilde F_p=p^{k/2}F_p.
\end{equation}
All signs, original phases, and both powers of $p$ are retained.

\subsection{Characteristic polynomial and the two local determinants}

The characteristic polynomials and local determinant polynomials are
\begin{align}
 \det(zI-C)&=\det(z^dI-A)
      =\prod_{a,b=0}^k(z^d-\chi\omega_{ab}^{\chi}),\tag{TPT7}\\
 \det(zI-\widetilde C)&=
      \prod_{a,b=0}^k(z^d-\chi p^{kd/2}\omega_{ab}^{\chi}),\tag{TPT8}\\
 D_{p,d}(u):=\det(I-uC)&=
      \prod_{a,b=0}^k(1-\chi\omega_{ab}^{\chi}u^d),\tag{TPT9}\\
 \widetilde D_{p,d}(u):=\det(I-u\widetilde C)&=
      \prod_{a,b=0}^k(1-\chi p^{kd/2}\omega_{ab}^{\chi}u^d).
      \tag{TPT10}
\end{align}
Indeed $P_{ab}^{(d)}E^{\oplus d}$ is an invariant $d$-dimensional
space, and $C$ on it is the cyclic companion with scalar corner
$\chi\omega_{ab}^{\chi}$. Its eigenvalues are all $d$ roots of that
nonzero scalar, with the labelled eigenvectors of (TOB16). The roots
within this block are distinct. The original projectors decompose
the full space as the direct sum of these invariant blocks, so the
products above include the correct multiplicities even when roots
from different original labels coincide. Multiplication by $p^{k/2}$
gives (TPT8); putting the eigenvalues in $\det(I-uC)$ gives
(TPT9)--(TPT10).

Write, with the indicated original operator retained,
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT11}
 D_p^{\pm}(v)=\det(I-vF_p^{\pm1}),\qquad
 \widetilde D_p^{\pm}(v)=\det(I-v\widetilde F_p^{\pm1}).
\end{equation}
Here $D_p^+$ is the original $\det(I-vF_p)$, and $D_p^-$ is its
inverse-operator comparison. Equations (TPT9)--(TPT10) give the exact
substitutions
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT12}
 \begin{split}
 D_{p,d}(u)&=D_p^{\chi}(\chi u^d),\\
 \widetilde D_{p,d}(u)
   &=D_p^{\chi}(\chi p^{kd/2}u^d)
    =\widetilde D_p^{\chi}(\chi p^{k(d-\chi)/2}u^d).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
For $d\equiv1\pmod4$, the argument of $\widetilde D_p^+$ is
$p^{k(d-1)/2}u^d$. For $d\equiv3\pmod4$, the argument of
$\widetilde D_p^-$ is $-p^{k(d+1)/2}u^d$.

There is also an exact relation between the two original labelled
spectra. Define the involution $R:E\to E$ on the original basis by
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT13}
 R\ell_{ab}=\ell_{k-a,k-b},\quad R^2=I,\qquad
 \omega_{k-a,k-b}=\omega_{ab}^{-1},\quad
 RF_pR=F_p^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The scalar identity follows directly from (TPT1). Applying both
operators in the last identity to each $\ell_{ab}$ proves it.
Consequently $D_p^-=D_p^+$ and $t_p(-m)=t_p(m)$ as scalar invariants.
These equalities are mediated by the explicit label permutation $R$;
they do not identify an original labelled eigenline with its reflected
one, and they do not assert that $R$ is unitary for $G_N$.
The weighted counterpart is
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT14}
 R\widetilde F_pR=p^k\widetilde F_p^{-1},\qquad
 \widetilde D_p^-(v)=\widetilde D_p^+(p^{-k}v).
\end{equation}
This follows by multiplying (TPT13) by $p^{k/2}$; the displayed
$p^k$ must not be dropped.

For completeness, the product of all original eigenvalues is $1$:
the sums of $2a-k$ and $2b-k$ over their full ranges are zero in
(TPT1). Since $q=(k+1)^2$ is even and $d$ is odd,
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT15}
 \det F_p=1,\qquad \det C=\det A=1,\qquad
 \det\widetilde C=p^{kdq/2},\qquad
 D_p^+(v)=v^qD_p^+(v^{-1})\quad(v\ne0).
\end{equation}
The determinant of a $d$-block cyclic companion is
$(-1)^{(d-1)q}\det A$, which here equals $\det A$.
For the final equality, multiply
$\prod_{a,b}(1-v\omega_{ab})$ by writing each factor as
$-v\omega_{ab}(1-v^{-1}\omega_{ab}^{-1})$, and use (TPT13).

\subsection{Formal logarithmic series and their precise convergence}

In the formal power-series ring $\mathbb C[[u]]$, the logarithm of a
series with constant coefficient $1$ is defined by its zero constant
coefficient and the usual formal derivative. Thus no analytic branch
is needed for the following identities:
\begin{align}
 -\log D_{p,d}(u)
   &=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\chi^m t_p(\chi m)}{m}u^{dm},\tag{TPT16}\\
 -\log\widetilde D_{p,d}(u)
   &=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\chi^m p^{kdm/2}t_p(\chi m)}{m}u^{dm},
       \tag{TPT17}\\
 -u\frac{D'_{p,d}(u)}{D_{p,d}(u)}
   &=d\sum_{m\ge1}\chi^m t_p(\chi m)u^{dm}
     =\sum_{a,b=0}^k\frac{d\chi\omega_{ab}^{\chi}u^d}
                               {1-\chi\omega_{ab}^{\chi}u^d},
       \tag{TPT18}\\
 -u\frac{\widetilde D'_{p,d}(u)}{\widetilde D_{p,d}(u)}
   &=d\sum_{m\ge1}\chi^m p^{kdm/2}t_p(\chi m)u^{dm}
     =\sum_{a,b=0}^k
       \frac{d\chi p^{kd/2}\omega_{ab}^{\chi}u^d}
            {1-\chi p^{kd/2}\omega_{ab}^{\chi}u^d}.
       \tag{TPT19}
\end{align}
To prove them, expand each factor in (TPT9)--(TPT10) with
$-\log(1-x)=\sum_{m\ge1}x^m/m$, a valid formal identity, and
then differentiate term by term. Equivalently, for either receiver
$B=C$ or $B=\widetilde C$,
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT20}
 \begin{gathered}
 -\log\det(I-uB)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\operatorname{tr}(B^n)}n u^n,
 \\
 -\frac{d}{du}\log\det(I-uB)
     =\operatorname{tr}\bigl(B(I-uB)^{-1}\bigr)
     =\sum_{n\ge1}\operatorname{tr}(B^n)u^{n-1}.
 \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Substitution of (TPT3) or (TPT6) into (TPT20) retains both the
$d$-fold trace multiplicity and the factor $1/(dm)$; their product is
the exact coefficient $1/m$ in (TPT16)--(TPT17).

The exact radii of these Taylor series about zero are
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT21}
 \mathcal R_C=p^{-k\delta/d},\qquad
 \mathcal R_{\widetilde C}=p^{-k/2-k\delta/d}.
\end{equation}
Both logarithmic series and logarithmic-derivative series converge
absolutely on the corresponding open disk. On its boundary the
logarithmic series converges conditionally precisely at the points
which are not zeros of the determinant; at its zeros it diverges.
The logarithmic-derivative Taylor series diverges at every point of
that boundary. Neither series converges outside the closed disk.

Here is a proof including boundary behaviour. The eigenvalues of $C$
are those of (TOB16), so their largest modulus is $p^{k\delta/d}$;
the labels $a=0,k$ ensure that this maximum is attained. Multiplication
by $p^{k/2}$ gives the second radius. For a fixed receiver $B$, list its
eigenvalues with algebraic multiplicity as $\beta_j$.
For $|u|<\rho(B)^{-1}$ every $|u\beta_j|<1$, giving absolute
convergence by a finite sum of scalar geometric or logarithmic series.
On $|u|=\rho(B)^{-1}$, the eigenvalues of smaller modulus still give
absolutely convergent series. Each eigenvalue of maximal modulus gives
$\sum_{n\ge1}(u\beta_j)^n/n$. If $u\beta_j\ne1$, its partial sums
are bounded geometric sums and summation by parts proves convergence.
If $u\beta_j=1$, it contributes the harmonic series with positive
multiplicity; all other terms converge, so it cannot be cancelled.
This proves the claimed convergence and divergence for the logarithm.

To show that the convergence at the other boundary points is not
absolute, group equal maximal phases into distinct unit complex numbers
$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s$ with positive integer multiplicities
$c_1,\ldots,c_s$. Put $b_n=\sum_jc_j\alpha_j^n$.
Finite geometric summation gives
\[
 \lim_{M\to\infty}\frac1M\sum_{n=1}^M|b_n|^2
        =\sum_{j=1}^s c_j^2>0.
\]
Since $|b_n|\le\sum_jc_j$, the average of $|b_n|$ has a positive
lower bound. The smaller-modulus contributions tend to zero
exponentially and do not change that bound. Summation by parts then
shows that the series of absolute values divided by $n$ diverges.
The same positive mean square shows that the terms of the
logarithmic-derivative series fail to tend to zero on the boundary.
If $|u|>\rho(B)^{-1}$, factor the dominant growth
$(|u|\rho(B))^n$ out of its coefficients. The same finite-phase
mean-square argument provides a subsequence bounded away from zero;
exponentially smaller spectral terms cannot cancel it. Along this
subsequence even division by $n$ does not make the terms tend to zero.
This proves the asserted exterior divergence.
Finally every nearest determinant zero gives a genuine pole of
$-D'/D$ whose residue is minus its positive multiplicity. Thus
(TPT21) is also the exact analytic Taylor radius, without a possible
pole cancellation.

\subsection{The exact prime variable and reverse recovery}

Only for this fixed prime define
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT22}
 Z_{p,d}(s)=\widetilde D_{p,d}(p^{-s})^{-1},
 \qquad p^{-s}=e^{-s\log p}.
\end{equation}
It is a meromorphic function of $s$. In the precise open half-plane
$\operatorname{Re}s>k/2+k\delta/d$, its branch with the indicated
convergent series satisfies
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT23}
 \begin{split}
 \log Z_{p,d}(s)
   &=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\chi^m t_p(\chi m)}m
                                  p^{-dm(s-k/2)},\\
 -\frac{Z'_{p,d}(s)}{Z_{p,d}(s)}
   &=d\log p\sum_{m\ge1}\chi^m t_p(\chi m)
                                  p^{-dm(s-k/2)}.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
The second identity follows by the chain rule from (TPT19), including
the sign $d(p^{-s})/ds=-(\log p)p^{-s}$.
The original prime powers which appear are indexed by $dm$, the
retained mass is $p^{kdm/2}$, and the derivative contributes $d\log p$.
The support of these prime-power indices is not replaced by all
positive integers.

Put $b_d=(1-\chi)/2$. Every zero of the denominator in (TPT22), with
its original label retained, is
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT24}
 s_{ab,\ell}=\frac{k}{2}+\frac{\chi(2a-k)\delta}{d}
  +i\left(\frac{\chi(2b-k)\gamma}{d}
          +\frac{\pi b_d+2\pi\ell}{d\log p}\right),
 \qquad \ell\in\mathbb Z.
\end{equation}
Indeed substituting this expression into
$\chi\omega_{ab}^{\chi}p^{-d(s-k/2)}=1$ proves the equality;
conversely every solution differs by one indicated logarithmic
period. If labels produce the same point, their positive
multiplicities add. These are poles of the specified finite local
function $Z_{p,d}$, not a statement about a global zeta function.

The trace information has an exact reverse map. For every integer $m$,
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT25}
 t_p(\chi m)=\frac{\chi^m}{d}\operatorname{tr}(C^{dm})
    =\frac{\chi^m p^{-kdm/2}}d
                               \operatorname{tr}(\widetilde C^{dm}).
\end{equation}
For a specified original projector, even its eigenvalue is recovered by
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT26}
 \omega_{ab}=
   \left(\frac{\chi}{d}\operatorname{tr}(P_{ab}^{(d)}C^d)\right)^\chi
 =\left(\frac{\chi p^{-kd/2}}d
              \operatorname{tr}(P_{ab}^{(d)}\widetilde C^d)\right)^\chi.
\end{equation}
These follow directly from (TPT3)--(TPT4), using $\chi^2=1$ and the
nonzero original eigenvalues.

Even without the labelled projectors, the first $q$ nonzero-time trace
slots determine the characteristic polynomial of $A$. Set
$a_m=d^{-1}\operatorname{tr}(C^{dm})$ and recursively define
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT27}
 e_0=1,\qquad
 j e_j=\sum_{m=1}^j(-1)^{m-1}e_{j-m}a_m
       \quad(1\le j\le q).
\end{equation}
Then $\det(I-vA)=\sum_{j=0}^q(-1)^je_jv^j$.
For proof, the identity
$-vD_A'(v)/D_A(v)=\sum_{m\ge1}a_mv^m$ follows from its finite
eigenvalue product. Multiplying by $D_A$ and comparing coefficients
gives exactly (TPT27), which recursively determines all coefficients.
The eigenvalues of $A=\chi F_p^\chi$ then recover the original
eigenvalue multiset by $\omega=(\chi\alpha)^\chi$.
Trace data alone do not recover an ordering of its labels or the
original metric $G_N$; (TPT26) uses the actual retained projectors
when such labelled recovery is intended.

Finally the exact operator recovery, stronger than spectral recovery,
uses the original block inclusions and projections:
\begin{equation}\tag{TPT28}
 \begin{aligned}
 \pi_r C^d\iota_r&=A,&
 \pi_r\widetilde C^d\iota_r&=p^{kd/2}A,\\
 F_p&=\chi(\pi_r C^d\iota_r)^\chi,&
 \widetilde F_p
   &=p^{k/2}\chi
         (p^{-kd/2}\pi_r\widetilde C^d\iota_r)^\chi
          \qquad(0\le r<d).
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Substituting $C^d=\operatorname{diag}(A,\ldots,A)$ proves every
equality. The inclusions remain isometries for the original block
metric, and the projections remain their adjoints. Thus the
$1/d$ appearing in the labelled radii (TPT24) has exact return maps:
it retains the original operator, its inverse twist, all mass
factors, all labelled traces, and the metric defects of (TOB13).
The finite local determinant calculation therefore does not discard
the complete return while retaining only its contracted root radii.
