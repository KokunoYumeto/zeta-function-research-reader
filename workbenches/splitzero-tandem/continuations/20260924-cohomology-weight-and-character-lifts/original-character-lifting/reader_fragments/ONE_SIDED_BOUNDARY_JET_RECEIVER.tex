\subsection{The full one-sided boundary calculation}

Retain $L>0$ and $f,g\in C^\infty([0,L],\mathbb C)$. Extend them by zero
outside this interval for convolution only. For $0\le y\le L$ set
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ1}
 C_{f,g}(y)=\int_0^{L-y}
 \bigl(\overline{f(x)}g(x+y)+\overline{f(x+y)}g(x)\bigr)\,dx,
 \qquad Q_{\mathcal D}(f,g)=\mathcal D(C_{f,g}).
\end{equation}
This is precisely $(f^**g)(y)+(f^**g)(-y)$ for the source involution
$f^*(x)=\overline{f(-x)}$. Substitution $x\mapsto x+y$ in the first
convolution proves the identity; both integrals are over their actual
overlap interval. The function $C_{f,g}$ is smooth from the right at zero.
For the one-sided distribution use the unaltered convention
$\delta_0^{(n)}(h)=(-1)^n h^{(n)}(0+)$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ2}
 Q_{\delta_0}(f,g)=2\int_0^L\overline f g,\qquad
 Q_{\delta'_0}(f,g)=\overline{f(0)}g(0)+\overline{f(L)}g(L).
\end{equation}
The factor two in the first formula and the two distinct endpoint
values in the second are retained.

Here is the all-order formula, with no periodic endpoint identification:
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ3}
 Q_{\delta_0^{(2r+1)}}(f,g)
 =\sum_{x\in\{0,L\}}\sum_{j=0}^{2r}(-1)^j
       \overline{f^{(2r-j)}(x)}g^{(j)}(x),\qquad r\ge0.
\end{equation}
To prove it, let
$A_{f,g}(y)=\int_y^L\overline{f(x-y)}g(x)\,dx$. Differentiation at the
moving lower endpoint gives
$A'_{f,g}(y)=-\overline{f(0)}g(y)-A_{f',g}(y)$. Induction yields
\[
 A_{f,g}^{(n)}(0+)=(-1)^n\int_0^L\overline{f^{(n)}}g
 +\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{j+1}\overline{f^{(j)}(0)}g^{(n-1-j)}(0).
\]
The other half of $C_{f,g}$ is $\overline{A_{g,f}}$. For odd
$n=2r+1$, repeated integration by parts gives
\[
 \int_0^L(\overline{f^{(n)}}g+\overline f g^{(n)})
 =\left[\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^j
       \overline{f^{(n-1-j)}}g^{(j)}\right]_0^L.
\]
The two lower-endpoint sums from $A$ and its conjugate each contribute
minus the bracket at zero. Consequently $C_{f,g}^{(n)}(0+)$ is minus
the sum of the brackets at zero and $L$. Multiplication by $(-1)^n=-1$
proves (OBJ3), including (OBJ2). This also proves directly that each
form in (OBJ3) is Hermitian: replacing $j$ by $2r-j$ conjugates the
formula without changing its sign.

On genuinely smooth functions $h$ defined across zero,
$(h(y)+h(-y))^{(2r+1)}|_{y=0}=0$. Thus all of these odd distributions
are erased by ordinary smooth symmetrization. Formula (OBJ3)
identifies exactly the boundary data that survives their action on
the source's piecewise smooth correlations.

\subsection{The exact jet space, signature, and completion}

Put $m=2r+1$ and write the complete trace map
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ4}
 J_r:C^\infty([0,L])\longrightarrow\mathbb C^m\oplus\mathbb C^m,
 \qquad J_rf=((f^{(j)}(0))_{j=0}^{2r},(f^{(j)}(L))_{j=0}^{2r}).
\end{equation}
Let $A_r$ have entries $(A_r)_{ij}=(-1)^j$ when $i+j=2r$, and zero
otherwise, for indices $0\le i,j\le2r$. It is a real symmetric
involution. Formula (OBJ3) becomes the exact pullback
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ5}
 Q_{\delta_0^{(2r+1)}}(f,g)
       =(J_rf)^*\operatorname{diag}(A_r,A_r)J_rg.
\end{equation}
The map $J_r$ is onto. Indeed, on polynomials of degree at most
$2m-1$, zero jets at both endpoints mean divisibility by
$x^m(x-L)^m$, a polynomial of degree $2m$. The polynomial must be
zero. Domain and codomain both have dimension $2m$, so this restricted
map is bijective. This supplies a unique polynomial right inverse
with all original derivative values, without rescaling its coordinates.

The paired coordinates $j,2r-j$ for $j<r$ contribute one positive
and one negative eigenvalue each. The middle coordinate has eigenvalue
$(-1)^r$. The boundary form on the quotient by $\ker J_r$ therefore
has the exact inertia
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ6}
 \begin{cases}(2r+2,2r,0),&r\text{ even},\\
               (2r,2r+2,0),&r\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Its algebraic radical on $C^\infty([0,L])$ is exactly $\ker J_r$:
surjectivity of $J_r$ and invertibility of both $A_r$ show that a
vector pairing to zero with every test has zero jet. Thus the higher
odd derivatives provide nontrivial indefinite forms, even though
the first odd derivative is positive.

They also specify a concrete boundary completion. Retain the norm
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ7}
 \|f\|_{\mathrm{jet},r}^2=\|f\|_{L^2(0,L)}^2+\|J_rf\|_{\mathbb C^{2m}}^2.
\end{equation}
The completion of this exact norm is
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ8}
 \mathcal H_{\mathrm{jet},r}
       =L^2(0,L)\oplus\mathbb C^{2m},
 \qquad f\longmapsto(f,J_rf),
\end{equation}
with the displayed product norm. For a proof of density, choose a
one-sided smooth cutoff $\chi\in C^\infty([0,\infty))$ supported
in $[0,1)$ that equals one near zero.
For prescribed endpoint jets $(a_j)$, the functions
$\chi(x/\varepsilon)\sum_{j<m}a_jx^j/j!$ have exactly those left
jets and have $L^2$ norm tending to zero. At the right endpoint use
$\chi((L-x)/\varepsilon)\sum_{j<m}b_j(x-L)^j/j!$. Their supports
are disjoint when $2\varepsilon<L$, and all opposite jets vanish.
Approximate an arbitrary $L^2$ function by interior compact smooth
functions, add these two functions, and let $\varepsilon$ tend to zero.
This approximates any $(h,a,b)$ in the product norm, proving (OBJ8).
The map forgetting the finite boundary vector is a continuous
surjection, with kernel exactly $0\oplus\mathbb C^{2m}$. Equation
(OBJ5) extends to this completion as the bounded finite boundary form
with the same matrix; none of its boundary coordinates is discarded.

Already $Q_{\delta'_0}$ is not closable as a quadratic form on the
original $L^2(0,L)$ with smooth domain. In fact
$f_\varepsilon(x)=\chi(x/\varepsilon)$ tends to zero in $L^2$,
$Q_{\delta'_0}(f_\varepsilon-f_\eta)=0$, but
$Q_{\delta'_0}(f_\varepsilon)=1$. These are the exact three conditions
contradicting closability. Adding a fixed multiple of the $L^2$ inner
product leaves this countersequence unchanged in the limit.
This is an obstruction for this specific boundary form; it makes no
claim about the closability of a different full arithmetic form.

In the source's original trigonometric basis
$U_n(x)=L^{-1/2}e^{2\pi inx/L}$, the $r=0$ matrix is
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ9}
 (Q_{\delta'_0}(U_i,U_j))_{i,j\in\mathbb Z}
             =(2/L)_{i,j\in\mathbb Z}.
\end{equation}
For $u_N(x)=\frac1{2N+1}\sum_{n=-N}^N e^{2\pi inx/L}$,
$u_N(0)=u_N(L)=1$ and $\|u_N\|_2^2=L/(2N+1)$.
Hence the same nonclosability occurs on precisely that core:
$Q(u_N)=2$ and $Q(u_N-u_M)=0$. Its finite matrices have one positive
eigenvalue $2(2N+1)/L$ and $2N$ zero eigenvalues. The boundary form
alone does not have the simple isolated bottom eigenvector required
by Connes--van Suijlekom's real-zero theorem. Equations (OBJ5)--(OBJ8)
construct the space retained by this obstruction.

\subsection{Transport to the original Mellin endpoints, at every odd order}

Return to the unchanged real coordinate $v$. Fix any $a<b$,
$L=b-a$, and $f\in C_c^\infty((a,b),\mathbb C)$. Write
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ10}
 M_f(s)=\int_{\mathbb R} f(v)e^{-(s-1/2)v}\,dv,
 \quad A=M_f(0),\quad B=M_f(1),\quad
 u(v)=-\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-|v-w|/2}f(w)\,dw.
\end{equation}
The operator $T=\partial_v^2-1/4$ satisfies $Tu=f$:
the kernel $-e^{-|v|/2}$ is continuous and its first derivative has
jump one at zero, so its distributional image under $T$ is $\delta_0$.
Smoothness follows by transferring derivatives to $f$.
For $v$ to the left or right of its support, respectively, the exact
exterior formula is
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ11}
 u(v)=-e^{v/2}B\quad(v\le a),\qquad
 u(v)=-e^{-v/2}A\quad(v\ge b).
\end{equation}
Because the support lies strictly inside $(a,b)$ these identities
hold in neighborhoods of the endpoints. Thus for every $j\ge0$
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ12}
 u^{(j)}(a)=-2^{-j}e^{a/2}B,\qquad
 u^{(j)}(b)=-(-2)^{-j}e^{-b/2}A.
\end{equation}
Here $(-2)^{-j}=(-1/2)^j$; neither the original Mellin factors nor
the length $L$ has been changed.

Use the explicit translation $u_a(x)=u(a+x)$ on $[0,L]$ only to
apply (OBJ1); it is a named linear map, not a change to the original
arithmetic test $f$. The endpoint comparison is the invertible
matrix
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ13}
 \begin{pmatrix}u(a)\\u(b)\end{pmatrix}
  =B_{a,b}\begin{pmatrix}A\\B\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 B_{a,b}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-e^{a/2}\\-e^{-b/2}&0\end{pmatrix},
 \quad \det B_{a,b}=-e^{(a-b)/2}\ne0.
\end{equation}
Equations (OBJ3) and (OBJ12) calculate the whole odd-jet family:
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ14}
 \boxed{\quad Q_{\delta_0^{(2r+1)}}(u_a,u_a)
 =4^{-r}\bigl(e^a|M_f(1)|^2+e^{-b}|M_f(0)|^2\bigr)
 \quad(r\ge0).\quad}
\end{equation}
For each endpoint, the product of derivatives of total order $2r$
is $4^{-r}$ times the squared endpoint value; at the right endpoint
the two derivative signs multiply to $(-1)^{2r}=1$. The remaining
sum is $\sum_{j=0}^{2r}(-1)^j=1$. This proves the formula at every
order, rather than inferring the higher orders from the first one.
The polarized version follows by retaining $\overline{M_f}M_g$.

Consequently all odd one-sided jets on these actual Green images
factor through the same two-dimensional endpoint space. Their common
kernel is precisely
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ15}
 \{f\in C_c^\infty((a,b)):M_f(0)=M_f(1)=0\}
 =T C_c^\infty((a,b)).
\end{equation}
The inclusion from right to left is integration by parts:
$M_{Tg}(s)=s(s-1)M_g(s)$. Conversely (OBJ11) shows that $u$ is
compactly supported inside $(a,b)$ when both endpoints vanish, and
$Tu=f$. A compactly supported solution of $Th=0$ is zero because
the only solutions are linear combinations of $e^{v/2}$ and
$e^{-v/2}$. This proves uniqueness and the equality in (OBJ15).

For completeness the endpoint map is onto on this fixed interval.
Choose $\beta\in C_c^\infty((a,b))$ nonnegative and positive on an
interval, set $h_0(v)=e^{v/2}$, $h_1(v)=e^{-v/2}$, and retain
$C_{ij}=\int\beta h_i h_j$. Its real quadratic form is strictly
positive because $h_0,h_1$ are linearly independent on every interval.
The explicit right inverse is
$R(w)=\beta\sum_j(C^{-1}w)_j h_j$. Direct integration gives
$(M_{R(w)}(0),M_{R(w)}(1))=w$. Hence the complete exact sequence is
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ16}
 0\longrightarrow C_c^\infty((a,b))\xrightarrow{T}
 C_c^\infty((a,b))\xrightarrow{(M(0),M(1))}\mathbb C^2
 \longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}

The source's positive boundary form must not be substituted for the
programme's endpoint form. In the original $A,B$ coordinates HWA gives
$P_e(f,f)=2\operatorname{Re}(\overline A B)$ and
$K_e(f,f)=\frac12(|A|^2+|B|^2)-\frac53\operatorname{Re}(\overline A B)$.
Their exact images under (OBJ13), writing $y_0=u(a),y_L=u(b)$, are
\begin{align}
 P_e(f,f)&=2e^{(b-a)/2}\operatorname{Re}(\overline{y_L}y_0),\tag{OBJ17}\\
 K_e(f,f)&=\tfrac12(e^b|y_L|^2+e^{-a}|y_0|^2)
 -\tfrac53e^{(b-a)/2}\operatorname{Re}(\overline{y_L}y_0).\tag{OBJ18}
\end{align}
These are exact congruences by $B_{a,b}^{-1}$; they keep every original
coefficient and sign. They prove the relation between the constructions
even though their individual Hermitian forms differ.

The complete Weil trace from BPZ2 is
$\mathcal Z(f,g)=\sum_\rho m_\rho\overline{M_f(1-\bar\rho)}M_g(\rho)$.
The accompanying BPZ proof shows that every actual off-line zero
produces a compact test in $\ker(M(0),M(1))$ with
$\mathcal Z(f,f)<0$. Choose $a,b$ containing that test's support;
then (OBJ14)--(OBJ18) vanish for that same test, for every $r$.
Thus increasing the order of these boundary jets never by itself
removes the complete primitive zero detector. The retained arithmetic
value is still $\mathcal Z(f,f)$, including the prime and archimedean
terms in HWA33--34. This is a proved common-kernel statement with an
exact return map, not an assertion that boundary geometry is unrelated
to the arithmetic trace.

\subsection{The arbitrary lattice and its injective comparison}

For every vector space $V$ in (OBJ4), (OBJ8), (OBJ10), or (OBJ16), keep
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ19}
 G_\Lambda(V)=\{(0,\lambda):\lambda\in\Lambda\}
       \cup(V\times\{1_\Lambda\}),\quad
 (v,\lambda)+(w,\mu)=(v+w,\lambda\vee\mu),\quad
 (c,\nu)(v,\lambda)=(cv,\nu\wedge\lambda).
\end{equation}
Here $\Lambda$ is the full original bounded distributive lattice;
the change of its letter distinguishes it from the interval length
$L$ and changes none of its elements or operations. Each displayed
linear map $F$ has the exact lift $F^\uparrow(v,\lambda)=(Fv,\lambda)$.
Direct substitution in (OBJ19) proves additivity and scalar linearity.
The lifted composition is the lift of the composite, so all the
displayed diagrams, right inverses, and inverse maps still commute.
On nonzero kernel vectors at top support the image is
$e=(0,1_\Lambda)$; every lower labelled zero retains its own label.
In particular such a kernel vector is not mapped to
$\tau=(0,0_\Lambda)$.

Each Hermitian form $Q$ lifts as
$Q^\uparrow((v,\lambda),(w,\mu))=(Q(v,w),\lambda\wedge\mu)$.
It is well-defined since a nonzero amplitude requires both input
amplitudes, and hence both labels, to be top. Sesquilinearity follows
from distributivity of meet over join. It follows in particular that
(OBJ14) is an equality of the retained supported values with the
original common input label. Independent labels are not assigned to
the two components of $J_rf$ or to the two Mellin endpoints.

Finally retain the programme's idempotent semifield $K$ and its
Boolean base $\mathbb B$. The comparison is
\begin{equation}\tag{OBJ20}
 \Phi_V(v,\lambda)=(v,1_K\otimes\lambda)
 \in V\times(K\otimes_{\mathbb B}\Lambda),\qquad
 (F\times I)\Phi_V=\Phi_WF^\uparrow.
\end{equation}
The map $\chi:K\to\mathbb B$ sends zero to zero and every nonzero
element to one. Idempotent addition cannot sum a nonzero element to
zero, and a semifield has no zero divisors; therefore $\chi$ preserves
addition and multiplication. Consequently $\chi\otimes I$ retracts
$\lambda\mapsto1_K\otimes\lambda$, proving injectivity of (OBJ20).
All boundary vectors, all common kernels and every lower support
label calculated above survive this comparison. No localization at
$e$ and no Boolean specialization of the lattice has occurred.
