\section{Spectral-action derivatives with every scalar retained}
\label{sec:spectral-action-retained-constants}

The source of the Loewner matrix and its rank-one perturbation is
Alain Connes and Walter D. van Suijlekom,
\emph{Quadratic Forms, Real Zeros and Echoes of the Spectral Action},
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.23257v1}{arXiv:2511.23257v1}.
The original author file is \path{Araki-final-oct25.tex}.
The relevant source locators are equations \texttt{form2} and
\texttt{QD-sa}, Lemmas \texttt{basics-general}, \texttt{key-general}
and \texttt{lem:sa}, and Proposition \texttt{prop:der-sa}; the
spectral-action displays occur at lines 1124--1147 and 1396--1523
of the local original-source file. The derivation below proves the
finite-dimensional identities directly, including the cyclic factor
and the original mass of the kernel vector. It does not alter the
source matrices, their spectra, or the parameter of perturbation.

\subsection{The all-order derivative and its cyclic factor}

Let $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_M)$ with real
entries, and let $R$ be any complex $M$ by $M$ matrix, without a
selfadjointness assumption. Let $f$ be holomorphic on a neighbourhood
of the finite spectrum. Choose a positively oriented union of small
contours $\Gamma$ in that neighbourhood enclosing every eigenvalue.
For sufficiently small complex $t$, define $f(D+tR)$ by the same
contours. For nodes inside them, including repeated nodes, put
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC1}
 f[x_1,\ldots,x_n]
 =\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma
       \frac{f(z)}{\prod_{j=1}^n(z-x_j)}\,dz.
\end{equation}
For one node this is Cauchy's formula. Subtracting the two rational
integrands verifies the divided-difference recursion at distinct
end nodes. Taking limits proves the repeated-node extension; in
particular $f[x,\ldots,x]=f^{(n-1)}(x)/(n-1)!$.

For every integer $n\geq1$ the exact derivative is
\begin{align}
 \left.\frac{d^n}{dt^n}\operatorname{tr}f(D+tR)\right|_{t=0}
 &=n!\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n=1}^{M}
       R_{i_1i_2}\cdots R_{i_ni_1}
       f[\lambda_{i_1},\ldots,\lambda_{i_n},\lambda_{i_1}]
       \tag{SAC2}\label{eq:SAC-repeated}\\
 &=(n-1)!\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n=1}^{M}
       R_{i_1i_2}\cdots R_{i_ni_1}
       f'[\lambda_{i_1},\ldots,\lambda_{i_n}].
       \tag{SAC3}\label{eq:SAC-cyclic}
\end{align}
For $n=1$ the product is $R_{i_1i_1}$. To prove (SAC2), write
$A(z)=(z-D)^{-1}$. The norm-convergent Neumann series, uniformly
on $\Gamma$ for small $|t|$, is
\[
 (z-D-tR)^{-1}
 =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^n(A(z)R)^n A(z).
\]
The trace of its $n$th coefficient is
\[
 \sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n}
 \frac{R_{i_1i_2}\cdots R_{i_ni_1}}
 {(z-\lambda_{i_1})^2
       \prod_{k=2}^n(z-\lambda_{i_k})}.
\]
Integrating against $f$ and differentiating at zero proves (SAC2),
with its factor $n!$.

Here is the separate step which changes that factor. The integral
of the derivative of $f(z)/\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-x_j)$ around
$\Gamma$ is zero. Consequently
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC4}
 f'[x_1,\ldots,x_n]
 =\sum_{r=1}^{n} f[x_1,\ldots,x_n,x_r].
\end{equation}
This remains true for repeated nodes by the same rational identity,
with no limiting division by a vanishing difference. In the full
sum of (SAC2), cyclically moving $i_r$ to the first position does
not change the product of the $n$ scalar matrix entries and is a
bijection of the ordered index tuples. Each of the $n$ summands
in (SAC4) therefore has the same full cyclic sum. The full sum
containing $f'$ is $n$ times the sum containing one repeated first
node. Dividing the coefficient $n!$ by that $n$ gives (SAC3).
Tuples with a nontrivial cyclic stabilizer cause no exception:
the argument uses $n$ bijections of the full index set, not a
count of orbits.

The same finite-dimensional result applies to the source's
Fourier definition whenever its stated factorial moment bounds
hold. More explicitly, suppose
$f(x)=\int_{\mathbb R}\widehat f(u)e^{iux}\,du$ and
\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}|u|^n|\widehat f(u)|\,du
 \leq C^{n+1}n!\qquad(n\geq0).
\]
Tonelli's theorem applied to the exponential power series gives
$\int e^{a|u|}|\widehat f(u)|\,du\leq C/(1-aC)$ for
$0\leq a<C^{-1}$. Thus the Fourier integral extends $f$
holomorphically to $|\operatorname{Im}z|<C^{-1}$, with uniform
domination on narrower strips. Cauchy functional calculus and the
Fourier definition of $f(D+tR)$ agree: insert the uniformly
dominated Fourier integral in the contour formula and use
$e^{iu(D+tR)}=(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_\Gamma
e^{iuz}(z-D-tR)^{-1}\,dz$. The domination just proved permits
the interchange. Hence (SAC2)--(SAC3) also give the finite
specialization of the source's Fourier construction.

In particular,
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC5}
 F'(0)=\sum_i R_{ii}f'(\lambda_i),\qquad
 F''(0)=\sum_{i,j}R_{ij}R_{ji}f'[\lambda_i,\lambda_j],
 \quad F(t)=\operatorname{tr}f(D+tR).
\end{equation}
The diagonal divided difference is $f''(\lambda_i)$.
The statement of source Lemma \texttt{lem:sa} prints $n!$
instead of $(n-1)!$ in the expression using $f'$. Its proof
ends with the repeated-node expression, for which $n!$ is
correct. The earlier display in the source subsection
\emph{Relation to the spectral action} prints $F''(0)/2$
equal to the right-hand side of (SAC5); (SAC5) proves that the
right-hand side is $F''(0)$. The later first and second derivative
display and Proposition \texttt{prop:der-sa} have the correct
second derivative factor.

A one-dimensional check already determines both factors. For
$D=(0)$, $R=(1)$ and $f(x)=x^n$, $F^{(n)}(0)=n!$ whereas
$f'[0,\ldots,0]=n$. Formula (SAC3) gives
$(n-1)!n=n!$; replacing its coefficient by $n!$ gives an extra
factor $n$. For $n=2$, $f(x)=x^2$ gives $F''(0)=2$ and
$R_{11}^2f''(0)=2$. The discrepancy also occurs within the
source's decaying Fourier class: $f(x)=e^{-x^2}$, $D=(0)$,
$R=(1)$ gives $F''(0)=-2$, not $-4$. Its Gaussian Fourier
transform satisfies the displayed factorial moment bound,
because $\int e^{a|u|}|\widehat f(u)|\,du<\infty$ for every
$a>0$ and $|u|^n\leq n!a^{-n}e^{a|u|}$.

\subsection{The original kernel mass and the rank-one perturbation}

Now retain the source index set $I=\{-N,\ldots,N\}$ and its
distinct real numbers $\lambda_i$ with $\lambda_{-i}=-\lambda_i$.
Let $a_i,b_i$ be real, $a_{-i}=a_i$ and $b_{-i}=-b_i$, and put
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC6}
 D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)_{i\in I},\quad
 q_{ii}=a_i,\quad
 q_{ij}=\frac{b_i-b_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}\ (i\ne j),\quad
 \eta=(1)_{i\in I},\quad\beta=(b_i)_{i\in I}.
\end{equation}
The evenness of the diagonal data is stated explicitly because
it is needed for $Q\gamma=\gamma Q$, where
$(\gamma x)_i=x_{-i}$. We use
$\langle x,y\rangle=x^*y$ and
$\mathcal Q(x,y)=x^*Qy$. Suppose $Q\xi=0$, $\gamma\xi=\xi$
and let the original, unaltered mass be
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC7}
 s=\eta^*\xi=\sum_{i\in I}\xi_i\ne0,\qquad
 v=D\xi,\qquad R=-\frac{v\eta^*}{s},\qquad D'=D+R.
\end{equation}
No rescaling of $\xi$, of $D$, or of the parameter $t$ is made.
The vector may be complex; the real-vector case is recovered
by taking $\xi\in\mathbb R^{2N+1}$.

Entrywise computation and parity give
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC8}
 DQ-QD=\beta\eta^*-\eta\beta^*,\qquad
 \beta^*\xi=0,\quad\eta^*D\xi=0,\qquad Qv=-s\beta.
\end{equation}
Indeed the $ij$ entry of the commutator is $b_i-b_j$ off
the diagonal and zero on it. In both scalar parity sums the
terms $i$ and $-i$ cancel and the index-zero term is zero.
Applying the commutator to $\xi$ gives
$-QD\xi=s\beta$, proving the last formula. In particular
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC9}
 R^2=0,\quad D'\xi=0,\quad QR=\beta\eta^*,\quad
 R^*Q=\eta\beta^*,\quad QD'=D'^*Q.
\end{equation}
The first identity follows from $\eta^*v=0$; the second uses
$\eta^*\xi=s$; the last follows from the two preceding
identities and (SAC8). These equations remain valid without
assuming $Q$ positive.

There is always an even polynomial $f$ realizing the entire
finite data $f'(\lambda_i)=b_i$, $f''(\lambda_i)=a_i$.
Here is an explicit construction, so that the application does
not require an assumed interpolating function. Put
\begin{align}
 \ell_i(x)&=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j},
 &h_i(x)&=(1-2\ell_i'(\lambda_i)(x-\lambda_i))\ell_i(x)^2,
 &k_i(x)&=(x-\lambda_i)\ell_i(x)^2,\notag\\
 g(x)&=\sum_{i\in I}\bigl(b_i h_i(x)+a_i k_i(x)\bigr),
 &f(x)&=c+\int_0^xg(u)\,du,\qquad c\in\mathbb R.
 \tag{SAC10}
\end{align}
Direct evaluation gives $g(\lambda_i)=b_i$,
$g'(\lambda_i)=a_i$. Uniqueness of this interpolating
polynomial of degree at most $2(2N+1)-1$ follows because a
difference has a double zero at every node and degree less
than $2(2N+1)$. The polynomial $-g(-x)$ has the same data,
so uniqueness gives $g(-x)=-g(x)$ and $f$ is even. The
constant $c=f(0)$ is retained as an arbitrary constant and is
never set to zero to change the action.

For this polynomial, or for any holomorphic $f$ with the same
first two derivative values at the nodes, (SAC5) yields
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC11}
 F'(0)=F''(0)
 =-\frac{\beta^Tv}{s}
 =\frac{v^TQv}{s^2}
 =\frac{v^*Qv}{|s|^2}.
\end{equation}
To check every equality, $R_{ii}=-v_i/s$ gives the first
derivative $-\beta^Tv/s$. Since $q_{ij}=f'[\lambda_i,\lambda_j]$
also on the diagonal, the second derivative is $v^TQv/s^2$.
Equation (SAC8) equates these two expressions.
Finally $v^*Qv=-s\,\overline{\beta^Tv}$ is real because $Q$
is Hermitian. Taking its conjugate gives
$v^*Qv=-\overline{s}\,\beta^Tv$, which proves the last
identity. Thus for the original real vector and its real mass,
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC12}
 F'(0)=F''(0)=\frac{\mathcal Q(D\xi,D\xi)}{s^2}.
\end{equation}
For a complex vector the denominator of the Hermitian
expression is $|s|^2$, as in (SAC11). If $Q\geq0$ the common
value is nonnegative. If its kernel is one-dimensional and
$D\xi\ne0$, it is strictly positive: $D\xi$ is odd, whereas
the kernel is even, so $D\xi$ is outside the kernel.

In the one-dimensional even-kernel situation the required
$s\ne0$ follows from the original data. If $s=0$, (SAC8)
gives $D\xi\in\ker Q$. Its odd parity forces $D\xi=0$.
Distinctness and symmetry of the $\lambda_i$ show that
$\ker D$ is spanned by $e_0$. Then nonzero $\xi$ has
$s=\xi_0\ne0$, a contradiction. If $D\xi=0$ from the outset,
$R=0$ and (SAC11) gives the correct zero derivative.

\subsection{The exact all-order rank-one expression}

Write
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC13}
 h(z)=\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\xi_i}{z-\lambda_i},\qquad
 B(z)=\frac1s\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\lambda_i\xi_i}{z-\lambda_i}
     =\frac{z h(z)}s-1.
\end{equation}
The determinant identity for a rank-one perturbation gives
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC14}
 \det(z-D-tR)=\det(z-D)(1+tB(z))
             =\det(z-D)\left(1-t+\frac{tz h(z)}s\right).
\end{equation}
For completeness, for an invertible $A$ the formula
$\det(A+u w^*)=\det(A)(1+w^*A^{-1}u)$ follows by
multilinearity in the columns: terms taking the added column
twice vanish because they are proportional. Apply this to
$A=z-D$, $u=tv/s$, $w=\eta$. The resulting rational identity
extends to a polynomial identity at the nodes by multiplication
by $\det(z-D)$.

Keeping the same contours as above and taking $|t|$ small
enough that $|tB|<1$ there, Jacobi's determinant formula and
integration by parts give
\begin{align}
 F(t)-F(0)
 &=-\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma f'(z)\log(1+tB(z))\,dz,
                                                        \tag{SAC15}\\
 F^{(n)}(0)
 &=\frac{(-1)^n(n-1)!}{2\pi i}
           \int_\Gamma f'(z)B(z)^n\,dz\notag\\
 &=\frac{(-1)^n(n-1)!}{s^n}
   \sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n\in I}
       \left(\prod_{r=1}^n\lambda_{i_r}\xi_{i_r}\right)
       f'[\lambda_{i_1},\ldots,\lambda_{i_n}].
                                                        \tag{SAC16}
\end{align}
The logarithm in (SAC15) is its convergent power series on
the contour; there is no global choice of logarithm across
the eigenvalues. Jacobi's formula is verified by differentiating
the determinant in its columns, which gives
$\partial_z\log\det(z-A)=\operatorname{tr}(z-A)^{-1}$
away from the eigenvalues. Subtract the two contour functional
calculus formulas, use (SAC14), and integrate the derivative of
$f\log(1+tB)$ to obtain (SAC15). Termwise expansion, uniformly
convergent on $\Gamma$, proves (SAC16). This formula retains
all higher derivatives instead of extrapolating the equality
of the first two.

\subsection{An exact three-dimensional check}

With indices $-1,0,1$, take the unaltered data
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC17}
 D=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
 Q=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&-1\\-1&2&-1\\-1&-1&2\end{pmatrix},\quad
 \xi=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\\2\end{pmatrix},\quad
 s=6,\quad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Here $Q=3I-\eta\eta^T$ is positive with kernel spanned by
$\eta$, and its off-diagonal entries are exactly the divided
differences of the displayed $\beta$. We have
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC18}
 v=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\0\\2\end{pmatrix},\quad Qv=-6\beta,
 \quad v^TQv=24,\quad
 R=\begin{pmatrix}1/3&1/3&1/3\\0&0&0\\-1/3&-1/3&-1/3\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Use the explicit even polynomial
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC19}
 f(x)=x^2-\frac{15}{8}x^4+\frac34x^6.
\end{equation}
Its derivative is $2x-15x^3/2+9x^5/2$ and its second
derivative is $2-45x^2/2+45x^4/2$. Therefore
$f'(-1)=1$, $f'(0)=0$, $f'(1)=-1$ and
$f''(-1)=f''(0)=f''(1)=2$, as required.
Directly from (SAC14), or from the displayed matrices,
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC20}
 \det(z-D-tR)=z\left(z^2-1+\frac{2t}{3}\right),\qquad
 F(t)=-\frac14+\frac23t+\frac13t^2-\frac49t^3.
\end{equation}
For the second equality put $u=1-2t/3$. The characteristic
polynomial gives $\operatorname{tr}(D+tR)^{2k}=2u^k$ for
$k\geq1$; this also holds at repeated roots by polynomial
continuation. Substitute it into (SAC19) to get
$2(u-15u^2/8+3u^3/4)$, whose expansion is (SAC20). Hence
\begin{equation}\tag{SAC21}
 F'(0)=F''(0)=\frac23=\frac{24}{6^2},\qquad
 F'''(0)=-\frac83.
\end{equation}
Thus the retained-mass first and second derivative identity
is exact, while even a positive Loewner matrix with a simple
even kernel does not make the third derivative equal to them.
The rank-one action in (SAC15)--(SAC16), rather than an
unproved equality of all derivatives, is the complete finite
object supplied by these source data. No identification with
the programme's theta function is asserted by this calculation.
